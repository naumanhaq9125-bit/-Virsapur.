Boreendo: a small instrument and a large safeguarding task

Safeguarding the boreendo means sustaining makers, players and opportunities to learn—not merely preserving a small clay instrument in a display.

The boreendo is small enough for its visual simplicity to conceal the work around it. A hollow clay body with sound holes may appear to be a modest object. Yet making it sound, shaping its musical possibilities and teaching someone else to use it involve knowledge that cannot be preserved by keeping the object alone.

UNESCO’s boreendo record describes clay that is dried and fired, with sound produced by blowing and altered through the handling of the openings and mouthpiece. It also notes recent modifications that extend musical possibilities. This is evidence of an instrument being worked with and developed, not a form that must remain physically unchanged to count as heritage.

The element was inscribed in 2025 on the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding. The decision records community participation in the nomination and calls for continued involvement in safeguarding, with particular attention to education. Recognition is therefore connected to a task, not a declaration that the task has been completed.

Follow the work beyond the finished object

A display can identify the material and shape of a boreendo. To understand the craft, a record also needs the maker’s explanation of decisions: how the body is formed, how openings are handled and how a finished piece is judged. Such documentation should distinguish what a particular maker demonstrates from a universal rule for every instrument.

Playing introduces another kind of expertise. An object that produces a sound does not automatically reveal the repertoire or the control needed for performance. A short demonstration may attract attention while leaving the process of learning almost entirely invisible. Repeated lessons, feedback and opportunities to play can matter more than a single public appearance.

UNESCO’s account describes transmission within families and newer teaching settings, including schools and cultural events. These settings raise practical questions. Who has time to teach? Can learners obtain suitable instruments? Is there a place to practise? Which forms of support do the people involved consider useful? A safeguarding plan becomes meaningful through such details.

Avoid the romance of disappearance

Calling an instrument the last trace of an ancient civilization may produce a dramatic caption, but it requires evidence that a contemporary performance cannot supply. Likewise, a dated statement about the number of expert practitioners should not be repeated indefinitely as a current census. Attention is more useful when it remains accurate.

The people who make, decorate, teach and perform should be credited for their particular contributions. Roles recorded in one nomination should not become permanent restrictions on who may participate. The point of safeguarding is to support a living practice, including the choices its participants make about its future.

The boreendo’s scale makes this lesson especially clear. A museum case can protect a clay object from damage. Sustaining the heritage around it requires relationships, learning and material support outside the case. The instrument deserves to be seen, but also understood as something people continue to make meaningful through use.
