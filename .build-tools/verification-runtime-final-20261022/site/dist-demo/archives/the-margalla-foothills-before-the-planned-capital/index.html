Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The Margalla foothills before the planned capital

Routes, caves and excavated structures in the Margalla foothills reveal histories that precede Islamabad’s modern plan and cross its administrative boundary.

The modern plan of Islamabad can make the surrounding foothills appear to be its natural frame. Their history is not confined to that role. Routes, shelters and archaeological remains belong to landscapes that existed before the capital’s streets and sectors. Understanding them requires looking across the present boundary between Islamabad and neighbouring Rawalpindi rather than treating that line as an ancient division.

A pass belongs to a wider route

The official heritage catalogue records remains of the Grand Trunk Road at Margalla Pass. The relevant record is geographically associated with Rawalpindi, not simply absorbed into Islamabad because the capital is nearby. This distinction matters for both mapping and interpretation.

A road fragment can help establish the material presence of a route, but it cannot demonstrate every historical journey later associated with that corridor. Road surfaces change, alignments shift and repairs accumulate. Describing a recorded stretch is more precise than assigning all movement through the hills to one unchanged ancient road.

Shelter, water and uncertain chronology

Shah Allah Ditta’s caves and spring provide another kind of evidence. Their combination encourages questions about shelter and water, but the official record should not be stretched into a detailed account of named occupants or uninterrupted use. Popular age claims need stronger support than their repetition in visitor descriptions.

An archive can be useful while leaving those questions open. It can identify the place and explain which features are recorded, then distinguish material description from religious attribution or local tradition. That separation protects the significance of the site from dependence on a dramatic but poorly supported date.

Excavation changes the questions

Ban Faqiran offers a different evidential situation. A report in Pakistan Archaeology describes excavations carried out between August 2015 and February 2016, investigating a stupa and a mosque. Their presence within the investigated area does not make them contemporary or parts of one original institution.

The stupa’s surviving masonry and architectural components provide evidence that can be studied through excavation and recording. The official catalogue also notes losses to its upper structure. A reconstruction of the complete monument would therefore be an interpretation, not simply a photograph of what survives.

These distinctions help explain why a landscape history cannot be assembled by lining up attractive sites and assigning them one era. Excavated relationships, standing fabric, documentary references and attributed traditions have different strengths. Each can contribute without being made to answer questions beyond its scope.

Before the capital, not outside its story

The point is not to describe an ancient Islamabad. That would project a modern city backwards onto places with their own histories. Instead, the foothills show how the capital now sits beside and within older patterns of movement and occupation.

A regional map can make those relationships visible while retaining accurate district assignments. The road, caves and stupa do not become interchangeable attractions. Together they invite a more attentive view of the Margallas: a terrain carrying several histories, not merely a scenic edge to a planned city.
