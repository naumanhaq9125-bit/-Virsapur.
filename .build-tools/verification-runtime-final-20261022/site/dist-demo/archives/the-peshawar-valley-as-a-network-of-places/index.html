Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The Peshawar valley as a network of places

Monastery, town and river-junction settlement offer different perspectives on the Peshawar valley’s interconnected archaeological landscape.

The Peshawar valley becomes more intelligible when its heritage is read as different kinds of places rather than a sequence of interchangeable Buddhist ruins. A monastery, a fortified town and a settlement near a river junction had distinct settings and functions. Their relationships are the subject of investigation, not something a line on a tourist map can establish by itself.

The World Heritage property of Takht-i-Bahi and Sahr-i-Bahlol offers a useful starting point because it deliberately brings two components together. The monastic remains occupy hills; the neighbouring town remains lie in the plain. Their shared recognition does not erase the difference between a religious complex and an urban settlement.

Complementary places, different evidence

UNESCO’s account describes Takht-i-Bahi through its courts, stupas, monastic spaces and passages, while Sahr-i-Bahlol is represented by the remains of a fortified town. It also notes the contrast in their present survival: urban occupation has affected the town’s archaeological remains, whereas the hilltop complex retains a more immediately legible arrangement.

This contrast matters for public understanding. A site that looks more complete is not necessarily the only one with a substantial history. Visibility depends on later occupation, conservation and the kinds of structures originally built. The less photogenic component may be essential to understanding the wider landscape.

A settlement at a junction

The Cambridge and Peshawar universities’ research project at Bala Hisar, Charsadda, places the mound near the meeting of the Swat and Kabul rivers and within wider communication routes. Here the geographical question differs from that of a hilltop monastery: how did a settlement relate to converging waterways and movement through adjoining regions?

This is the Bala Hisar at Charsadda, not the similarly named fort in Peshawar city. Keeping the locations distinct is essential when linking records, photographs or historical references. Familiar names can otherwise produce convincing but false connections.

Durham University’s exhibition records collaborative fieldwork at Charsadda in the 1990s, while the Cambridge project describes a later research collaboration. The sequence is a reminder that understanding a site develops through successive investigations. An old excavation does not close every question about its history.

What makes a network visible?

Shared architectural features can suggest comparison, but demonstrating a network requires more: chronology, material distributions, inscriptions or evidence of movement. Two sites need not have been occupied at the same time simply because both appear under a broad label such as Gandhara.

For discovery, the practical benefit is to ask a different question at each place. How were spaces organised at the monastery? What survives of the town? What made the river-junction location significant? Following those questions preserves the character of each site while allowing the valley to emerge as a connected landscape—one composed of varied communities and changing relationships rather than a single repeated monument type.
