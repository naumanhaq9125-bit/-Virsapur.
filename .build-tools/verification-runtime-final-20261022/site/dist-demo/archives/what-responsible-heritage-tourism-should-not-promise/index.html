What responsible heritage tourism should not promise

Responsible tourism requires evidence about access, impacts and benefits. An appealing heritage itinerary should not promise more than it can demonstrate.

A heritage journey can create opportunities to learn, support local livelihoods and encounter places with care. None of those outcomes is guaranteed simply because the itinerary uses the word responsible. The description needs to explain what is being done, who is involved and how claims about benefits or reduced harm are supported.

The 2022 ICOMOS International Charter for Cultural Heritage Tourism places conservation, participatory governance, interpretation and community resilience within the same framework. Its principles are not an endorsement of a particular tour. They offer questions against which tourism practices can be examined, including how impacts are monitored and whose rights and interests shape management.

Promises that deserve scrutiny

Exclusive access is one example. A tour organiser may legitimately arrange a visit with a responsible manager, but an advertisement should not imply permission that has not been secured. Nor should restricted access be treated as an obstacle to overcome for a better photograph. Some limits may be essential to safety, conservation, privacy or religious practice.

Claims of community benefit need similar precision. Paying a local guide is a concrete action. Declaring that a visit supports the entire community is a much larger claim. A reader should be able to distinguish a documented payment, a partnership, a donation and a general aspiration. These are not interchangeable descriptions of the same transaction.

Conservation language can also become vague. A visitor fee may contribute to a management budget, but that does not prove that a particular journey directly restores a monument. If an organiser claims a contribution to a project, it should explain the arrangement and identify the recipient rather than rely on a photograph of repair work.

Learning is part of the experience

Good interpretation can acknowledge uncertainty. A guide need not turn every tradition into a verified historical event or every building phase into a simple royal story. Explaining why an attribution is disputed can make the visit more interesting. It also respects the difference between a local account, an inscription and a later interpretation.

For travellers, practical restraint matters alongside enthusiasm. Follow the arrangements communicated by the responsible hosts, avoid interfering with ordinary use and ask before recording people or sensitive activities. These are general editorial principles, not a substitute for site-specific advice or applicable rules.

A credible heritage offer should leave room for change: a site may close, conditions may alter, or a community may decline participation. A responsible description makes such limits understandable instead of promising that everything will always be available. The best test is not how many ethical adjectives appear in the brochure, but whether the actual arrangements remain accountable to the place and the people who live with it.
