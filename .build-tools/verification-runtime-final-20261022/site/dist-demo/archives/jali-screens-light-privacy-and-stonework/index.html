Jali screens: light, privacy and stonework

A jali is a material screen and a changing field of light. Its geometry matters together with thickness, position and the space around it.

A jali makes a boundary without making it completely solid. Its openings allow a relationship between spaces while the remaining material establishes a pattern. Seen frontally, it may resemble a geometric drawing. Seen in place, it also has thickness, weight, changing shadows and a particular relationship to the people on either side.

The Metropolitan Museum's marble and sandstone screens illustrate that material difference. Its curatorial descriptions also distinguish the screen itself from the effects of light passing through it. These are comparative objects attributed to the subcontinent, not components to be reassigned to a Pakistani monument without provenance.

Geometry is made in material

A drawn pattern can be repeated indefinitely. A carved stone panel must hold together, fit an opening and survive handling and use. The widths of connecting elements, the treatment of the border and the transition between patterns therefore matter as much as the attractive shapes of the holes.

Some screens are cut from a single piece; others use different assembly methods or materials. Do not assume that every perforated architectural panel demonstrates the same process. A technical record should identify the material and construction before a caption praises the supposed difficulty of making it.

The view changes with position

Move sideways within the permitted area and an opening's apparent size can change. Thickness and angle influence what is visible through the screen. Light from outside and inside also affects the relationship between seeing and being seen. Privacy is therefore conditional, not an absolute property permanently guaranteed by a patterned surface.

A screen may divide an interior, fill an external opening or protect an edge. Those positions create different spatial tasks. The presence of a jali does not, by itself, establish the gender, status or identity of everyone who used the room.

Shadow is part of the composition

The Met's interpretation draws attention to the moving patterns projected into an interior. A useful architectural photograph can show both the panel and its effect on an adjoining floor or wall, provided the setting and permission allow it. Recording the time helps distinguish a momentary pattern from a permanent feature.

At the tombs of Shah Burhan or Jahangir, the linked place records offer starting points for investigating screens in their own settings. Their materials, repairs and specific functions should be checked individually rather than inferred from the museum comparisons.

A broken or replaced section also belongs in the history. A visually complete pattern may include conservation work, while weathering can alter the apparent sharpness of an opening. Touching fragile edges to test thickness is not an acceptable way to investigate.

The pleasure of a jali lies in several relationships at once: solid and open, pattern and support, visibility and shelter, surface and shadow. Describing those relationships keeps the craft connected to architecture instead of reducing it to an ornamental motif that could be pasted anywhere.
