Haveli Sujan Singh: the courtyard as an urban refuge

Sujan Singh Haveli’s courtyard and sequence of thresholds reveal an urban house beyond its ornate doorway. Its changing surroundings are part of the architectural story.

Sujan Singh Haveli is often encountered through its ornate entrance, but the doorway is only the beginning of its architecture. The house is organised around an open courtyard, reached through a sequence of entrance spaces. That movement from street to interior is central to its character. A facade photograph can celebrate workmanship while leaving the actual experience of the building almost unexplained.

The archaeological catalogue describes a recessed entrance porch, a substantial main door and routes leading toward the courtyard. Rooms flank parts of the court, while a larger hall occupies another side. Stairs connect levels. Rather than treating these as a list of components, imagine them as a series of choices about access, visibility and the relationship between shared and enclosed spaces.

The courtyard as an organising space

An open court gives a house an interior centre that is not itself a room. Doors and verandas can address it, and changes in light can make its edges more prominent than its middle. In reading Sujan Singh Haveli, the useful question is not simply whether the courtyard is attractive. It is how the surrounding spaces depend on it to form a coherent domestic arrangement.

The idea of an urban refuge is therefore architectural rather than a claim that the house was always secluded or silent. A threshold can delay a view; a change of direction can separate an entrance from a principal space. These effects can be investigated from a plan and photographs without inventing the household’s daily routines.

The catalogue’s description also recalls a former garden setting and a more dispersed surrounding landscape. That reminder complicates the present association with dense Rawalpindi streets. A building does not retain the same urban meaning when its surroundings change. Its former relationship to open ground deserves to be studied alongside its later place in the city.

Repairing a house, not just a front

Reports of conservation activity provide another layer. The National College of Arts’ involvement was reported in 2015, and an official statement carried by APP in 2021 described a restoration planning exercise. The latter noted surviving doors, iron elements and tiles. These are dated accounts of work and intentions, not evidence that every proposed intervention has since been completed.

They nevertheless identify an important principle: the building’s character rests in a combination of materials. Replacing a door, altering a roof or removing a floor finish can change more than a decorative detail. Each may affect the way rooms connect and the way the interior is understood.

An archive presentation should therefore follow the house inward. Begin at the street, pass through the entrance, establish the courtyard and then examine selected details in their positions. That sequence makes the haveli legible as a place of habitation and changing urban circumstances. It also gives its craftsmanship a purpose beyond the production of an impressive image.
