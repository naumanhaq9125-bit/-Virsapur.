Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

How archaeologists build an Indus chronology

An Indus chronology is built by connecting stratigraphy, artefact comparison and scientific dating. Period names organise the evidence, but they should not be mistaken for events that happened everywhere at once.

A timeline of the Indus civilisation can look deceptively simple: an early phase, an urban phase and a later phase arranged in neat blocks. The archaeological work behind those blocks is less tidy. Researchers have to establish relationships between deposits, compare material and evaluate dates before deciding how a sequence should be described.

A particularly revealing example appears in Dales and Kenoyer’s report on the Harappa Project’s 1986–89 seasons. The authors explain the need for a secure internal relative chronology supported by radiocarbon dates. They also warn that broad labels can obscure regional and temporal variation. Their numbered periods were analytical tools, not natural divisions waiting to be discovered intact.

First establish the order

Relative chronology asks which event or deposit came before another. A floor covered by later material, a wall cut by a pit or a sequence of rebuilding can provide such relationships. The result is an order of events even before any calendar date has been attached.

That order is not always a simple vertical stack. Later activity can cut into earlier deposits, while older material can be moved into a newer fill. The location and character of a find matter as much as the depth at which it appears.

The Harappa report’s caution about terminology is important here. If material is assigned to a phase solely because it resembles another assemblage, the classification should not then be used uncritically as independent proof that the two deposits are exactly contemporary.

Bringing dates into a recorded sequence

The Beas Landscape and Settlement Survey provides a concrete example from a test trench. Its authors distinguish cultural fills, hearths, burnt floors, water-related deposits and mudbrick structures. Radiocarbon results from different positions in that sequence were considered alongside the pottery rather than replacing the stratigraphic observations.

The published discussion also treats a possible early occupation as something that further study could resolve. That wording is part of the result, not an inconvenience to remove from a public account. A small number of diagnostic-looking sherds can support a hypothesis without making it final.

From one settlement to a region

Connecting a local sequence to an Indus-wide chronology requires another step. Similar changes need not occur in every settlement at precisely the same moment. A regional phase is a useful framework for comparison, but it should not erase differences in the evidence from particular places.

This is why a good timeline should identify approximate ranges, the source of its dates and the meaning of its period labels. It should also distinguish an older published framework from a later revision rather than mixing their dates without explanation.

Chronology is not merely a preliminary list of numbers attached to a finished history. It is an argument about relationships in the evidence. Understanding how that argument is built makes the Indus past more accessible, because readers can see why an interpretation changes and what new work would be needed to improve it.
