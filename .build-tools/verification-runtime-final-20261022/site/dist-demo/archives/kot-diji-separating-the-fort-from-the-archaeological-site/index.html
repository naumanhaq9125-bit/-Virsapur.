Kot Diji: separating the fort from the archaeological site

Kot Diji names both a Talpur fort and an ancient archaeological settlement. Separating their dates and evidence reveals two different histories that share a landscape, not a single construction story.

Kot Diji is a place where one name can carry two very different dates. The dramatic hilltop fort belongs to the Talpur period, while the archaeological settlement associated with the same locality reaches back into the development of the Indus civilisation. Confusing them turns a rich landscape into an impossible claim that the standing fortress is thousands of years old.

The distinction is straightforward but important. The federal archaeological catalogue records the fort as a stronghold associated with Mir Sohrab Khan. Sindh's Directorate General of Antiquities separately describes Diji ji Takri, the archaeological mound, and the excavations that helped identify a pre-Harappan cultural phase. These are related through location, not interchangeable historical objects.

Reading the fort on its own terms

The Department of Archaeology describes a steep approach, successive gateways and terraces, stone construction and projecting bastions. Those features belong to a defensive architectural sequence. An entrance is not simply a hole in the outer wall: the approach itself can organise and constrain movement before a person reaches the interior.

Pakistan's March 2026 Tentative List submission places the fort in the late-eighteenth-century Talpur context. That is a nomination-stage record, not inscription on the World Heritage List. The document contains differing precise construction dates, so a broad period is more responsible here than false certainty about a single year.

The fort's height allows the surrounding landscape to become part of its interpretation. Visibility, approach and the placement of enclosure walls are useful observations. They do not require claims that the fort was literally impregnable or that its defences were never tested. Architectural capacity and documented military events are different kinds of evidence.

Reading the mound differently

At the archaeological site, the evidence works through deposits and objects rather than standing Talpur walls. Sindh's Antiquities account explains that excavation recovered a sequence of occupation, mud-brick building and distinctive pottery. These findings contributed to understanding developments preceding the mature Harappan cities.

An archaeological culture is a scholarly way of describing recurring material characteristics; it is not automatically the name an ancient population used for itself. Likewise, shared pottery forms do not prove that all people using them belonged to one political state. The value of the Kot Diji evidence lies in making comparisons and changes investigable, not in supplying an effortless identity label.

Two histories in one visit

The fort and the mound invite different questions. At the fort, examine approach, elevation, masonry and enclosure. At the archaeological settlement, ask how deposits were recorded, which materials distinguish phases and how conclusions depend on excavated context. Neither method can simply be transferred to the other.

Keeping the two histories distinct does not make the landscape fragmented. It reveals how a locality can acquire significance at widely separated times and for different reasons. The strongest account of Kot Diji holds both stories together while preserving the distance between them: an ancient settlement important to archaeological research, and a much later fortress expressing Talpur power through the control of a prominent site.
