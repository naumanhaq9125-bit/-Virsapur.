Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Shahi Tump and the archaeological sequence of Makran

Shahi Tump preserves evidence of settlement and later burial in the Kech valley. The French mission’s work shows why those episodes must be separated rather than flattened into a single ancient date.

Shahi Tump lies in the Kech valley near Turbat, in a landscape whose archaeology cannot be reduced to a distant reflection of the Indus cities. The Department of Archaeology and Museums records settlement remains and later graves at the mound. That sequence is the essential starting point: a place used for living can subsequently become a place used for burial.

The French archaeological mission in Makran investigated Shahi Tump between 1997 and 2006. Its institutional account locates the site across the Kech river from Miri Qalat and describes the investigation as a way to study an early village sequence. The two sites belong to the same research landscape, but their names and discoveries should not be exchanged.

One place, different uses

The mission reports buildings using stone and earth construction, followed by evidence from two fourth-millennium BCE burial grounds. It places the later cemetery within its regional phase IIIa, approximately 3400–2800 BCE. Pottery, metal objects and ornaments contributed to comparisons with southeastern Iran.

These finds matter partly because they occur in recorded relationships. A vessel from a burial does not necessarily represent the equipment of an earlier house beneath it. Combining everything from a mound into a single list of ancient objects would destroy the distinction between different uses of the place.

The relationship between settlement and burial also raises questions that cannot be answered from the mound’s outline alone. Did the later community remember the earlier buildings? Was the location chosen for its prominence, its associations or some other reason? Such possibilities should be identified as questions unless the excavated evidence supports a more definite explanation.

Connections across Makran

Comparisons with material farther west help place Shahi Tump within a wider regional history. They do not automatically establish the identity or language of the people buried there. Similar objects can travel through exchange, shared production practices or imitation; the particular explanation requires additional evidence.

This is why regional phase names are useful but limited. They organise differences in material and chronology. They are not necessarily the names that ancient communities used for themselves, and they should not be converted into a map of fixed ethnic territories.

The archive behind the excavation

The CNRS-associated archive catalogue lists mission documents including campaign reports, burial inventories and stratigraphic records. Even at the level of catalogue descriptions, it reveals that excavation produces much more than a selection of display objects. The records connect finds to observations made during fieldwork.

That documentary work is crucial because excavation changes the deposits it investigates. Later researchers depend on the surviving plans, photographs, registers and samples to reconsider an interpretation. Preserving the archive is therefore part of preserving the site’s knowledge.

Shahi Tump offers a particularly clear introduction to this layered understanding of heritage. Its significance lies not in assigning the mound one spectacular age, but in separating episodes, recording their relationships and asking what those relationships reveal about life and remembrance in ancient Makran.
