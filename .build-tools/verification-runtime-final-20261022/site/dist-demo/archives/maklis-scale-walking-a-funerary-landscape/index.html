Makli’s scale: walking a funerary landscape

Makli’s extent is best understood through relationships among tombs, terrain, living attachments and conservation needs—not by counting monuments alone.

Makli is not one tomb enlarged to monumental scale. It is a funerary landscape in which individual structures, groups, open ground and changes in terrain must be understood together. The first challenge for a visitor is therefore not to identify every monument, but to recognise that the spaces between them are part of the place.

UNESCO’s recognition concerns an ensemble extending across a substantial necropolis near Thatta. A close view of one carved surface can reveal exceptional workmanship, yet it cannot explain the organisation or significance of the whole. Both scales are necessary: the detail that invites attention and the wider setting that gives it relationships.

Distance changes interpretation

From a single viewpoint, monuments can appear to belong to one continuous composition. Moving through a landscape reveals differences in enclosure, approach, size and grouping. Those differences should not automatically be translated into a simple hierarchy of wealth or a chronological sequence. Such interpretations require records of the particular monuments.

An attentive approach is to compare relationships: whether a structure stands within a group, how its entrance addresses surrounding space, and what parts of its setting remain legible. This is a method of looking, not a proposed walking route. Current access, safe paths and conservation restrictions need to be checked with the site authorities.

The necropolis is not socially empty

The Sindh Directorate of Antiquities describes its We Connect Makli initiative as a project linking communities, government bodies and other custodians around heritage protection and disaster preparedness. Implemented between March 2021 and October 2022, it included objectives for emergency response, communication and risk planning. The project account explicitly recognises religious attachments to the site.

That perspective changes the meaning of an apparently quiet landscape. A funerary site can remain important to living communities without functioning like a conventional residential neighbourhood. Conservation must consequently consider relationships and responsibilities as well as the physical condition of stone or brick.

Water moves across the whole site

Conservation reporting submitted to UNESCO following the 2022 rains discusses drainage and the effects of water within Makli’s terrain. Such concerns cannot be solved by looking at each monument as an isolated object. Water follows levels and pathways that cross the boundaries of individual structures, making the broader landscape part of the conservation problem.

A photograph that excludes the ground around a tomb may therefore omit evidence relevant to its condition. Conversely, a distant panorama may conceal vulnerable surfaces. Good documentation needs both context and detail, dated carefully enough to support comparison over time.

Makli’s scale becomes meaningful when it changes how we look: outward from an inscription or carving to a group of monuments, then to terrain and living attachments. The result is more than an impressive count of tombs. It is a recognition that heritage here is an ensemble of material, space and continuing human concern.
