Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Ban Faqiran Stupa above the capital

Ban Faqiran’s excavated stupa brings the archaeological landscape around Islamabad into focus through masonry, recorded finds and later structures.

Ban Faqiran introduces a history older than Islamabad’s planned streets without requiring the city to be described as an ancient capital. The archaeological site lies on a Margalla spur between the Taxila and Soan landscapes. Its principal excavated feature is a Buddhist stupa, while other remains belong to later activity. The distinction between the age of a modern city and the age of occupation in its territory is essential.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes a square stupa base with diaper masonry, shaped kanjur stone and surviving traces of lime plaster. Its upper structure is missing. These details make the building an archaeological remnant, not a complete monument whose original height can be read from what stands today.

What excavation adds

Abdul Ghafoor Lone’s preliminary report identifies the Buddhist monument broadly within the early centuries CE and discusses the wider complex, including a later mosque and substantial water reservoir. The report provides a research framework that is more precise than treating every structure on the ridge as one Buddhist establishment.

Contemporary reporting of the department’s work records the start of excavation in 2015 under Lone’s field direction. The finds included coins, pottery and iron arrowheads. Such objects are valuable because they can help establish sequences when their contexts are recorded. Their mere presence does not mean that every coin dates the construction of the stupa.

The excavation report itself distinguishes finds from different layers and notes a surface coin that could not be securely connected with the structures. This is a particularly useful example of archaeological caution. An old object found at a site may have moved, been retained for a long time or come from an uncertain context. Its date and the date of a wall are separate propositions.

A monument in a wider terrain

Ban Faqiran’s position allows it to be considered alongside the region’s better-known Buddhist remains. That geographical relationship does not automatically place it within the boundaries of the UNESCO World Heritage property at Taxila. Cultural connection, proximity and formal designation answer different questions and should not be collapsed into one label.

The surviving masonry also invites close observation. Diaper work combines larger stones with smaller filling material; moulded elements articulate the base; traces of plaster indicate that the visible stone was not necessarily intended to remain exposed. These features help a reader imagine construction and finish without requiring a speculative reconstruction of the missing superstructure.

A visit or photograph should preserve the distinction between excavated evidence and later repair. Exposure creates a need for maintenance, and a current condition assessment should be dated rather than inferred from an old discovery announcement.

Ban Faqiran matters because it brings archaeological method into the story of the capital’s surroundings. A hilltop remnant becomes intelligible through masonry, stratigraphy and carefully recorded objects. The reward is not an inflated claim to the oldest anything, but a more grounded understanding of how different periods left evidence in the same landscape.
