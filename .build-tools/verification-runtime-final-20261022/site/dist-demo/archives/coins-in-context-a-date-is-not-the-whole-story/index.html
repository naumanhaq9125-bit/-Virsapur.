Coins in context: a date is not the whole story

A coin's issue date is not automatically a building's construction date. Archaeological context determines which chronological question a coin can help answer.

A coin appears to offer the archaeologist an unusually precise object: a recognisable ruler, an issue or a date range. The temptation is to transfer that precision directly to the room in which it was found. The missing step is the history between the coin's manufacture and its final deposition.

Three dates, three different events

A coin was made, used and eventually deposited. Those events need not be close together. A worn or retained object can enter a context later than its issue date; material from an older deposit can also be moved into a newer one. A date on an object therefore does not automatically date every surrounding feature.

The chronological principle often described as a terminus post quem is a lower boundary: a secure deposit containing an object cannot predate that object's manufacture. It is not a statement that deposition happened immediately afterwards. Research on residual coins in other archaeological settings makes this distinction explicit and demonstrates why context cannot be omitted from interpretation.

Barikot's chronological framework

The numismatic study of Barikot emphasises recorded find locations and stratigraphic contexts. Research on the site's revised cultural sequence also draws on radiocarbon dating. Together, these sources illustrate a stronger approach than asking one coin to carry the entire chronology: different evidence is assessed within the excavation's sequence.

This does not mean radiocarbon dates and coin dates can simply be averaged. Each concerns different material and has its own relationship to the event being studied. The analytical task is to explain why a sample or object belongs to a particular phase and how its date constrains that phase.

Consider a hypothetical coin in the fill beneath a later floor. It might help establish that the fill was deposited after a certain point. It would not, without further evidence, identify the exact year the floor was laid or prove who commissioned the building. A coin found loose on an exposed surface presents a different evidential situation again.

What a useful label should tell you

A museum or site label becomes more informative when it separates identification from archaeological association. “Coin of this ruler” is one statement. “Recovered in this documented context” is another. The argument linking that context with a building phase should remain visible.

For visitors to Swat's archaeological sites, coins are consequently best read as participants in a chronology rather than miniature calendars attached to walls. Their value grows when the excavation records survive with them.

The most precise historical account is not always the one with the most exact-looking year. It is the one that explains which event is dated and why.
