A samadhi and a grave are not interchangeable

Funerary terms describe more than a building’s appearance. Samadhi, cremation memorial and grave should be distinguished through the history of the particular place.

A dome and a chamber do not tell us what happened to the body of the person commemorated there. Similar architectural forms can shelter a grave, mark a cremation place or serve a memorial purpose. Translating every funerary monument as a tomb may be convenient, but it can conceal distinctions that matter to its history.

Samadhi is a particularly important term to retain and explain carefully. In a built-heritage record, its meaning should be established through the particular institution and source, rather than assumed from a dictionary definition or extended to every Hindu or Sikh funerary practice.

Two monuments, specific associations

The federal catalogue describes the Samadhi of Ranjit Singh in Lahore as occupying the place of his cremation. Its record for Mahan Singh’s samadhi in Gujranwala likewise connects the monument with a cremation site. Those descriptions are more precise than an unqualified statement that each ruler is buried beneath the building.

They also show why the history of the memorial must be distinguished from the death commemorated. Construction may follow later. Patronage, subsequent additions and restoration can belong to different moments, even when the monument continues to bear one person’s name.

The short catalogue entries are not sufficient to establish every detail about the handling or present location of remains. Where the evidence does not support that claim, an archive should use the narrower description of a commemorative association.

Historical practice and religious prescription

The SGPC’s Sikh Rehat Maryada discusses funeral ceremonies and cremation. It also rejects raising a monument at the place of cremation. This makes an important distinction visible: the existence of a historical royal samadhi should not be presented as proof that it represents the prescription for all Sikh funerals.

A code and a surviving monument answer different historical questions. The code states an institutional religious position; the monument documents a particular act of commemoration. Their relationship requires historical context, not an attempt to erase either source.

This is also why a visitor should not infer the beliefs of present-day Sikhs from a royal building alone. Monumental patronage, family memory and religious norms do not always produce a single uniform record.

Read the label before the silhouette

A useful description separates the site’s name, its commemorative subject and the physical evidence. It might state that a building is identified as a samadhi and that a cited record associates it with cremation, then describe its plan and decoration independently.

The same approach helps with a grave. A visible marker is not necessarily the burial chamber itself, and a later inscription may replace an earlier one. Architectural observation should be paired with documentary evidence rather than made to carry an identification it cannot establish.

For the visitor, this precision makes the buildings more interesting, not less. Ranjit Singh’s and Mahan Singh’s memorials become particular historical constructions instead of examples of an imagined universal custom. Keeping the original term visible allows the archive to explain the commemorative relationship on its own terms, while avoiding a misleading translation that makes every funerary place appear to be the same kind of burial.
