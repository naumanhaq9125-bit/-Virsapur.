Architecture & Built Heritage · Lahore · Punjab

Tomb of Anarkali

Lahore’s Anarkali tomb holds more than a celebrated romance: its architecture and successive uses reveal the making, adaptation and preservation of public memory.

Anarkali’s name carries a story that can overwhelm the building associated with it. The Lahore tomb is often introduced through the romance of Prince Salim and a woman said to have suffered for their attachment. For an architectural visit, it is more useful to begin with a distinction: the monument is tangible evidence; the celebrated personal narrative requires its own historical proof.

The federal archaeological catalogue describes an octagonal building with unequal alternating sides and a central dome raised on a cylindrical neck. It also records a marble cenotaph decorated with floral work and calligraphy. These details give us something concrete to examine without assuming that every element of the popular story has been established.

A building with successive lives

The account published by the Punjab Walled Cities & Heritage Areas Authority follows a later history that is easier to connect to institutional change. After the annexation of Punjab, the building accommodated administrative staff. It was converted into a church in 1851 and continued in that role until 1886, before becoming a record office in 1891.

Each change asks a practical question. How did a funerary space become a place for a congregation? How were circulation, seating and storage accommodated within an inherited form? The authority’s account mentions additions associated with the church, demonstrating that reuse was not simply a matter of changing a sign at the entrance.

A monument that kept documents

The historical description of Punjab Archives published by Sindh Archives identifies the tomb with the Historical Record Office. It discusses Persian and English records, including administrative material connected with the Lahore Darbar and the subsequent colonial administration. This is a history of the repository, not a current promise about which documents can be consulted inside the monument today.

The association is nevertheless striking. A building remembered through an elusive personal story became a setting for documentary evidence about government and society. These two forms of memory do not cancel one another. They show how a monument can acquire meanings that its original builders could not have anticipated.

The heritage authority also reports a 2024 handover and investigation of painted decoration concealed beneath later finishes. That work illustrates the delicate problem of deciding what to reveal and what to retain. Removing a later layer may disclose earlier fabric while also changing evidence of subsequent use.

Anarkali’s tomb deserves attention beyond a choice between believing or rejecting a romance. Its plan, cenotaph, adaptations and institutional associations provide a substantial history in their own right. Keeping the legend distinct from that evidence allows both the building and the questions surrounding its name to remain visible.
