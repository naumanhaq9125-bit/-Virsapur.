Pharwala Fort and the Gakhar landscape

Pharwala connects Gakhar political history with the Soan landscape, differentiated gateways and a foundation tradition that still requires care.

Pharwala is a place where regional power becomes legible through terrain. Set beside the Soan in the Potohar landscape, the fort is closely associated with the Gakhars. Its history is not simply a smaller version of an imperial capital’s story. It concerns local authority, control of approaches and the changing relationship between regional rulers and larger powers.

Pakistan’s 2026 submission to UNESCO’s Tentative List describes the fort on the left bank of the river and identifies it as a principal Gakhar centre. The same text presents the early foundation under Sultan Kaigohar as a traditional attribution. That qualification matters. A remembered origin around the early eleventh century is not equivalent to a securely dated surviving wall.

The submission also recounts Babur’s capture of the fort in 1519 and the Gakhars’ later relationship with the Mughals. These historical associations give the site context, but they should not be used to assign every gateway or ruin to the moment of a particular campaign.

Gateways are not interchangeable

The Tentative List description differentiates entrances by name, scale and architectural treatment. Hathi Gate is described with substantial flanking towers and carved rosettes; other entrances relate to different approaches and internal areas. The older archaeological catalogue also records named gates, but gives a different total. That discrepancy should be resolved through a clear site inventory rather than hidden behind an exact-sounding number.

The material account is more secure at a broad level: stone construction, mortar and surviving portions of fortification. The Department of Archaeology and Museums notes that the earlier residential buildings were not preserved in the condition it recorded. A visitor should therefore resist filling the enclosure with an imagined palace plan merely because a historic account mentions a ruler’s residence.

A landscape, not an isolated object

The relationship between river, slope and gateway helps explain the fort’s position. It also reminds us that protection involves more than a single entrance façade. Approaches, views and neighbouring remains can carry information that disappears if the fort is treated as a decorative object cut out from its setting.

The government’s 2022–23 heritage yearbook records a feasibility study for conservation and preservation. This is evidence of a planning stage, not a completion certificate. Likewise, Tentative List inclusion is a proposal within a longer heritage process; it is not inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage property.

Pharwala is strongest as a story when these distinctions remain intact. Its Gakhar associations deserve attention without inflated foundation certainty. Its gateways deserve individual description without assuming every name maps cleanly onto a surviving structure. Its landscape deserves to remain part of the record, not merely the background of a photograph.

Together, those elements show why the fort matters: it preserves a material point of entry into Potohar’s regional political history, while also revealing how much careful survey and interpretation still contribute to understanding a well-known name.
