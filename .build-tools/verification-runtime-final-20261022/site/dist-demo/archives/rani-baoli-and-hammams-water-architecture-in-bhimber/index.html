Rani Baoli and Hammams: water architecture in Sudhnoti

Rani Baoli stands at Palandri in Sudhnoti, not Bhimber. Its stepped tanks, courtyard and adjoining cells reveal a carefully organised water building whose precise chronology remains uncertain.

Rani Baoli and its adjoining cells belong to Palandri in Sudhnoti. The location needs stating at the outset because a mistaken association with Bhimber can send the building into the wrong regional story. The official archaeological record places it near Palandri’s administrative centre, and the AJK archaeology department includes it among Sudhanoti’s heritage sites.

The building offers a compact lesson in how water can organise architecture. It is not simply a well with an ornamental surround. The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes a rectangular enclosure entered from the south, with an octagonal stepped tank in the courtyard and rooms arranged along its northern and western sides.

Water, enclosure and privacy

The catalogue records another stepped tank within the larger central cell, together with smaller troughs outside the enclosure. It also notes pointed-arched entrances, surviving wooden doors and perforated projections associated with curtains. These details suggest that access, privacy and different points of water use were considered within the design.

The rooms are described as possible hammams rather than every function being conclusively established. That qualification matters. The presence of a tank or enclosed chamber is not enough to reconstruct a complete bathing routine, heating system or division of users. Those interpretations require additional evidence from the fabric and documentary record.

The distinction between the central courtyard and individual cells is nevertheless observable in the published description. Water is encountered at more than one scale: in a shared open area, within a room and at the exterior edge. A plan can make those relationships clearer than a photograph concerned only with a picturesque arch.

A name and an unsettled chronology

The AJK archaeology department also calls the site Balaspuri Baoli and associates its construction with a rani of Balaspur in the Dogra period. The federal catalogue gives a broad relative chronology of 1799–1849. These attributions do not establish an exact construction year, and they should not be combined into an invented inscription or foundation event.

Names can preserve patronage traditions even where documentary details remain incomplete. Recording the tradition is worthwhile, provided readers can distinguish it from a date demonstrated by a contemporary source. A measured survey, archival search and study of repairs could help refine the sequence.

The building as a connected system

Conservation should consider the tanks, walls, doors and external ground together. Repairing an attractive façade while overlooking drainage or structural movement would address only part of the building. Equally, a newly uniform surface could hide evidence of different construction episodes.

Rani Baoli rewards close attention precisely because its scale is manageable. A reader can follow the movement from entrance to courtyard to chamber and consider how water structured that movement. Keeping the location correct and the uncertain functions qualified allows the architecture to speak more clearly, without relying on a romantic story that the available evidence cannot securely sustain.
