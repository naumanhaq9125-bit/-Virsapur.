Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Mehrgarh before the Indus cities

Mehrgarh opens a view onto settlement before the Indus cities. New dating research makes its chronology a live question rather than a fixed origin story.

Mehrgarh matters because it allows questions to be asked before the familiar skyline of the Indus cities appears. On the Kachi plain near the Bolan Pass, archaeological mounds preserve evidence of settlement, building and burial over successive periods. The place offers an account of changing ways of living, not simply an earlier version of Mohenjo Daro waiting to become a city.

Pakistan’s Tentative List submission describes low mounds and substantial deposits, much of them beneath later alluvium. It records compartmented mud-brick structures whose functions are not always certain, as well as excavated cemeteries. The arrangement of small units may suggest storage in some cases, but an interpretation should remain attached to the specific structure and its evidence.

A mound is accumulated history

Mehrgarh is described as a tell: a mound built up through repeated occupation and the survival of earlier material beneath later construction. Its depth is therefore not a simple measure of age. Buildings can be rebuilt, abandoned, eroded or covered at different rates. Understanding the sequence requires the relationship between layers as well as measurements of when particular materials formed.

Excavations opened by the French archaeological mission in collaboration with Pakistan’s archaeological department brought this evidence into sustained research. The resulting importance of Mehrgarh should not be confused with a claim that every stage of early farming across a vast region is represented at this one location. It is an unusually informative record, not the whole record.

Why the dates are being reconsidered

A 2025 study by Benjamin Mutin and colleagues analysed tooth enamel from twenty-three burials in the Period I cemetery and modelled the results alongside their stratigraphic positions. It proposed a beginning around 5200–4900 BCE for that cemetery sequence, substantially later than influential older chronologies. The paper also argued for a shorter duration than had often been assumed.

The distinction between a dated sample, a cemetery phase and a regional historical claim is essential. A burial belongs to a particular context; the authors then use the pattern of dates to reconsider broader interpretations. Readers should be told both what was measured and how far the argument extends, rather than being given a newly simplified headline date for the invention of agriculture.

This research also shows why an older heritage description and a recent scientific paper may disagree. The discrepancy is not resolved by silently selecting the more dramatic age. Dates belong to methods, samples and arguments that can be checked and revised.

Mehrgarh’s value survives that revision. Its structures and burials still contribute to understanding settlement before the major Indus urban centres. Indeed, the ability to return to excavated material with new methods is part of its importance. The strongest introduction to the site leaves room for that ongoing enquiry: a history made more precise through evidence, rather than a contest to claim the earliest beginning.
