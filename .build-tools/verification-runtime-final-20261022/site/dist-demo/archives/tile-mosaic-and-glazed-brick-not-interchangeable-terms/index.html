Tile mosaic and glazed brick: not interchangeable terms

A blue surface may be painted tile, cut-tile mosaic or glazed masonry. Technique should be identified before style.

Blue-and-white architectural decoration is often described with a handful of interchangeable words: tile, mosaic, glazed brick. They may refer to related materials, but they do not describe the same process. A useful account begins by asking how a pattern was made and assembled, rather than treating colour as a complete technical identification.

In cut-tile mosaic, individual pieces are shaped and combined into a larger design. The Metropolitan Museum's study of Persian tiles explains this construction through a surviving mihrab from Isfahan: pieces cut from differently coloured glazed tiles form the composition. This is a comparative example, not proof that every patterned surface in Pakistan was manufactured in the same way.

Find the boundaries of the units

A painted tile can carry more than one colour or part of a design on a single unit. In a mosaic, changes in colour may coincide with joins between shaped pieces. Glazed brick introduces another relationship between a ceramic surface and masonry. These distinctions affect how curves, lettering and repeated patterns are produced, and how damaged areas can be understood.

From an appropriate viewing distance, look for seams and repeated modules. Does a flower continue across rectangular tile joints? Are small pieces fitted around the curves of a letter? Does the glazed face follow the dimensions and bond of masonry units? A clear answer may require technical photography or conservation documentation; a distant image can conceal the relevant joins.

Compare without flattening regional difference

The Shah Jahan Mosque at Thatta, Wazir Khan Mosque in Lahore and Shah Rukn-e-Alam's tomb in Multan are valuable starting points for comparison. Their decorative programmes should not be collapsed into one generic blue-tile tradition. Materials, geometry, placement and repair histories deserve individual description.

AKDN's Lahore conservation account distinguishes glazed tile mosaics from painted surface decoration at Wazir Khan Mosque. That vocabulary is useful because a single building can contain several techniques. Calling every coloured surface a fresco, or every repeated design a mosaic, obscures the work of different crafts and the different conservation problems they present.

Repairs can complicate what we see

A replaced area may imitate the colour and geometry of an earlier surface while using a different method. Conversely, an irregular colour can result from weathering rather than a different construction phase. The Met's published discussion of its Isfahan mihrab records extensive restoration, a reminder that even a museum masterpiece has a material history after its initial making.

A responsible caption can therefore say patterned glazed ceramic decoration when a closer technical identification has not been established. Precision includes knowing when to remain general.

The visual reward is substantial. Once the eye begins to follow unit boundaries, a wall becomes a record of cutting, arranging, fitting and repair. The pattern is still beautiful, but its beauty is connected to decisions made by skilled people rather than explained by colour alone.
