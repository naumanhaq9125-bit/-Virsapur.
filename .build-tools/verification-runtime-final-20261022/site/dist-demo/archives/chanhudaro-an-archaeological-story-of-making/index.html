Chanhudaro: an archaeological story of making

Chanhudaro’s workshop evidence reveals the Indus world through making. Bead drills, unfinished pieces and furnace-related fragments can be more informative than a finished ornament alone.

Chanhudaro offers a way into Indus archaeology through the evidence of work. Its importance is not confined to complete objects that might look impressive in a display case. Drills, unfinished beads, fragments and production debris can reveal decisions that disappear in the polished result. A workshop is understood through the relationship among those materials, not through one attractive find.

The national archaeological catalogue records Ernest Mackay’s excavations of 1935–36 and identifies brick houses, drainage and a range of objects. The settlement therefore provides a domestic and urban setting for craft activity. It should not be imagined as an isolated factory detached from the other requirements of life.

The hole inside the bead

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer’s study of Indus trade and technology discusses a Chanhudaro carnelian-bead workshop with numerous drills and drill-making waste. The specialised material known as ernestite takes its name from Mackay. The association of tools, waste and products allows a more detailed account of manufacture than the finished beads could provide alone.

A bead’s perforation is particularly informative because it records a process that the outer polish may obscure. Tool marks can be compared, and unfinished pieces can show where work stopped. Such evidence supports questions about technique and specialisation. It does not automatically tell us the name, status or personal identity of the person who used the drill.

Production debris also changes the meaning of an apparently insignificant fragment. An abandoned blank may reveal an intermediate stage; a broken tool may indicate a technical problem. Their value lies in context and association. Removing them as souvenirs would destroy precisely the relationships that make them useful.

Reconsidering an old interpretation

Massimo Vidale’s 1987 study returned to another class of Chanhudaro material: the so-called paste plaques and cylinders. Mackay had connected them with raw material for faience. Vidale proposed instead that some were components of small high-temperature installations, potentially associated with glazing processes. He treated traces of glaze and the composition of the fragments as evidence for testing that interpretation.

The study also discusses an unfinished steatite bead fragment that helps illuminate a manufacturing sequence. This is a good example of how renewed examination can extract information from material already known. Archaeological progress is not limited to opening a fresh trench; it can come from asking a better question of an old collection.

Chanhudaro’s workshop evidence should therefore be presented as an argument, not a romantic scene of craftsmen reconstructed without qualification. What was found together? Which stage of production does it represent? Which interpretation remains a proposal? Those questions allow readers to follow the reasoning.

The result is a human-scale view of the Indus world. Large cities and long-distance exchange remain important, but here they can be approached through the precision of a hole, the shape of a blank and the discarded remains of making. Small things become evidence of substantial technical knowledge.
