Proverbs as records of use, not timeless wisdom

A proverb records an expression in circulation, not an unquestionable truth. Dictionaries and critical studies help separate wording, use and social judgement.

A proverb can sound persuasive partly because it arrives already shaped for repetition. Its brevity suggests experience condensed into a sentence. That effect deserves attention, but it is not a guarantee of truth. A saying may comfort, tease, warn, justify authority or express a prejudice, depending on who uses it and what happens around it.

Rauf Parekh’s study of Urdu proverb dictionaries traces a substantial history of collecting these expressions. He notes their value for examining social settings and changes in language. A dictionary entry establishes that a form has been recorded; it does not tell us that every speaker agrees with it or uses it in the same way.

A critical study by Tufail Chandio approaches Sindhi proverbs through their potential to reinforce social attitudes. Its broad account should itself be read critically rather than treated as proof that sayings determine an entire society’s behaviour. The useful question it raises is narrower: what work can a familiar expression do in a particular exchange?

Record the moment of use

Imagine two people using the same saying, one earnestly and one with deliberate irony. A transcript that preserves only the words may make their positions look identical. Tone, timing, the audience’s response and the relationship between speakers can distinguish endorsement from criticism.

A careful field note would therefore identify the occasion, the speaker’s intended meaning where they explain it, and the response. It should also distinguish a proverb offered because a researcher requested examples from one used spontaneously in conversation. Both are valuable records, but they answer different questions about everyday use.

Older dictionaries introduce another layer. The compiler may provide a literal meaning, a conventional explanation or an illustrative anecdote. Those editorial choices are not necessarily statements made by the people who use the proverb today. A dated entry can be compared with contemporary testimony without requiring either to cancel the other.

Heritage does not require endorsement

Some expressions carry assumptions about gender, status or social groups that deserve examination rather than celebration. Preserving a record of such language can support historical understanding, but presenting it as charming collective wisdom may reproduce the injury it contains. Context and purpose should determine whether quotation is necessary at all.

A useful educational comparison might place two explanations of a saying together, showing how readers disagree about its force. That is different from collecting contradictory proverbs as evidence that a language is confused. People draw on expressions selectively, and contradiction can reveal the range of arguments available to them.

For a heritage archive, the strongest unit is therefore not an isolated line on an attractive background. It is a documented expression with its source, date where known, interpretation and conditions of use. Such a record leaves room for change, disagreement and humour. Proverbs become evidence of people speaking to one another, rather than anonymous commands handed down unchanged from an undifferentiated past.

References

  2. Social Dynamics of Sindhi Society: An analysis of Proverbs and their impact on Mindset — Tufail Chandio / Kalich Research Journal (accessed 2026-10-22)
