A courtyard is more than empty space

A courtyard connects rooms through light, routes and views. Reading domestic architecture requires attention to how the open centre works, not just to the ornament around it.

The centre of a courtyard house can seem to be the part where nothing has been built. In architectural terms, that apparent emptiness is active. Rooms face it, routes pass through or around it, and openings connect spaces that would otherwise be separated by walls.

The centre belongs to the plan

Sethi House in Peshawar is documented in official heritage material, including the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa archaeology report's courtyard view. The image offers a useful starting point for looking at the relationship between an open centre and its surrounding elevations. It does not, on its own, explain every room's historical use.

The first question is how the house is entered. Does the approach reveal the court immediately, or does a threshold interrupt the view? Then consider how rooms connect with the centre and with one another. A court can distribute movement without every room needing to open directly onto a street.

Vertical relationships matter as well. An upper-level opening may overlook the same court from a different position. That shared visual space does not mean that access to every level was identical. A photograph can show architectural connections while leaving the social arrangements unresolved.

Light, shade and daily activity

An open court admits daylight, but its effect depends on the height and orientation of the surrounding building. A narrow, tall enclosure and a broad, low one cannot be assumed to behave in the same way. The word “courtyard” identifies a spatial relationship, not a universal environmental formula.

Claims about cooling require similar care. Shade, airflow, materials and water can interact, but a general statement that all old houses were naturally comfortable conceals variation. The useful approach is to study the particular arrangement rather than turn the courtyard into an effortless answer to every climatic problem.

Daily uses may also change over time. A space might accommodate several activities at different hours, yet assigning those activities to a named historic family requires evidence. Architecture can suggest possibilities without giving us permission to script domestic scenes.

Ornament does not replace circulation

Carved wood, painted surfaces and decorative openings often dominate photographs of historic houses. They deserve attention, but their relationship with the plan matters. An elaborately treated opening may also frame a route or mediate a view. Seeing only its ornament removes part of its architectural work.

WCLA's presentation of Lahore's historic environment offers another route into domestic and urban settings, but individual houses should not be treated as interchangeable examples of one timeless way of life. Sethi House, Haveli Sujan Singh and Haveli Nau Nihal Singh each require their own documented history.

A good courtyard account therefore begins with relationships that can be observed and identifies the evidence behind broader social claims. The open centre becomes more than a photogenic void: it is the space through which much of the building's organisation can be understood, while the lives once arranged around it remain a subject for careful historical inquiry.
