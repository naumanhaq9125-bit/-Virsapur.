Babra Tombs and a regional funerary tradition

The Babra graveyard near Bela contains several funerary forms, inviting a reading of a monument group rather than one uniform “Chaukhandi” type.

A cemetery is rarely a single architectural statement. At Babra, north of Bela, the official archaeological inventory describes a group of funerary structures with different materials and forms. Reading these differences is more rewarding than assigning the whole place one convenient label. The graveyard is associated with the Jams of Lasbela, but that association does not by itself identify every person commemorated there or establish when each monument was made.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums records a brick-built tomb, stepped graves, a pillared sandstone canopy, lime-plastered graves and another group within an enclosing wall. It also records the local name Tharle. These details suggest an ensemble that deserves to be understood through its parts: an enclosure, a roofed space, a grave marker and a tomb are not interchangeable objects.

A regional family, not a single design

The catalogue compares some of Babra’s monuments with the Chaukhandi-type funerary architecture of Sindh and Balochistan. It places the comparison broadly in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. That is a useful starting point, but it is not a securely established construction date for every element of the cemetery.

Scholarship on Chaukhandi tombs shows how a name associated with a particular cemetery became a wider classificatory term. Such terminology helps researchers recognise relationships across sites. It can also encourage a misleading impression that all related monuments were made at the same time, by the same workshop, or for the same community. The comparison should lead to closer observation, not end it.

At Babra, the contrast between a brick tomb and a sandstone canopy is already enough to open questions. Does a change in material correspond to a different construction period, a different patron, or another practical choice? The inventory alone cannot decide. A documented inscription, a measured survey or a carefully established building sequence would be needed before one explanation became a historical claim.

See the group before the detail

A thoughtful record would begin with the arrangement of the cemetery, then move to individual structures. The relationship between a canopy and the graves it shelters matters as much as the profile of a carved stone. Likewise, an enclosing wall may organise access or distinguish a group, but its meaning should not be inferred solely from an aerial photograph.

For visitors, this approach encourages respectful attention without turning the burial ground into a decorative backdrop. Looking closely does not require climbing a marker, touching a fragile surface or moving loose material for a better image. A photograph should preserve the relationship between an object and its setting wherever possible.

Babra’s interest lies in its variety and in the questions that variety makes possible. It belongs to the heritage of Lasbela as a specific place, not merely as an outlying example of a better-known cemetery near Karachi. Keeping its different forms, local associations and evidential limits visible allows that regional identity to emerge without inventing a complete dynastic narrative.
