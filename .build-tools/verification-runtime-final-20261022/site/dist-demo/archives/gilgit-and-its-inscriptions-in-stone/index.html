Gilgit and its inscriptions in stone

Danyor’s inscription and Kargah’s carved Buddha reveal different forms of evidence for Gilgit’s connections—and the care required to interpret stone records.

Stone can preserve writing and imagery in the same regional landscape without making them the same kind of document. Near Gilgit, Danyor’s inscription and the carved Buddha at Kargah offer a useful contrast. One requires the reading of a text and its script; the other requires the interpretation of an image, its setting and its formal details. Both need careful documentation before large historical claims are built upon them.

The federal catalogue identifies Danyor as a royal inscription in a late Brahmi script tradition. That description is an orientation, not a translation. The distinction between a script and a language is important: naming the form of writing does not, on its own, explain what the record says or how it should be dated.

A difficult surface is part of the evidence

Gérard Fussman’s 1978 discussion records his examination of the Danyor inscription and explains why he postponed a full edition. He describes shallow lettering on an irregular, fissured surface and difficulties obtaining a complete photographic record. This is a valuable account of scholarly caution. The inscription’s existence was not in doubt, but secure reading required better documentation.

The Collège de France archive also preserves a catalogued photographic record associated with Danyor. Such material allows the history of documentation to be traced alongside the history recorded on the stone. An older photograph can be evidence for what was visible at a particular time, although it must be assessed for lighting, framing and technical limits.

Image is not inscription

Kargah’s Buddha is described in the official inventory as a standing figure carved in low relief within a niche high on a rock face. Its raised hand and treatment of the robe are features through which specialists compare Buddhist imagery. It is not a written itinerary left by a traveller, and it should not be presented as though it supplied a named patron or exact date without further evidence.

The comparison with Danyor clarifies what each source can do. A text may preserve names or titles, if securely read. An image can reveal iconographic choices and the significance of a particular location. Neither automatically proves how far a political authority extended or which route every traveller followed through the region.

Connections need precise claims

Gilgit’s position invites narratives of exchange across mountains, but a broad crossroads label should not replace analysis. The origin of a script, the language of a text, the affiliation of a ruler and the movement of an artist are separate questions. They may intersect, but one cannot simply stand in for the others.

These stone records are most compelling when their limits remain visible. Do not trace letters, wet a surface or climb towards an image to improve a photograph. Specialist documentation should preserve the evidence rather than alter it. Danyor and Kargah offer access to different historical worlds; the task is to explain those worlds without making the stone say more than can securely be read.
