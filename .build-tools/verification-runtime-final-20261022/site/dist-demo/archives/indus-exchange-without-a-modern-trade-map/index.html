Indus exchange without a modern trade map

Evidence for Indus exchange can connect materials to sources without revealing the exact route or terms of a transaction. Geological and isotope studies help distinguish different kinds of movement.

A map of ancient exchange often uses arrows that resemble modern shipping routes. The arrows are easy to understand, but they can imply more than the evidence establishes. An object may connect a settlement with a raw-material source without telling us which path it followed, how many hands it passed through or what was exchanged for it.

Randall Law’s study of Harappa’s rock and mineral assemblage provides a useful alternative. It compares archaeological material with samples from potential geological sources. The resulting connections are not all presented with the same confidence. His summary distinguishes firm analytical assignments from reasonable but less directly tested inferences.

A line is an argument

Law explicitly explains that the pathways drawn on his maps are conjectural. A solid line can indicate strong evidence for a material-source relationship without documenting the exact road, river journey or coastal passage. This is a small cartographic distinction with large consequences for interpretation.

The study of steatite illustrates why source research is necessary. Potential deposits occur in several regions, and appearance alone cannot securely choose among them. Comparing the archaeological objects with geological samples supplies a more disciplined basis for an acquisition argument.

Even a strong match does not reveal the complete transaction. Material might move directly, through intermediaries or after being partly worked. Those possibilities require other evidence. A map should not silently turn a source attribution into proof that a named city controlled a distant quarry.

Objects and people leave different traces

A 2013 study by Kenoyer, Price and Burton explored another approach: isotope analysis of tooth enamel from individuals buried at Harappa and Ur. Its published abstract presents the work as a preliminary investigation of whether non-local people and movement could be identified more directly.

That is a different question from the geological origin of a bead. The movement of an object does not require its maker to travel with it, and the movement of a person does not identify their occupation. An isotope result should not be converted into a biography of a merchant without further support.

What exchange evidence can make visible

Taken together, these approaches widen the history beyond a list of exotic goods. They can illuminate changing access to materials, connections between regions and the mobility of people. They also reveal how much remains unknown about the institutions and everyday arrangements through which those connections operated.

For an atlas reader, a useful exchange map would identify the kind of evidence behind each connection and the period to which it belongs. It would separate a confirmed material source from a suggested route and avoid combining many centuries into one supposedly simultaneous network.

The Indus world does not need a modern-looking trade diagram to appear connected. Its relationships become more compelling when the evidence is allowed to retain different levels of certainty. A carefully explained line can then open a question about movement instead of closing it with an invented itinerary.
