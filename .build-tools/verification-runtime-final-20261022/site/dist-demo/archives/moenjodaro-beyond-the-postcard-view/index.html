Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Moenjodaro beyond the postcard view

A view beyond Moenjodaro’s famous monuments reveals a large, partly excavated urban landscape whose neighbourhoods, later remains and conservation needs deserve equal attention.

Moenjodaro is often compressed into two images: the Great Bath and the familiar silhouette of the mound behind it. Neither is an adequate picture of the city. A more revealing introduction begins by changing scale, moving from the famous structure to its surrounding buildings, from those buildings to a neighbourhood, and from the excavated neighbourhood to the wider archaeological landscape.

UNESCO’s account distinguishes a western elevated sector from extensive lower-town remains to the east. It also makes clear that excavation has exposed only part of the protected area. The visible walls are therefore not the full outline of everything that once existed. A blank space on a visitor’s mental map may represent unexcavated deposits rather than an ancient absence of settlement.

A city seen in fragments

The archaeological photographs assembled by Harappa range across different excavation areas, showing streets, wells, rooms and large structures. Their area labels are helpful because they keep an image attached to a particular location. A photograph of one well is evidence about that installation; it should not silently become a picture of every household’s water supply.

The same discipline applies to an itinerary. Choosing a few contrasting examples can be more illuminating than treating the visit as a race between celebrated monuments. Compare a substantial public-looking space with a smaller domestic arrangement. Notice how an entrance meets a lane, or how the level of a well relates to the surviving walls around it. Such comparisons reveal the different scales at which a city operates.

More than one time in the view

The Buddhist stupa on the western mound belongs to a much later period than the Indus city beneath it. Its visibility in panoramic photographs can make the two appear to belong to one architectural episode. They do not. The later structure is part of the place’s long history, while the urban remains require their own chronology.

This is why a picturesque skyline needs interpretation. Height, proximity and prominence do not establish contemporaneity. The element most easily recognised at a distance may not be the one that best explains the period a visitor has come to understand.

The exposed city needs care

UNESCO’s conservation discussion identifies saline action associated with the water table as a threat to the remains. This introduces a final change of perspective: excavation is not simply the moment when a hidden city is revealed for everyone to enjoy. Once exposed, fragile fabric requires continuing decisions about protection and maintenance.

The site is consequently neither a complete recovered city nor a static collection of ruins. It is a place where archaeological knowledge, surviving material and conservation choices meet. Moving beyond the postcard does not mean abandoning the Great Bath. It means giving the lesser-known walls, routes and unfinished questions enough attention that the celebrated monument becomes part of a city again.
