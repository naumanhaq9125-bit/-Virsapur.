Living Traditions

What community-led safeguarding changes

Community-led safeguarding changes who defines the work, controls the record and judges whether it remains useful to the people concerned.

Documentation can produce a valuable archive without ensuring that a practice remains viable. A recording may preserve a performance, yet say little about whether younger practitioners have opportunities to learn. Community-led safeguarding starts from a different question: what do the people who recognise and maintain this heritage consider necessary for its continuation?

UNESCO’s guidance on involving tradition bearers gives practitioners an active role in identifying and inventorying their heritage. Its discussion of inventories also recognises that communities may limit the information they provide. An inventory is therefore not simply a collector’s attempt to gather everything that can be recorded. Its scope and access conditions are part of the work.

From subjects to decision-makers

Imagine a proposed archive of a craft practice. An externally designed project might prioritise photographs of finished objects because they display well. Practitioners might instead identify teaching time, access to tools or recognition of individual makers as the more urgent concern. Neither viewpoint should be invented in an article about a real community. The example shows why the project’s priorities need to emerge through discussion rather than be settled in advance.

Control over description matters too. A label chosen by an outsider may flatten distinctions that practitioners consider important. Names of techniques, relationships between stages of work and the meaning of particular occasions can require explanation in the language used by the people involved. A database should be able to accommodate that knowledge instead of forcing every practice into an overly broad category.

Community leadership does not require a claim that every person agrees. Different generations, households or practitioners may have different interests. A useful process makes those differences discussable and records how decisions were reached. It should not treat an institution’s preferred representative as automatically authorised to settle every question.

Measure the result differently

The number of files uploaded is an easy output to count. It is not the same as successful safeguarding. A more useful evaluation asks whether the agreed aims were met, whether participants can access and use the record, and whether the work creates burdens or risks that were not anticipated.

For an editorial platform, this changes the language of achievement. Publishing a story should not be described as saving a tradition unless there is evidence for that much larger claim. It may be a contribution to documentation, an invitation to learn or part of a wider practitioner-led effort.

The difference is not modesty for its own sake. It is accuracy about agency. A community-led approach recognises that a heritage practice has a life beyond its appearance in an archive, and that those who sustain it should help determine how the archive serves that life.
