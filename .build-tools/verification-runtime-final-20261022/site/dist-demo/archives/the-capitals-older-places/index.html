Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The capital’s older places

Saidpur, Sarai Kharbuza and Shah Allah Ditta reveal different histories within the capital’s landscape before and alongside the planned city.

Islamabad’s planned form can make its landscape seem to begin with the modern capital. Older settlements, religious buildings and route infrastructure show otherwise. Saidpur, Sarai Kharbuza and Shah Allah Ditta preserve different kinds of evidence for places that cannot be understood solely through the city’s sector plan.

The federal inventory describes Saidpur’s religious complex as including temples and a dharamshala. Its architectural record identifies brick construction and a principal shrine with a domed superstructure. These are specific buildings within a settlement, not generic reminders of an undefined ancient past. Their religious identities and uses deserve to remain clear even when the surrounding village acquires new visitor-oriented functions.

A settlement is more than its oldest building

A temple can provide a focal point for understanding Saidpur, but it cannot stand for every household or period of village life. Buildings of worship, accommodation and ordinary residence organise different kinds of access. The relationship between them is part of the settlement’s history. Treating the entire place as a decorative heritage quarter risks obscuring those distinctions.

Sarai Kharbuza introduces movement through the region. Its catalogue describes an enclosure with opposing gateways, accommodation cells and verandas, together with a mosque in one corner. It also records repairs and a reported older water feature. This is evidence of alteration within a route-related institution, rather than an intact single-period complex waiting outside modern history.

Natural shelter and human modification

At Shah Allah Ditta, the record describes two natural rock shelters beside a spring. Added walls, plaster, a platform and water-management features indicate human use and modification. The geological age of a shelter is not the age of its occupation, and the presence of a later wall does not date every activity within it. Those questions require archaeological context rather than a single impressive antiquity claim.

The spring and associated channel and tank make water part of the interpretation. A shelter is not simply an isolated cavity in a hillside: its relationship with a dependable resource may help explain use. Yet the significance of water does not establish the identity of every group associated with the place or prove a continuous occupation sequence.

Several histories inside the capital’s landscape

Together, the three places show why pre-capital history cannot be reduced to one civilisation or one route. Saidpur contributes evidence of settlement and religious institutions; Sarai Kharbuza of accommodation and movement; Shah Allah Ditta of shelter and water. Their differences are precisely what makes the comparison useful.

Accurate geography matters as well. Nearby places outside Islamabad’s administrative boundary retain their own districts and histories; they need not be absorbed into the capital to make its heritage richer. A planned city can coexist with older places without having created or exhausted their significance. Recognising those places allows Islamabad to be read as a contemporary urban project within an already inhabited landscape, rather than as an entirely new beginning.
