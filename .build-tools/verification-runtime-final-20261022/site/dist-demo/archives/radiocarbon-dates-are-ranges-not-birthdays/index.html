Radiocarbon dates are ranges, not birthdays

Radiocarbon dating estimates the age of a sampled organism, not automatically the founding year of a settlement. Calibration, context and reported uncertainty are essential parts of the result.

A radiocarbon result is sometimes turned into a headline announcing the exact age of a building or civilisation. That shortcut loses two essential distinctions. The laboratory measures a sample, and the result has uncertainty. Connecting it to a historical event requires an archaeological argument as well as a measurement.

Oxford’s radiocarbon guidance explains that the method concerns carbon incorporated while an organism was alive. After death, radioactive decay changes the amount remaining. The resulting measurement is conventionally expressed in radiocarbon years before 1950, with an uncertainty. In this terminology, present does not move forward with the date on today’s calendar.

Why calibration is necessary

The amount of radiocarbon in the atmosphere has not remained constant. A radiocarbon age is therefore not simply a calendar year waiting to be subtracted from 1950. Calibration compares the result with material of known age to estimate possible calendar dates.

Oxford’s explanation shows why this can produce more than one likely calendar interval. The shape of the calibration curve matters, as does measurement uncertainty. Reporting only a midpoint can hide the actual distribution and create an impression of precision that the analysis does not support.

Historic England’s guidance recommends stating the calibration method and curve, together with the probability associated with the reported range. A range without its probability is incomplete information. The uncertainty is not an embarrassing weakness to remove; it is part of what the result means.

Which event has been dated?

Consider a hypothetical timber reused in a later structure. Dating its carbon would not automatically date the rebuilding. The relationship between the tree’s growth, the cutting of the timber and its eventual deposition would have to be considered. The same general question applies whenever sampled material may be older than the context in which it was found.

A short-lived sample securely associated with an event can answer a different question from unidentified charcoal in mixed soil. Neither should be described simply as the date of the site. Sample identification and context belong beside the number in any serious interpretation.

Results that can be checked again

Historic England also stresses retaining the laboratory identifier, the uncalibrated result and its uncertainty. Calibration curves are refined over time, so a future researcher may need to revisit the calculation. A polished timeline that preserves only a rounded calendar year makes that work harder.

For readers encountering early settlements such as Mehrgarh, the practical lesson is to ask what was sampled, where it came from and how the published date is expressed. A revised estimate need not mean that the site’s importance has disappeared; it may mean that the evidence has become better understood.

Radiocarbon dating is powerful precisely because its assumptions and uncertainties can be examined. Treating dates as supported ranges rather than birthdays allows scientific evidence to strengthen history without pretending that it offers a certainty beyond its reach.
