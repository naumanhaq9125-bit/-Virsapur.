Translation and the problem of a beautiful mistranslation

A beautiful translation can still mislead if its choices are hidden. Comparing specific versions helps separate poetic transformation from unsupported attribution.

A line in English may be memorable, moving and entirely convincing as a poem while remaining a poor guide to the text it claims to translate. The problem is not beauty itself. It is the moment when a translator’s choices become invisible and the resulting words are attributed directly to an author who never wrote them in that form.

Translation involves decisions about meaning, sound, rhythm and the needs of a new audience. Those priorities can pull in different directions. A version made for singing may solve a musical problem differently from one prepared for a scholarly edition. Neither purpose removes the obligation to explain what kind of version is being offered.

Nasir Mehmood and Hazrat Umar’s study compares three English translations of a selected stanza from Iqbal’s Shikwah. They find differences in procedure and form while arguing that a core of theme and tone remains broadly shared. The study’s limited selection is important: its conclusion is not a verdict on every line of the poem or on translation in general.

Compare choices, not just fluency

Begin with a passage whose source can be identified. Establish which edition a translator used and whether the published wording is a translation, an adaptation or a quotation through another writer. A polished sentence copied from an unattributed image has not passed that first test, however plausible it sounds.

Then ask specific questions. Has a concrete image become an abstract statement? Has an ambiguous pronoun been assigned a definite identity? Has a question become an assertion? These are points to investigate with a knowledgeable reader of the source language, not invitations to declare a translation wrong merely because another version differs.

Research on creative translation of Bulleh Shah’s poetry, published in Kalich Research Journal, also treats the style of the English text as an object of analysis. That focus is useful: the translated poem has its own verbal construction. Its effects should be examined rather than mistaken for direct, transparent access to the original.

Give interpretation a visible place

An explanatory note can be more honest than forcing every nuance into the main line. It may identify an allusion, describe an alternative reading or explain why a literal rendering would mislead. Such notes need not overwhelm a general reader; they can reveal one consequential choice at the point where it matters.

A heritage website should credit translators separately from poets and retain the edition or recording behind a quotation. If the wording is a new paraphrase, label it as such. If no dependable source can be found, an appealing line should remain out of the poet’s mouth until the attribution is resolved.

The aim is not to make translated literature timid or graceless. It is to let readers appreciate artistry without losing track of responsibility. A beautiful version becomes a stronger bridge when its construction is visible: we can admire what the translator has made and still ask how it relates to the work that prompted it.

References

  1. Exploring the Invariant Core: An Analysis of Multiple Translations of a Selection from Iqbal’s Shikwah — Nasir Mehmood and Hazrat Umar / Bazyāft, University of the Punjab (accessed 2026-10-22)
  2. Foregrounding and Creative Translation: Stylistic Deviation in the English Translation of Bulleh Shah’s Poetry — Mehvish Riaz / Kalich Research Journal (accessed 2026-10-22)
