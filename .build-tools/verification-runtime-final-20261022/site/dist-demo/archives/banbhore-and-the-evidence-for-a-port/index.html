Banbhore and the evidence for a port

Banbhore’s port history is supported by its creek-side position, urban remains and evidence of production and exchange. Recent ivory-workshop research adds labour and manufacturing to the familiar harbour story.

A port is more than a place where ships stop. It connects landing, storage, exchange, manufacture and movement inland. Banbhore's importance emerges from this wider set of relationships: a fortified settlement beside Gharo Creek, internal streets and substantial buildings, activity beyond the walls, and finds that connect the site to networks across the Indian Ocean.

Pakistan's Tentative List submission locates the site on the creek's northern bank and describes a long occupation sequence extending into the early thirteenth century. It identifies several urban zones and notes that earlier deposits are affected by waterlogging. The visible surface therefore does not provide an equally complete view of every period in the site's history.

The port in the plan

The federal archaeological catalogue describes fortification walls, gateways, streets, a mosque and an industrial area beyond the defended settlement. The UNESCO-hosted account also emphasises imported ceramic and metal goods. Taken together, these are different kinds of evidence: architecture shows the arrangement of space, while objects help investigate production, circulation and contact.

A creek-side position alone would not establish a complete trading history. The argument becomes stronger when location is considered alongside excavated material and the organisation of the settlement. Equally, an imported object does not by itself identify the nationality of its owner or the exact route by which it travelled.

What discarded ivory can reveal

Research reported by archaeologist Simone Mantellini through the Fondation Max van Berchem describes ivory-working evidence in Trench 9. Two workshops were associated with offcuts, small chips, powder and unfinished or broken objects. The report places this production in the twelfth century and distinguishes the abundant manufacturing waste from the much smaller number of complete pieces.

Waste is especially informative because it records making rather than only possession. An attractive finished object might have arrived from elsewhere; a concentration of intermediate fragments and offcuts can reveal stages of work at the site itself. The workshop shifts attention from the harbour as a point of arrival to the town as a place of skilled labour.

The same project sets out further questions about manufacturing techniques and the origin of raw material, including proposed scientific analysis. Those research aims should not be presented as already completed answers. Evidence that elephant ivory was worked at Banbhore does not, on its own, establish where every tusk originated.

Connecting scales of evidence

Banbhore becomes most intelligible when the large plan and the small fragment are read together. Walls and gates organise movement through the town; a workshop locates an activity; the waste from that activity helps reconstruct a sequence of actions. Each contributes something the others cannot provide.

For visitors, this means allowing the museum, excavation records and landscape to complement one another. A monumental wall may provide the strongest photograph, but an unassuming fragment can explain how the settlement participated in a much wider economy. The port's history includes both the routes across water and the hands at work on land.
