Seals and sealings: an archaeological distinction

A seal makes an impression; a sealing preserves one. That difference changes what each object can tell us about handling, authority and archaeological context.

A museum photograph can place an engraved seal beside a crisp impression and make them appear to be a single ancient discovery. They are not necessarily the same kind of evidence. The seal is the object used to create a mark. A sealing is material bearing that mark. Before interpreting either, establish what the catalogue actually describes.

Read the label before the image

The Metropolitan Museum of Art labels its Indus example as a stamp seal with a modern impression. The seal is catalogued as burnt steatite and assigned to the Mature Harappan period. The accompanying impression helps a viewer see the design, but its modern manufacture means it cannot independently demonstrate an ancient act of sealing.

That distinction also affects orientation. A recessed design and the raised impression it produces are counterparts, not identical views. A reader comparing photographs needs to know which surface is shown before treating differences in direction as evidence about an inscription. Good documentation should make this clear rather than rely on visual familiarity.

Tool, action and surviving trace

The museum discusses stamp seals in relation to ownership and status. Those broad possibilities do not identify the owner of this particular object or reconstruct every occasion on which it was used. A seal could survive without the material it once marked. Conversely, an impression might survive when the tool that made it has disappeared.

A simple analytical exercise helps. Imagine two otherwise similar discoveries: a seal found among accumulated settlement debris, and an impression recovered on material still associated with a container. The second situation would offer an additional connection between mark and use. This is a hypothetical comparison, not a report of a specific find at Chanhudaro. Its purpose is to show why archaeological association matters.

Mackay's account of the Chanhudaro excavations discusses different kinds of seal-like objects across occupation levels. It should be read as an early excavation report, not as a timeless explanation of every object's purpose. Its classifications and historical language require critical assessment alongside the recorded material.

The useful question is consequently not just “What does this symbol mean?” It is “What happened to this object, and which part of that history survives?” A catalogue that separates ancient tool, ancient trace and modern demonstration gives readers a firmer starting point. It also protects an attractive reconstruction from quietly becoming evidence for an event that has not actually been documented.
