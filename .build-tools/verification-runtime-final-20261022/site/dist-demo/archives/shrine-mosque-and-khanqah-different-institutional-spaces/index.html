Shrine, mosque and khanqah: different institutional spaces

Mosque, shrine and khanqah describe different emphases within religious life. Their functions may overlap inside one precinct without making the terms interchangeable.

A religious precinct may contain a prayer hall, a revered burial, rooms associated with teaching and spaces for hospitality. Calling the entire place a mosque can conceal the tomb; calling everything a shrine can conceal congregational prayer. The terms are useful when they help us distinguish activities and histories, rather than when they force a complex institution into a single box.

In broad usage, a mosque is a place of Muslim prayer. A shrine identifies a focus of reverence, often associated with a saint or a burial, but the word alone does not specify every building around it. A khanqah is associated with Sufi religious life and may involve assembly, teaching, residence or hospitality. The combination and institutional meaning vary by place and period.

A building with several kinds of room

The federal catalogue’s account of Thagas Khanqah in Ghanche describes a hall, an eastern entrance veranda and smaller meditation rooms on two sides. It also records nearby astanas associated with Mir Arif and Mir Ishaq. This is more informative than treating a photograph of its timberwork as a complete account of the institution.

The recorded arrangement suggests useful questions for reading the site: which space accommodated gathering, which offered relative seclusion, and how did the adjacent sacred structures relate to the principal building? These are questions for investigation, not permission to assign a known ceremony to every room.

A comparative Archnet account of Samarkand’s Registan describes an earlier khanqah serving alongside a madrasa. That example is outside Pakistan, but it illustrates why institutional relationships matter. Teaching, lodging and devotion can be linked without becoming identical activities. It would be misleading to infer the plan of Thagas from the Samarkand example.

Distinguish the precinct from its centre

At a saint’s shrine, the tomb may be the element that gives the larger place its name. The visible precinct, however, can include additions from different periods. A date associated with the saint’s life is not automatically the construction date of a courtyard wall, gateway or prayer space.

This distinction matters when describing a complex such as the shrine of Bahauddin Zakariya in Multan. The subject of an architectural account might be the tomb structure; the subject of a devotional account might be the saint; the subject of a contemporary institutional account might be the people responsible for the precinct. A good article says which of these it is discussing.

Names should clarify, not settle belief

ICCROM’s discussion of living religious heritage is a reminder that conservation must engage with religious use as well as material fabric. For an archive, this means listening to how an institution names its own spaces while retaining the ability to explain historical changes and differing sources.

A caption can be precise without becoming lengthy: identify the building, the larger complex and the activity actually documented. Avoid guessing from a dome, a courtyard or a group of visitors. Architectural forms travel between institutions, and the same structure can acquire new uses.

The result is a richer reading of sacred heritage. A mosque, a shrine and a khanqah may be neighbours or parts of one compound. Their relationship is often the story worth telling, rather than a problem to remove through a single label.
