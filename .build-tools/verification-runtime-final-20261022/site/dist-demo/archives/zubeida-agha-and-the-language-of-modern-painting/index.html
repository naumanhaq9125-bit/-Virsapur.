Zubeida Agha and the language of modern painting

A documented late painting and an archive of exhibition work place Zubeida Agha’s abstraction within a wider practice of making and organising art.

Zubeida Agha’s painting can be approached more closely by beginning with a particular work than by assigning her a rank among pioneers. Composition, dated 1988 and presented at the 2024 Venice Biennale, offers one such starting point. Its significance lies in the decisions within a painted surface, not simply in its position on a national timeline.

In the Biennale’s catalogue text, curator Devika Singh describes a central grouping of asymmetrical geometric forms, with uneven rather than mechanically exact edges. She also draws attention to a band across the foreground that interrupts an easy resolution of the image. This is a named curator’s reading of a specific painting; it should not be presented as a fresh visual inspection undertaken for this article.

Abstraction as a set of decisions

The description points towards a useful question: how do shapes relate when they are not required to illustrate a recognisable scene? Edge, interval, colour and placement become active parts of the work. Geometric form does not necessarily mean impersonal regularity. An irregular boundary can make a shape feel made and contingent rather than simply calculated.

The Jordan National Gallery of Fine Arts lists another work, Abstract Study, dated 1967. Its catalogue establishes a separate object and an earlier point in the practice. A title and date alone do not justify a detailed account of its appearance, but they provide a basis for future comparison with the later Composition. Such comparison should examine actual works or reliable reproductions rather than assume that all abstraction follows one continuous stylistic formula.

The painter also made institutions

Agha’s archive expands the subject beyond finished canvases. The Lahore Biennale Foundation records Saira Ansari’s research fellowship documenting the archive in collaboration with Asia Art Archive. A researcher-informed account of its photographic material discusses Agha’s public role in exhibitions, including a 1961 presentation of contemporary Pakistani painting in Milan.

Those records matter because the work of arranging an exhibition is easily lost behind the image of the solitary painter. Selection, correspondence, installation and public discussion help determine which art becomes visible. An archive can preserve that labour alongside evidence of individual artistic practice.

It also cautions against turning personality into explanation. Photographs of a public event cannot reveal every aspect of an artist’s private life, just as reports of privacy do not erase an active institutional role. The relevant question is what a particular document shows and what remains outside its frame.

Seen together, the painting and archive offer two scales of agency: decisions within an image and decisions that create a setting for other images to be encountered. Agha’s contribution becomes more substantial when both are considered. Modern painting is then not only a story of imported styles or celebrated firsts, but of specific works, public platforms and the records through which their histories can be reconstructed.
