Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Reading Kulli material culture without inventing a kingdom

Kulli describes a recognisable archaeological complex, not a kingdom whose borders and rulers are known. Settlement surveys reveal both shared forms and significant local differences.

Kulli is a useful archaeological term, but it can become misleading when treated as the name of a known kingdom. Recognisable objects and settlement patterns do not automatically supply a ruler, a capital or a political boundary. The term allows researchers to discuss relationships within material culture while leaving those larger social questions open.

Ute Franke-Vogt’s account of fieldwork in southeastern Balochistan explains that the complex takes its name from the site of Kulli in Kolwa, investigated by Aurel Stein. Her team’s surveys found a substantial range of settlements associated with this material. Some occupied elevated positions; others formed compact groups on open ground.

Shared forms, different settlements

The survey describes arrangements of houses, lanes and terraces, with stone used in substantial construction. It also records dams associated with many settlements. This evidence widens the discussion beyond painted pots and animal imagery. Kulli-related communities were making places as well as objects.

Variation within those places is important. A hilltop settlement and an open hamlet may participate in related practices without serving identical functions. Size, setting and internal arrangement should be considered before drawing conclusions about their relationship. A shared ceramic label is not a complete settlement hierarchy.

Nindowari provides one substantial archaeological point of reference. The official catalogue records excavation under Jean-Marie Casal. Its place in a researched landscape should not, however, turn it automatically into the capital of an imagined Kulli state. That would require a different kind of argument.

Where categories overlap

Franke-Vogt’s discussion of Bakkar Buthi is particularly instructive. The site’s assemblage is predominantly Harappan in character but also includes Kulli elements. Excavated building phases and pottery-production waste add further context. The combination does not fit a simple map divided into mutually exclusive cultural territories.

An assemblage can contain objects made through different traditions. Those objects might reflect exchange, local production, learning or several processes together. Choosing between these explanations requires attention to manufacture and context rather than treating the presence of a motif as a passport stamp.

What would demonstrate political organisation?

Questions about power remain legitimate. Differences in settlement scale, access to resources and investment in buildings may contribute to an interpretation. But each observation needs to be related to the others, and alternative explanations should be considered. Architectural prominence alone does not reveal a king’s name or the extent of his authority.

The same restraint should shape visual presentation. A map can show known sites and the distribution of material without drawing a hard border around a hypothetical kingdom. A caption can identify a Kulli-associated vessel without assigning its maker a language that has not been established.

Kulli’s importance does not depend on becoming a smaller version of a better-known civilisation. Its settlements and objects reveal a varied regional world with connections beyond it. Keeping the category flexible enough to accommodate that evidence makes the history more substantial, not less impressive.
