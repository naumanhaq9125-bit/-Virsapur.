What a pottery assemblage can say about a settlement

A pottery assemblage reveals patterns that an isolated decorated vessel cannot. Recording fabric, form, quantity and context helps distinguish evidence of making, use and deposition from attractive speculation.

A decorated pot can make a striking museum object, but an assemblage asks a different question: what can a group of pottery fragments reveal when their relationships are recorded? The answer depends on keeping ordinary material in view. Plain body sherds, damaged rims and undecorated bases can be as important as the pieces selected for display.

The Chartered Institute for Archaeologists’ pottery guidance separates attributes such as fabric, form, surface treatment and decoration. It also emphasises quantities, including fragment count and weight. These records allow patterns to be examined rather than leaving interpretation dependent on the most memorable examples.

Counting fragments is not counting pots

One vessel may break into many pieces, while another is represented by a single rim. A hundred sherds therefore do not necessarily mean a hundred pots. The method used to estimate vessel numbers needs to be stated, and different measures may answer different questions.

The guidance also recommends retaining the relationship between pottery and its contextual unit. That connection makes it possible to compare material from different deposits or structural areas. A spreadsheet of attractive vessel types without context would lose much of the archaeological value of the collection.

Official catalogue descriptions of surface finds illustrate the range that may be present: pitchers, cooking vessels, dishes, bowls, lids and different surface treatments. Such lists can identify a promising assemblage, but surface collection does not provide the same chronological control as material recovered from recorded layers.

A settlement sequence through ceramics

George Dales’s first excavation report on Balakot offers a comparative example. The published abstract distinguishes an earlier occupation sequence from a later Harappan period and notes relationships between its pottery and ceramic traditions in Balochistan and the southern Indus region. The point was not simply that painted pottery existed there, but that excavation could place comparisons within a sequence.

That distinction remains useful. Similar decoration may suggest a connection, yet it does not automatically show that a vessel was imported. Clay composition, manufacturing traces and the wider assemblage can support or challenge that interpretation. Appearance is evidence, but it is only one category of evidence.

The journey into the ground

A pottery group also reflects what happened after use. Material can be discarded, moved, broken again or incorporated into later construction. An assemblage from a fill may contain objects older than the event that created the fill. Its significance depends partly on understanding that history of deposition.

For a public archive, the best explanation makes these relationships visible without overwhelming the reader with codes. A few carefully chosen examples can show the difference between a fabric group, a vessel form and a decorative treatment.

The result is a richer picture of a settlement. Pottery becomes evidence for practices and connections rather than a collection of isolated motifs. The ordinary fragments matter because together, and in context, they allow questions that no single beautiful vessel can answer.
