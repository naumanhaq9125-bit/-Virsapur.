Dhamaal: representation, participation and consent

Dhamaal should not be reduced to a dramatic clip. Its documentation requires attention to participants’ meanings, the setting and consent for the uses an image may acquire.

Dhamaal is visually compelling, which makes it particularly vulnerable to being detached from its setting. A short clip can turn movement into a symbol of abandon, mystery or spectacle before a participant has said anything about what they are doing. The result may circulate widely while communicating very little about the practice.

At Sehwan, the association with Lal Shahbaz Qalandar gives dhamaal a specific religious context. It should not be approached as a generic dance available for a visitor to reproduce, direct or interrupt. Observation and participation are different relationships, and neither automatically includes permission to publish.

Meaning is not visible in a facial expression

Delphine Ortis’s research summary treats dhamaal in Sehwan through love, authority and the recognition of practice within a community. It points towards learned relationships and local understandings rather than an absence of order. The short summary does not provide a complete account of every form of dhamaal, and it is used here only for that limited argument.

Richard K. Wolf’s work on drumming and movement in Lahore likewise examines different accounts of participation. The comparison shows why a caption should not claim to know a person’s inner state from a photograph. The research sites are distinct; a Lahore observation is not direct evidence about a particular Sehwani participant.

Words such as trance can become especially misleading when used as a visual diagnosis. If a participant uses a term to describe an experience, attribute it. If no such account exists, describe only what the recording actually shows.

Consent follows the image

UNESCO’s ethical principles emphasise the role of communities and sustained informed consent in safeguarding living heritage. For a photographer, this means explaining the proposed use: a personal record, an educational archive and a promotional campaign are not the same request.

A person may agree to a photograph without anticipating that it will be cropped, captioned with a speculative claim or detached from the name of the occasion. Discussing those uses before publication is more meaningful than treating a quick gesture towards the camera as unlimited permission.

Where obtaining individual consent is impractical, that limitation should influence what is recorded. A distant scene may still identify people; a sensitive moment does not become ethically uncomplicated because it occurs in an accessible space.

Preserve the relationship, not only the movement

A stronger archive would name the place and date, identify the occasion where known and include the participant’s own explanation when offered. It would distinguish a recording of an event from a later demonstration made for documentation. Neither should be passed off as the other.

The sound also belongs to the record. Replacing it with unrelated music can sever the relationship between movement and the actual gathering. An edited clip should say that it is edited, and any interpretation should remain separate from the evidence.

Dhamaal deserves the same precision given to an inscription or an architectural plan. Its participants are people with responsibilities, preferences and differing understandings, not visual material awaiting an outside explanation. A smaller, consent-based record may preserve more of that reality than an impressive collection of decontextualised images.
