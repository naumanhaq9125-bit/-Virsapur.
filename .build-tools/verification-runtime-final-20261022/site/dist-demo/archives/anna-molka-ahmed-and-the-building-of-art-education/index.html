Anna Molka Ahmed and the building of art education

Anna Molka Ahmed’s contribution becomes clearer through the practical history of programmes, teaching and exhibitions at Punjab University.

Art education leaves a less obvious heritage than a monument. Its traces include a programme that continues after its founder, the habits students acquire, exhibition catalogues and the possibility of making a sustained living through art. Anna Molka Ahmed’s work at Punjab University should be understood through these structures as well as through her reputation as a painter.

The university’s account places the founding of its Fine Arts Department in 1940. Its College of Art and Design prospectus adds a more detailed institutional sequence: enrolments began in June 1942, a master’s programme in Fine Arts followed in 1955, and postgraduate Graphic Design was established in 1964. Foundation and first enrolment are different milestones; treating the two dates as a contradiction would obscure how an institution actually develops.

Building a place to learn

These stages matter because an art department requires more than a talented teacher. A continuing course needs admissions, teaching time, assessment and resources. A postgraduate programme creates a different level of study from an introductory class, while Graphic Design places visual communication beside the fine arts within the institution. The recorded sequence suggests expansion through sustained administrative work, not a single ceremonial founding moment.

Punjab University’s commemorative account also remembers Ahmed taking students outside the classroom to paint. Outdoor study changes the relationship between instruction and surroundings: light, scale and a changing scene cannot be arranged as readily as a studio exercise. This is a useful way to think about educational method without assuming that every former student painted alike or that one teacher single-handedly determined a national style.

The Asian Art Resource Room records her organisation of exhibitions and publication of catalogues at the university during the 1950s. Exhibitions add another stage to education. Work moves from the private exchange between teacher and student into a setting where it can be compared, discussed and remembered. Catalogues may preserve that encounter after the display has closed, although the contents of an individual catalogue still need to be consulted before naming its participants.

The institution as an archive

The university subsequently named an art gallery for Ahmed. Such commemoration can introduce new audiences to a founder, but it should also lead back to less spectacular records: prospectuses, course changes, student work and teaching correspondence. These can show what opportunities existed, who could take them up and how programmes changed over time.

An account centred only on awards risks making education appear to be the by-product of individual greatness. An institutional reading asks a harder question: what did Ahmed help make possible for people who came after her? The milestones recorded by Punjab University provide a starting answer in the growth of sustained study, specialist programmes and public presentation. Further work with its archives could turn that outline into a more detailed history of students, colleagues and the practical culture of the classroom.
