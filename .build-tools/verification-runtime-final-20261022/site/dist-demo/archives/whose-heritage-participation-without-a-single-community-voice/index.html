Whose heritage? Participation without a single community voice

Meaningful heritage participation makes room for differing interests, consent and decisions, rather than assuming one invited voice represents everyone.

A heritage project may describe itself as community-led after holding a public meeting or interviewing a respected local figure. Those actions can be useful, but they do not by themselves establish who shaped the project’s decisions. Participation is a relationship over time: people need opportunities to influence what is recorded, how it is presented and what happens to the results.

UNESCO’s intangible-heritage framework gives communities, groups and practitioners a central role in identifying and safeguarding their heritage. Its ethical principles also emphasise continuing informed consent and attention to the interests of those who maintain living practices. These principles offer a starting point for asking more precise questions than whether a consultation took place.

There may be more than one answer

Consider a hypothetical neighbourhood project about a historic market. Shopkeepers, residents, workers, property owners and visitors may value different aspects of the place. Some may welcome publicity; others may worry about disruption or unwanted exposure. The example does not assume inevitable conflict. It shows why a single spokesperson’s approval should not automatically be presented as agreement on behalf of everyone.

An editor can make the boundaries of participation visible. Who was invited? Who could not attend? Which languages were used? Did participants see the account before it was published? These questions are not a substitute for local judgement, but they help distinguish a collaborative process from one that extracts information and returns only a polished story.

Consent also concerns the form of publication. Agreeing to speak in an interview is not necessarily agreement to every future use of the recording. A transcript, translated quotation, photograph and promotional video can expose a person in different ways. A responsible project explains those proposed uses and provides a practicable way to raise concerns or correct an error.

Participation should affect the result

The strongest test is whether the process changes decisions. If participants identify a sensitive location, challenge a caption or ask for a recording to remain restricted, does the project have a way to respond? An invitation to speak carries little weight if every substantial editorial choice has already been closed.

For Virsapur, acknowledging different voices should improve precision rather than produce an artificial claim of consensus. An account can identify the people consulted, distinguish their perspectives and state the limits of the work. Community participation does not require every person to tell the same story. It requires a respectful process in which the story’s subjects have meaningful agency, and in which disagreement or a decision not to participate is not silently edited away.
