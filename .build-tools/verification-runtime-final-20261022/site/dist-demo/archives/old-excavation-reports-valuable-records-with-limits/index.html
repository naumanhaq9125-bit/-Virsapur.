Old excavation reports: valuable records with limits

Early excavation reports preserve observations that may no longer be recoverable. Reading them critically means separating documentation from inherited terminology and historical assumptions.

An old excavation report can be indispensable without being the last word. It may preserve a plan, a find location or a photograph of material that has since changed. At the same time, its classifications and explanations belong to the research habits of its period.

Preserve the observation, examine the claim

Ernest Mackay's report on Chanhudaro describes excavation levels, buildings and objects, but also makes broad claims about peoples and cultural change. A present-day reader should not reproduce its racial or evolutionary language as neutral description. The task is to distinguish what the excavator recorded from the historical explanation attached to it.

That distinction need not diminish the report's value. A documented association can remain important even when an interpretation is revised. A drawing can preserve useful detail while its caption uses a name no longer accepted. Critical reading allows those different parts of the record to be assessed separately.

Begin with the publication's identity. Is it a preliminary account, a final excavation report, a catalogue or a later synthesis? A preliminary statement may reflect incomplete analysis. Quoting it without its status can make a provisional suggestion appear more settled than the author intended.

Classifications also have histories

Kenoyer's discussion of Indus beads explains how influential early typologies shaped later research and why additional technological observations can refine them. The point is not simply that newer work is better. It is that a familiar classification may contain assumptions that have become difficult to see through repeated use.

When comparing publications, check whether the same term means the same thing. A category defined by appearance in one study may be defined through manufacturing technique in another. Counting both together without examining those definitions can create an artificial pattern.

Modern reporting guidance from the Chartered Institute for Archaeologists stresses explicit attributes and explained codes. That principle helps readers approach older documentation too: abbreviations, catalogue numbers and figure references are part of the evidence trail, not disposable clutter.

Revisions should be traceable

If a later researcher changes a date or identification, the archive should show the basis for that revision. Was new material excavated? Was an existing object re-examined? Did the chronological framework change? These are different reasons and should not disappear into the phrase “now believed”.

A digital archive can support this by keeping the early report linked to later work. It should neither delete the earlier interpretation nor repeat it without qualification. Readers can then see how knowledge developed rather than encountering a collection of apparently incompatible facts.

Old reports deserve particular care because some observations cannot be repeated. Excavation changes the ground, collections can become dispersed and landscapes can be altered. Preserving the report's information while questioning its assumptions is therefore not disloyal to earlier scholarship. It is how the record remains useful for questions its first authors may never have imagined.
