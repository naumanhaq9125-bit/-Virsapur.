Makran’s inland routes and coastal horizons

Makran’s settlement evidence connects inland valleys and coastal possibilities without proving a single continuous maritime route.

Makran is often imagined as a coastline with an inland margin. Archaeology suggests a more productive starting point: valleys, settlements and possible maritime connections that need to be investigated together. An inland site need not be isolated from exchange, and a site near the coast is not automatically a harbour.

Shahi Tump in the Kech landscape and Sutkagen-Dor farther west provide different points of reference. They should not be presented as two interchangeable stops on a single ancient shipping route. Their material evidence, dates and settings require separate attention before a connection can be proposed.

A research question shaped an expedition

Writing for Penn Museum in 1968, George Dales explained that his early Makran investigations were motivated by questions about contacts between South Asia and the Near East. Accounts of seafaring and evidence from Mesopotamia helped frame the search. This is valuable testimony about how archaeological research began, but an expedition’s objective is not the same thing as its final proof.

His article on Harappan outposts likewise treats the identification of Sutkagen-Dor as part of an argument about possible western contacts. The careful word is possible. A settlement’s participation in a wider material world does not by itself reveal the exact route by which every object or idea travelled.

The archive behind the route line

Penn Museum’s finding aid for the Dales papers lists field notes, maps, plans, artefact lists and pottery drawings from the Makran work. It includes separate records associated with Sutkagen-Dor and Sotka Koh. That range of material shows why a drawn route is only a synthesis: its strength depends on underlying observations that can be checked and compared.

The finding aid is not a substitute for inspecting those records. It establishes where research evidence survives and what kinds of documentation may be available. Detailed claims about a particular trench or object still require the relevant report or archive item.

Inland resources and coastal horizons

For an atlas reader, the useful distinction is between a demonstrated connection and a plausible corridor. Material similarities can suggest interaction; the location of valleys and coastlines can suggest ways movement might have occurred. Neither alone determines whether exchange was direct, passed through intermediaries or changed across periods.

The same caution applies to modern distances from the sea. Reconstructing an earlier shoreline requires geological evidence rather than an assumption that the coast has remained fixed or moved uniformly. A present map supplies the starting geography, not the completed ancient landscape.

Makran’s interest consequently extends beyond the search for ports. Inland settlements deserve attention as places of occupation and production, while coastal evidence opens questions about wider horizons. Keeping both in view allows the region to be understood as more than a transit strip between distant centres, without replacing genuine uncertainty with a confidently drawn but unproven ancient route.
