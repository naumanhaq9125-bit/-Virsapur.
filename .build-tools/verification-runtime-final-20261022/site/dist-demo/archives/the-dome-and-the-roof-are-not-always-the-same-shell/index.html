The dome and the roof are not always the same shell

An exterior dome need not reproduce the ceiling seen inside. Double-shell construction separates two architectural profiles, but a particular building needs documented evidence before receiving that label.

A dome seen across a skyline can appear taller or differently shaped than the ceiling experienced inside the chamber below it. That difference does not necessarily mean one view is misleading. In double-shell construction, the interior and exterior profiles can belong to distinct shells rather than opposite faces of one continuous thickness.

Two profiles, different experiences

The Government of Delhi's description of Humayun's tomb identifies its prominent double dome. It provides a documented comparative example for understanding the term. It does not establish that every visually similar dome in Pakistan has the same construction.

The architectural distinction is useful because the interior and exterior answer different spatial demands. A chamber is experienced from within, through its proportions and surfaces. A roof or dome also contributes to the building's distant silhouette. Separate shells can allow those profiles to differ rather than requiring one shape to serve both experiences identically.

That is an explanation of the principle, not a reconstruction of the engineering of every double dome. Materials, connections and construction histories need building-specific evidence. An elegant diagram is not a substitute for a documented section.

What a photograph cannot establish

A bulbous outline alone is not proof of double-shell construction. Nor does a difference between an interior photograph and an exterior photograph necessarily settle the matter: the views may be difficult to compare, and later alterations may complicate the relationship.

Measured drawings can clarify what a casual view cannot. Historic England's recording guidance distinguishes levels and methods of documentation for understanding buildings. The wider methodological lesson is relevant here: a description should state the evidence behind a structural identification.

A useful section needs to show which surfaces were measured and which were inferred. If a cavity was inaccessible, that limitation should be recorded. The most visually complete drawing is not automatically the most reliable unless its conventions and basis are clear.

Keep the local question open

Attock Tomb and Marhi Baba Dhyghana Singh may appear in a comparative heritage route concerned with domed architecture. Their inclusion should not be read as an unsupported assertion that both have a particular shell arrangement. Each requires its own technical documentation and chronology.

The distinction is also important in conservation. A crack or moisture stain on an interior surface does not, by itself, diagnose the condition of every part above it. Assessment belongs to qualified professionals with access to the relevant structure, not to an improvised interpretation from a visitor photograph.

For the interested reader, the reward is a more exact question: which surface am I looking at, and how does it relate to the building's other profile? That question turns a familiar silhouette into an investigation of architectural depth.

The dome is not always a single object with one shape. Learning to distinguish appearance from documented construction makes both the skyline and the room beneath it more intelligible.
