Living Traditions

Children’s games as a changing archive

Children’s games are transmitted by playing and negotiating, not just by preserving rules. Research in Chitral shows how documentation can support use while allowing adaptation.

A children’s game can survive without a printed rulebook because players teach it to one another. They demonstrate a movement, argue over a boundary, change a rule to fit the available space and invite someone new to join. That activity makes games a changing archive rather than a collection of instructions waiting to be copied exactly.

Rubina Nawab’s research at Aga Khan University offers a grounded example from Chitral. The project interviewed ten community elders, involved early-childhood education specialists in reviewing the material and selected seven games for classroom use. The choices considered age, inclusion and practicality rather than assuming that every remembered game would suit every group of children.

The study was carried out in a particular early-years class and used observation, reflection and discussion through successive cycles. Its findings concern that educational setting; they should not be inflated into a universal claim that any traditional game automatically produces the same developmental benefits. The value of the project includes the careful process of selection and adaptation.

A rule is only part of the record

To document a game, record what players call it, what materials they use and how play begins and ends. Then ask how they learned it and whether they know other versions. A disagreement about a rule may be valuable evidence of variation rather than a problem that the researcher must resolve into one official form.

Space matters too. A game played in a courtyard may be adjusted for a school ground or a narrow lane. The number and age of players can change what is practical. Describing those conditions helps a later reader understand the version recorded, instead of assuming that a short list of actions will reproduce the whole experience.

The 2025 publication Echoes of Play, by Nawab and Fozia Parveen, identifies traditional outdoor games of Chitral’s mountain communities as its subject. Its existence shows documentation being made into an educational resource. A book can support transmission, but it cannot replace the social work children do when they actually play.

Let children contribute to the archive

Adults’ memories are important, yet they should not be the only authority. Children may know variants, new names or solutions that make a game workable now. Recording those contributions avoids presenting change as evidence that they have failed to preserve the past correctly.

Ethical documentation is especially important here. Permission for research or a school activity is not automatically permission to publish identifiable photographs or recordings of children. A description of rules can often be shared without exposing names, faces or precise everyday locations. Participation should also allow a child to decline being recorded.

The aim is a resource that makes play possible, not a museum of compulsory authenticity. A well-documented game can carry a history while remaining open to thoughtful adaptation. Its heritage continues when children find it worth playing together—and when an archive respects their role as present-day makers of that experience, rather than treating them only as recipients of adult memory.
