Bhong Mosque: a modern work of many crafts

Bhong Mosque is a twentieth-century work of collective craftsmanship. Its architectural importance lies in the inventive meeting of materials, references and skilled labour.

Bhong Mosque can look older than it is to a viewer who equates dense ornament with a distant past. It is, instead, a major twentieth-century architectural project. Work began in 1932 under Rais Ghazi Mohammad, and the Aga Khan Award for Architecture records completion in 1982. Its recognition in the 1986 award cycle makes it part of the history of modern architecture as well as the history of mosque building.

The award record credits local master masons and craftsmen rather than a single professional architect. It describes specialists drawn from several places, including Rajasthan and Karachi. This is an important starting point. The building emerged from coordinated skills developed over decades, not simply from a finished design translated mechanically into stone and plaster.

Many materials, one demanding composition

The project brought together carving, inlay, tilework, mirror work, painting and calligraphy. The wider compound also employed industrially produced materials alongside traditional ones. The award account distinguishes these from the traditional materials of the mosque interior. That distinction helps explain why the building should not be reduced to either an untouched craft tradition or a modern imitation of an older style.

A study of its ornamentation discusses the involvement of skilled workers and their apprentices. Such a working relationship makes craft transmission part of the architectural story. A finished pattern is the visible outcome; the ability to set it out, cut it, fit it and repair it is another form of knowledge. The latter is less easily photographed but equally important.

The mosque’s intensity can initially make individual decisions hard to see. One useful approach is to follow a single material across a surface. Where does tile give way to paint? Where does reflective work concentrate light? How does a calligraphic band establish a boundary? These questions reveal organisation within abundance, rather than treating ornament as an undifferentiated covering.

Modern does not have to mean unadorned

Bhong challenges a narrow definition of modern architecture as necessarily plain, industrial or detached from inherited forms. Its eclectic references were part of the award discussion, not an embarrassment concealed behind the prize. The building asks whether invention can occur through combination and elaboration as well as through reduction.

That does not require every viewer to find the composition harmonious. A considered response can acknowledge tension, density and differences of taste. Architectural significance is not the same as universal agreement about beauty. The mosque provides a particularly clear opportunity to discuss that distinction without dismissing the makers or the community for whom it was built.

For an archive, the best presentation would pair an overall view with a small number of carefully explained details. Each detail should identify its material and position rather than simply celebrate intricacy. Bhong’s achievement becomes more legible when the work of making is visible: a sustained collaboration in which contemporary patronage, inherited skills and new materials all had a part.
