Hodar: learning to read a rock-art field

At Hodar, individual boulders, overlapping marks and scholarly catalogue numbers reveal a rock-art landscape made over time.

Hodar is not one carving with one date. It is a field of marked rock surfaces near the Indus, where images and inscriptions accumulated through different acts of making. The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes clusters across hundreds of boulders around the mouth of the Hodar stream and nearby slopes. A single map pin is useful for finding the area, but it cannot represent the complexity of the evidence spread across it.

The catalogue records animal and human figures, religious imagery and inscriptions, including Sogdian writing. These categories are not interchangeable. A recognisable animal may tell us something different from a personal name, while an architectural image can invite comparison without yielding an exact date. The site’s broad chronology must not become a claim that every mark is equally ancient.

A catalogue makes uncertainty visible

The detailed scholarly volume Die Felsbildstation Hodar, published in 1999, records individual stones and engravings with identifiers. One entry, 65:10, describes a stupa image whose upper part may have been constrained by an inscription above it. The authors suggest a possible sequence: the image might have followed the writing. They do not present that possibility as a certainty.

Nearby inscription entries distinguish clear readings from uncertain ones. One name is difficult to establish even when examined at the rock; another is differentiated from related examples by the hand that wrote it. Such observations make the catalogue more valuable than a collection of confident translations. They show the reader what the scholar could see, what could be compared and where doubt remains.

This is a practical lesson in archaeological literacy. An identifier lets another researcher return to the same mark. A measurement and orientation make a description testable. A question mark preserves a limitation that may otherwise disappear when the result is shortened for a visitor panel.

Surface colour is not a clock

The official overview discusses differences in repatination, the darkening of previously worked surfaces. These can contribute to a relative reading of marks, but colour alone should not be converted into a precise age. Exposure, surface condition and the relationship between engravings must also be considered. A dark animal figure is not automatically evidence for a named prehistoric community.

For visitors, the first task is therefore to keep the boulder in view as well as the image. Where is the mark on the surface? Is there writing nearby? Does another line cross it? A photograph that includes context can preserve these relationships better than a tightly cropped motif.

Hodar’s significance lies in this plurality. It is a place where movement, devotion and other forms of marking left material traces, without all those traces belonging to one moment or one explanation. Learning to read the field means resisting the urge to make every image instantly legible. Its uncertainties are part of the record, not gaps to fill with an attractive story.
