Sehwan as both archaeological and sacred city

Sehwan’s archaeological mound and Lal Shahbaz Qalandar’s shrine belong to a connected urban history, but they preserve different kinds of evidence.

Sehwan is both an archaeological place and a living sacred city. The distinction matters because its mound and shrine answer different historical questions. Purana Qila preserves traces of occupation and rebuilding; Lal Shahbaz Qalandar’s shrine is sustained through remembrance, worship and institutions. Neither should be used to explain the other through a single founding legend.

The federal inventory describes the fort site as a substantial mound containing fragments of pottery, brick and charcoal, with structures from different periods. Such a mound is not simply the remains of one fort built at one moment. Accumulated occupation can include houses, workshops, dumped material, defensive works and later disturbance. Its shape is the outcome of many processes rather than a straightforward ground plan.

A changing position within the town

Monique Kervran’s account of excavations, reproduced by the Endowment Fund Trust, distinguishes several major phases at Purana Qila. She describes a change in which a fortified garrison occupied the mound while civilian settlement developed to its south around the shrine. This offers a spatial relationship between the two histories without making them identical. A shift in where people lived can be as important as the construction of a prominent wall.

Kervran’s account combines excavated sequences with written evidence and differentiates successive fortifications. The resulting picture is richer than attributing the entire mound to Alexander or to one later dynasty. A name used in local memory may be historically significant, but archaeological attribution requires material and chronological evidence. The city’s depth does not depend on winning a competition for the oldest continuously inhabited settlement.

The shrine has its own institutional history

The inventory identifies Lal Shahbaz Qalandar’s tomb and records an early shrine foundation. That is not a date for every part of the present complex. Religious institutions can undergo rebuilding while retaining their dedication and central place in a community. Continued significance and unchanged fabric are different forms of continuity.

Researcher Delphine Ortis’s published presentation abstract identifies the shrine’s nationalisation in 1962 as an important change in its administration. This introduces a modern institutional history that a medieval foundation date alone cannot capture. Questions about management, ritual and authority remain part of the shrine’s life; they should not be concealed by presenting it as a survival outside history.

Sehwan’s two forms of heritage also call for different kinds of respectful attention. Archaeological deposits should remain undisturbed, while a place of worship requires sensitivity to people using it. Neither is merely a setting for a visitor’s photograph. A fuller account would place excavation reports alongside locally grounded histories and accounts from participants, with consent and clear attribution.

The most useful connection between mound and shrine is therefore a changing city. Buildings, settlement and sacred institutions shaped one another over time, but their records remain distinct. Keeping those distinctions visible allows Sehwan to be understood as more than either an ancient ruin or a timeless pilgrimage image.
