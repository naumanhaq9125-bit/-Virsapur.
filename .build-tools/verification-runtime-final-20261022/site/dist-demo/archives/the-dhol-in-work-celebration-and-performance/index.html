Living Traditions

The dhol in work, celebration and performance

The dhol’s force comes from musical choices and social occasions as well as volume. A player’s account shows tradition as something learned and continually reworked.

A dhol can announce its presence before the player comes into view. Yet describing it only as loud misses the musical decisions that make its sound meaningful: placement of strokes, changes in emphasis and the relationship between a drummer and the people moving or listening nearby. The drum belongs to an occasion, not just to a volume level.

In an essay for the Smithsonian, musician Sunny Jain describes the dhol as a double-headed wooden drum associated with farming, dance and special events in Punjabi life. He also discusses his interest in Pakistani Sufi dhol players after joining Junoon. This is a musician’s account of learning and influence, with named artists rather than an anonymous picture of regional tradition.

Jain’s own practice brings those influences into conversation with jazz and other music. He describes using electronic effects as well as acoustic sound. His account is useful not because it defines every dhol tradition, but because it shows a player making deliberate choices about what to inherit and how to work with it.

Listen for relationship, not just repetition

A repeated pattern can establish a framework in which a small change becomes significant. Notice when a player intensifies a phrase, leaves space or signals a return. The response of dancers or fellow musicians can help explain the effect, but it should not be assumed from an isolated audio clip.

Different settings also change what is audible. A drum in a procession competes with movement and surrounding sound. A microphone on a stage can present a different balance. A studio recording may isolate details that a nearby listener at an outdoor gathering would hear as part of a larger event.

An archival Smithsonian record from the 1976 Festival of American Folklife identifies a Pakistani programme that includes a Punjab folk tune with shehnai and dhol. That kind of entry offers a useful anchor: an occasion, a performance grouping and a record that can be investigated. It does not describe every use of the drum in Punjab.

Keep the work of the drummer visible

A cultural caption should credit the player and identify the setting whenever those facts are known. If a piece is associated with work, celebration or devotion, explain the basis of that association. Avoid turning a broad historical connection into an unsupported claim that the specific recording accompanied agricultural labour or a ritual.

The same care applies to change. A contemporary arrangement is not automatically a loss of tradition, just as an old-looking performance is not automatically faithful to a single original form. The musician’s account of learning, repertoire and intention can reveal a more complex relationship.

The dhol’s cultural power is not diminished by such precision. Its sound can bring people together while remaining the result of individual skill and shared expectations. Listening to those decisions allows a heritage archive to present a working musical practice—energetic, adaptable and situated—rather than a generic beat attached to every image of Punjabi celebration.
