Layers, cuts and fills: a first guide to stratigraphy

Stratigraphy reconstructs the order of archaeological events, including material added and ground removed. A simple hypothetical pit shows why depth alone cannot date a find.

Imagine a courtyard floor, a pit dug through it and soil later placed inside the pit. The floor came first, the digging happened next and the filling followed. All three belong to the archaeological sequence, but only two involve material added to the ground. The pit cut records removal. This simple example explains why stratigraphy is more than a stack of coloured layers.

Archaeologists study the relationships between deposits, structures and cuts to reconstruct an order of events. A context is an individually recorded unit within that work. The terminology may sound technical, but its purpose is practical: to preserve observations clearly enough that another researcher can examine the argument.

A cut and its fill are not the same event

Historic England’s recording manual distinguishes a cut from the deposits that fill it. The shape of a pit belongs to the event that created the hollow; material inside may have accumulated through one or several later episodes. Treating both as a single undifferentiated feature would conceal that sequence.

Return to the hypothetical courtyard. An old pottery fragment could be included in the soil used to fill the later pit. Its age would not make the pit older than the floor it cuts. The object’s manufacture and its final deposition are different moments.

This is why depth alone is unreliable. The bottom of a later pit can lie below an earlier floor. Without recording the cut, a deep find may appear older simply because it lies lower. The physical relationship corrects that misleading impression.

Turning observations into an ordered argument

A Harris matrix represents earlier and later relationships in a diagram. Historic England’s guidance emphasises direct stratigraphic relationships and warns against adding connections that the evidence does not establish. The diagram is not a picture of the trench; it is an account of sequence.

Two deposits at similar heights need not be contemporary. Conversely, parts of one deposit may be exposed in separate areas. Connecting them requires evidence and a stated basis, not just a convenient line on a chart. Where a relationship cannot be established, leaving the question open is more accurate than forcing an uninterrupted sequence.

Why the paperwork matters

The Museum of London’s field manual explains that excavation removes the contexts being investigated. Written descriptions, drawings, photographs and records of finds and samples therefore become essential evidence after the deposits are gone. Recording is not an administrative task added after discovery; it is part of the research itself.

The hypothetical courtyard could support several later questions about rebuilding, use or abandonment. Those questions would be impossible to revisit if the pit and floor had simply been cleared without recording their relationship.

Stratigraphy gives archaeological stories their sequence. It does not supply every date or explain every motive, but it establishes constraints that interpretations must respect. Learning to distinguish a layer, a cut and a fill is a first step toward understanding why careful excavation produces knowledge rather than merely exposing old things.
