Chottori Tombs and carved funerary architecture

Chottori’s tombs combine carved sandstone, brick and different forms of enclosure. The cemetery should be read through its own Talpur associations rather than borrowed descriptions of Makli.

Chottori, also written Chittori or Chitorri, is a cemetery near Mirpur Khas whose architectural character comes from a group of tombs rather than one dominant monument. Carved sandstone, brick construction, enclosed chambers and open canopies create different ways of marking a burial. The differences are essential to understanding the place; they should not be smoothed into a generic description of Sindh’s funerary architecture.

The national catalogue associates the cemetery with Talpur figures who held positions during Kalhora rule. A field survey by the Endowment Fund Trust in July 2017 distinguishes carved stone canopies, stone tombs and brick tombs. These are complementary kinds of information: one identifies historical associations, while the other describes visible building types.

A canopy is not a closed room

An open canopy frames a grave while allowing views through it. An enclosed tomb chamber creates a different relationship between inside and outside. This elementary distinction is more useful than treating every domed structure as the same architectural form. The supports, openings and roof must be considered together.

An architectural account published by the Talpur heritage project draws attention to carved yellow sandstone on pillars, entrances, ceilings and tombstones. It describes domed compositions and relationships between supporting elements. These observations invite a close reading of how carving works across a structure: whether it emphasises an edge, fills a field or changes at the point where one element meets another.

The cemetery’s visual richness does not mean every grave can be identified securely. An inscription may supply a name or date, while an uninscribed structure may depend on another form of attribution. Those kinds of evidence should be distinguished. A family association for the cemetery as a whole cannot identify every individual burial within it.

Comparison without substitution

Makli is a useful point of comparison for readers familiar with Sindh’s more celebrated funerary sites, but its history cannot simply be transferred to Chottori. Similar materials or architectural devices do not establish identical dates, patrons or workshop connections. Comparison should begin with a specific feature and then ask what the evidence supports.

Published inventories also vary in the number of structures they count. A survey may count standing monuments differently from an earlier list that includes ruined or subsidiary elements. Until the inventories are reconciled on a plan, the difference should remain visible rather than becoming an unexplained claim that the cemetery contains one exact total.

Chottori’s significance is strongest when the individual monuments remain part of a larger burial landscape. Their spacing, orientation and relation to neighbouring graves matter alongside the carved details. A respectful photographic record would include both the ensemble and selected close views, while avoiding contact with vulnerable surfaces. The result can reveal a distinctive regional history of commemoration without turning the cemetery into a catalogue of interchangeable ornamental objects.
