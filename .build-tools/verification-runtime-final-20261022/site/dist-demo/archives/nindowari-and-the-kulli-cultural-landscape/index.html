Nindowari and the Kulli cultural landscape

Nindowari connects a substantial settlement in the Ornach valley with the distinctive animal imagery of the Kulli cultural world.

Nindowari offers an important correction to a map of ancient Pakistan dominated by Harappa and Moenjodaro. The Department of Archaeology and Museums locates the settlement in the agricultural Ornach valley, south of Khuzdar, and describes a group of four mounds. This was a place with its own substantial archaeological footprint, not simply a point on a route between better-known cities. Its significance becomes clearer when settlement evidence is considered alongside the distinctive visual language associated with the Kulli culture.

The official catalogue records four excavation seasons directed by Jean-Marie Casal between 1962 and 1966. That history matters: a mound seen today is not the same thing as a complete picture of its former settlement. Excavation exposes selected areas and generates records, while much of the evidence may remain below ground. The visible outline alone cannot establish how every part of Nindowari functioned or how its occupation changed.

An animal-shaped vessel and a larger world

A painted ceramic drinking vessel in the Musée Guimet provides a particularly vivid introduction to Kulli workmanship. Modelled as a humped bull, it combines the volume of an animal with the practical idea of a container. The museum attributes this rare piece and a matching example to Nindowari and dates them to approximately 2300–2000 BCE. Its catalogue places Kulli alongside, rather than after, the urban Indus civilisation.

The museum emphasises the modelling of the animal’s body and suggests a possible ritual setting. Possible is the important word. An arresting shape does not by itself disclose a ceremony, the person who used it, or a belief shared by an entire population. Nor should the object’s museum attribution be silently upgraded into a detailed excavation history: the public catalogue records acquisition in 2014, but does not supply a complete chain from an excavated context to the collection.

Read connection without erasing difference

Looking at the vessel beside the settlement invites a productive question: how can a community participate in a wider world while retaining a recognisable way of making things? Comparison need not reduce Kulli to a provincial imitation of the Indus cities. An animal model, a painted surface and a settlement plan are different kinds of evidence; each may illuminate a different relationship.

For a heritage atlas, the strongest presentation is therefore a connection rather than a claim of equivalence. Nindowari’s place record can lead to the history of excavation, then to a carefully identified museum object, while keeping the evidence for each separate. The museum photograph cannot stand in for the appearance of the archaeological site, and the site pin cannot establish the provenance of every object labelled Kulli.

Nindowari rewards this slower approach. Its importance lies not in competing for an oldest or largest superlative, but in making the cultural variety of the third millennium BCE visible. A regional settlement and a finely modelled vessel together open questions about making, exchange and identity that a single civilisational label would conceal.
