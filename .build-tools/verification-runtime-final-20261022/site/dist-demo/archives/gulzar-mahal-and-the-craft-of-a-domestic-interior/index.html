Gulzar Mahal and the craft of a domestic interior

Chiniot’s Umar Hayat Mahal, also called Gulzar Mahal, brings the work of woodcarvers, painters and builders into an unusually expressive domestic setting.

The Gulzar Mahal discussed here is the building in Chiniot also known as Umar Hayat or Omar Hayat Mahal. It should not be confused with the palace of the same name in Bahawalpur. That distinction matters because the Chiniot building belongs to a different story: a richly worked domestic interior, a merchant family and a concentration of specialist craft.

The archaeological catalogue connects the house with Sheikh Umar Hayat and his son Gulzar. Its account places completion in 1935 after a prolonged building campaign. More unusually, it names people associated with the work, including the woodcarvers Rahim Bakhsh and Elahi Bakhsh. It also distinguishes brickwork, stucco and fresco painting as contributions of different specialists. This is a valuable corrective to the habit of assigning every artistic decision to the owner alone.

An interior made by several hands

Wood carving can be appreciated as pattern, but it is also a way of shaping thresholds and surfaces. A carved panel changes under oblique light; a projecting element casts a shadow that a flat painted motif cannot. Where several materials meet, the interest lies in the junction: how one border ends, how another begins, and whether their rhythms reinforce or interrupt one another.

These are questions to ask of photographs as well as a visit. A tightly cropped image may show extraordinary workmanship while concealing whether the object is a door, a balcony element or part of a ceiling. Including a wider view helps a reader understand the scale of the labour. It also makes the interior legible as a home with connected spaces rather than an unlimited accumulation of decorative details.

The term palace can encourage expectations of royal occupation. In this case, the domestic and mercantile context is essential. The account of a wealthy patron commissioning skilled work does not require a royal court to make the building important. It opens another route into architectural history: the ambitions and resources of people whose homes became exceptional local landmarks.

Survival is part of the design story

The official catalogue records losses to upper portions and later intervention, including a library use. The Urban Unit’s regional heritage assessment also draws attention to conservation concerns. Such records should be dated when cited; they are not substitutes for a current condition survey. They do, however, explain why an interior cannot be treated as an untouched snapshot of its completion year.

Any account should separate documented building history from dramatic family legends. Stories of death or haunting may have become attached to the house, but repetition does not verify them. The material record already offers a substantial subject without those embellishments.

Umar Hayat Mahal is most rewarding when attention shifts from the adjective ornate to the question of making. Who carved, who painted, how were the parts joined, and what has survived? That approach restores visibility to skilled labour and gives Chiniot’s craft heritage a human scale.
