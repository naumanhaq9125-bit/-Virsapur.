Kharoshthi and Brahmi in the north-west

Kharoshthi and Brahmi are writing systems, not interchangeable names for languages. Manuscripts and inscriptions help separate script, language and find context.

A script is a system for writing; a language is what is being written. The distinction sounds straightforward, yet heritage labels can blur it when unfamiliar names appear together. Kharoshthi, Brahmi and Gandhari should not be treated as three equivalent categories.

Begin with the catalogue's separate fields

The University of Washington describes the British Library fragments as texts in the Gandhari language written in Kharoshthi script on birch bark. Each part of that description answers a different question: language, writing system and support. Keeping them separate makes the evidence easier to compare with an inscription carved on stone.

The university also identifies uncertainty about the manuscripts' exact findspot. That caution matters. A probable regional association is not the same as a documented excavation location, and the physical survival of a manuscript does not repair a gap in its archaeological provenance.

Stefan Baums's outline of Gandhari grammar explains the writing system within a linguistic study. It offers a reminder that identifying letter forms and understanding grammar are related but distinct scholarly tasks. Recognising a script in a photograph does not mean that the text has been read.

The north-west was not a single-script landscape

Research on an inscribed sherd from Barikot discusses an Aramaic-script object alongside other evidence of writing at the site, including Greek and Brahmi inscriptions. The point is not to assemble a decorative list of scripts. It is to recognise that a settlement's writing practices can vary with period, object and purpose.

A site label that says only “ancient inscription” loses those distinctions. A better description asks which script has been identified, which language is proposed, what dates the object and whether the reading is complete. Where a scholar is uncertain, the catalogue should retain that uncertainty.

The coexistence of writing systems also does not prove that every inhabitant was multilingual or literate. A surviving inscription records a particular act of writing. Establishing who commissioned, produced or read it requires evidence beyond the existence of the marks themselves.

For readers comparing the edicts at Mansehra with later manuscript traditions, this framework prevents an attractive but misleading straight line. Similar regional settings do not erase differences in chronology, medium or audience.

The useful habit is to slow down at the label. Ask what each name denotes before turning it into a historical claim. Script, language and place can illuminate one another, but they should first be allowed to remain distinct.
