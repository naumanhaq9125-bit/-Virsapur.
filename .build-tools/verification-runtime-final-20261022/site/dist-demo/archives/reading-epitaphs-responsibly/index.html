Reading epitaphs responsibly

An epitaph is a text on a material object, not a ready-made biography. Transcription, translation and historical interpretation should remain distinct and checkable.

A few legible words on a tomb can feel like direct contact with a person from the past. They can also tempt a reader into filling gaps too quickly. An epitaph may include names, titles, prayer, poetry and dates, but those elements need to be separated before they can support a historical account.

Reading responsibly begins with the object. Where is the inscription? Is it part of a grave marker, a wall panel or a later plaque? Does it appear to belong to the surrounding fabric? A text moved or replaced during repair may have a different history from the monument beside it.

Record before translating

A transcription represents the writing that can be read. A translation attempts to convey its meaning in another language. Neither is the same as an explanation of the person’s life. Keeping the three stages separate makes it possible for another reader to identify where an interpretation entered the record.

EpiDoc’s guidance distinguishes a diplomatic transcription, with minimal intervention, from editorial additions and other treatments of a text. Its conventions also allow lost or illegible material to remain visibly missing. The broader lesson is useful beyond any particular digital format: uncertainty should survive into the published record.

If part of a name is damaged, supplying a familiar ruler’s name without marking the restoration creates false certainty. If a title has several possible meanings, a translation should not silently choose the one that makes the most dramatic biography.

Dates describe particular events

A date on a tomb may concern death, construction, repair or the installation of a panel. Identifying the event is as important as converting the numerals. Calendar conversions should also be checked, especially when a published equivalent offers more precision than the inscription supports.

A prayer or scriptural passage is not automatically a personal statement by the deceased. Nor does a poem establish authorship merely because it appears on a monument associated with a poet. The text’s function has to be considered alongside its wording.

The federal records for Tahir Khan Nahar’s tomb at Sitpur and Sheikh Sadan Shaheed’s tomb provide architectural descriptions, but they do not supply complete edited epitaphs. Their inclusion in the atlas is therefore not a licence to invent translated inscriptions for either place.

Make the reading recoverable

A useful record preserves a clear photograph, the location of the text, line divisions, the proposed reading and the translator’s name. Earlier published readings can be cited separately. If two readings differ, explain which letters or assumptions produce the disagreement.

A photograph taken in poor light may conceal a stroke that is visible in another image. That is a reason to improve non-invasive documentation with permission, not to scratch, chalk or wet the surface to make it easier to read.

The reward is more than an accurate caption. A carefully edited epitaph can connect a name with patronage, family relationships or a particular commemorative moment. Where it cannot, the gap remains part of the evidence. Preserving that distinction allows the inscription to speak as a historical source rather than as a prompt for a story we have already decided to tell.
