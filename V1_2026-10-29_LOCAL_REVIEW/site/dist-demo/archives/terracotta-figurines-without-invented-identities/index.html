Terracotta figurines without invented identities

Terracotta figurines invite stories, but observation, archaeological context and changing interpretations should come before invented identities.

A small clay figure seems to ask for a name. Goddess, ruler, child, animal companion: familiar identities can make an object immediately memorable. They can also conceal how little is known about its original use. The first task is not to suppress imagination, but to prevent a plausible story from replacing the surviving evidence.

Two early photographs from Harappa make the problem unusually visible. The archival images identified as ASI 416/86 and 423/86 show a terracotta human head and a humped bull. In one photograph the head was placed upside down; the other corrects its orientation. Before any larger interpretation, there was a basic problem of how to look at the fragment.

Begin with what can be checked

A description can record the surviving headgear, applied details, breaks and the relationship between separate parts. These observations are not trivial preliminaries to a more exciting religious explanation. They are the evidence against which an explanation must be tested. A missing feature should remain missing in the account rather than being supplied from an imagined complete figure.

The Harappa archive also preserves Daya Ram Sahni's report of figurines associated with smaller vessels inside a large jar. Such contextual information poses different questions from an isolated photograph. It can direct attention towards deposition and association without automatically explaining the ceremony, household activity or later disturbance that produced the arrangement.

The archive's discussion of Sharri R. Clark's research notes her challenge to an automatic “Mother Goddess” interpretation. The important change is methodological: representation, variation and context must be investigated rather than reduced to a single reproductive or sacred identity. This does not prove that no figurine had a religious role. It means that a religious role requires an argument.

Several questions can remain open together

For a hypothetical exhibition, compare three captions. “Mother Goddess” states an identity as settled. “Female figurine” describes an attribution based on representation but still needs attention to the surviving features. “Terracotta fragment with applied ornaments” may be more cautious where the evidence is incomplete. The most appropriate wording depends on the individual record; caution should sharpen description, not erase useful information.

Function is another question. A figure might have been handled, displayed or deposited in circumstances that are no longer recoverable. Size alone cannot decide whether it was a toy, a ritual object or something else. Nor can the presence of elaborate ornament establish a specific rank in an otherwise undocumented social hierarchy.

The pleasure of looking survives these limits. Clay carries decisions about proportion, attachment and expression that can be discussed without inventing a personal biography. An accurate account lets a visitor notice those decisions and follow the history of their interpretation. The object remains compelling because it has resisted complete explanation, not because a confident modern label has finally made it say more than the record supports.
