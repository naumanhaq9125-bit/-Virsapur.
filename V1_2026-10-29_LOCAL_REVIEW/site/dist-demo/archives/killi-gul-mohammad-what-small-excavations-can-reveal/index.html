Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Killi Gul Mohammad: what small excavations can reveal

Killi Gul Mohammad demonstrates both the value and the limits of a small excavation sample. Its early settlement sequence is important, but absence from a narrow trench cannot establish absence from an entire community.

Killi Gul Mohammad is important not because every aspect of its early community has been recovered, but because a limited excavation revealed a sequence that helped frame larger questions. Near Quetta, the mound became part of twentieth-century research into settlement before the mature Indus cities. Its evidence also offers a clear lesson in archaeological sampling.

The federal catalogue places the site in the northern part of the Quetta valley, near the Quetta–Chaman route, and records excavation by Walter Fairservis in 1950–51. The Penn Museum Archives' account situates this work within his broader survey of the Quetta–Pishin region. A single trench belonged to a larger effort to understand how settlements related across the valley.

A deep view is not a wide view

In a discussion of early shell use, archaeologist Jonathan Mark Kenoyer describes Fairservis's excavation at Killi Gul Mohammad as a deep sondage: a restricted intervention designed to investigate the sequence below the surface. It revealed occupation extending from a phase without pottery into later periods with ceramics.

Such a trench can be highly informative about succession. It may show one deposit above another and allow changes in material to be studied through depth. Its limitation is spatial: the sampled area cannot reveal every activity that took place across the settlement.

Imagine investigating a modern neighbourhood through one narrow slice of ground. The sequence might be clear, but whether that slice crossed a kitchen, workshop, yard or passage would strongly affect the objects recovered. The same principle applies to an ancient mound, even when its surviving deposits are much more complex.

An absence that needs interpretation

Kenoyer notes that shell was not reported from the lower levels and was also absent from the published account of succeeding deposits. He cautions that the sample may not adequately represent the region's material culture. The observation is not a licence to invent missing objects; it is a reason to avoid an overly broad conclusion from a restricted sample.

“No shell was reported in this excavation” and “this community had no contact with shell materials” are different statements. The first describes an evidence base. The second proposes a historical explanation that would need wider support. Preserving the difference is one of archaeology's most useful habits.

Pottery and changing practice

The federal catalogue emphasises changes in pottery manufacture across the site's sequence. Those changes can be investigated through fabrics, forming methods, firing and decoration. They should not be turned into a simplistic ladder from primitive to advanced people. Different techniques answer different needs and may coexist.

Killi Gul Mohammad therefore contributes both substantive evidence and a lesson in how to use it. Its early sequence matters, while the limits of excavation remain part of every responsible account. For the reader, the most valuable question is not merely how old the mound is, but what was actually sampled and how that sample supports the story being told.
