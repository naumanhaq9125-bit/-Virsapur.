Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Nausharo between village and urban worlds

Nausharo links regional Balochistan traditions with the Indus urban world through a sequence of buildings and objects. Its pottery and figurines reveal both connections and differences across time.

Nausharo is valuable because it complicates a clean dividing line between regional village traditions and an Indus urban world. The site preserves a sequence through which changes in architecture and material culture can be examined. Its significance is not that every phase looks the same, but that related and changing practices can be studied in their archaeological order.

The Department of Archaeology locates Nausharo in the Bolan plain of Kachhi and records four principal cultural periods. Its catalogue includes dwelling structures, painted pottery, human and animal figurines and Harappan seals among the evidence. This range allows questions about everyday activity and representation to sit beside those about wider cultural connections.

Sequence before labels

Jarrige, Didier and Quivron compare Nausharo's Harappan periods II, III and IV with sequences at other excavated sites. Such comparison is not simply a matter of matching famous names. It depends on relating material characteristics to stratigraphy and, where available, independent dating evidence.

A period label is a tool for organising evidence, not an event that every household experienced on the same day. Changes in vessels, buildings and other objects need not all occur together. A useful account therefore asks which practices continued, which changed and how securely those changes can be placed in time.

The authors also identify a minority component of material associated with the Oxus world in Nausharo's later Harappan period. That observation broadens the geographical discussion without requiring the whole settlement to be assigned a new identity. An assemblage can contain objects with different connections.

Figurines and local choices

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer's comparison of early art draws attention to greater variety among human figurines from Mehrgarh and Nausharo than in the Harappa material he discusses. He identifies applied clay decoration as an important technique at the Balochistan sites, contrasting it with other approaches visible at Harappa.

This comparison shows why resemblance and difference deserve equal attention. Shared participation in a wider tradition does not require identical visual habits. How a figure was formed or ornamented can carry evidence about learned practices and choices in making, even where the object's exact social or religious use remains uncertain.

Kenoyer presents further comparative study as a research opportunity. That phrasing should be retained rather than converted into a settled explanation of social identity. A human figurine is not automatically a goddess, a toy or a portrait; interpretation depends on context and a defensible argument.

A connected place with its own evidence

Nausharo helps replace a simple centre-and-periphery picture with a more demanding one. Regional communities could participate in broad networks while retaining particular techniques and histories. The task is to demonstrate those relationships through objects and deposits, not assume them from a map.

For readers, the best route into the site is to follow one class of evidence through its recorded sequence, then compare it with another. Pottery, figurines and architecture may tell overlapping but different stories. Together they make Nausharo an important place for understanding how cultural integration can occur alongside local continuity and change.
