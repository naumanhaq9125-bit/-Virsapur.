Bazira-Barikot and the evidence of a long-lived town

Barikot’s excavated streets, religious buildings and acropolis show a town repeatedly reorganised rather than one ancient city preserved at a single moment.

Barikot is compelling because excavation reveals change within a town, not simply the outline of a town. Known in historical discussion as Bazira or Vajirasthana, the site combines an elevated acropolis with settlement on the adjoining plain in the Swat Valley. Streets, houses, religious structures and defensive works belong to a long sequence of occupation. They should not be imagined as if all stood together in one timeless cityscape.

Pakistan’s 2026 Tentative List submission describes the relationship between hill, terraces and lower urban area. It also identifies craft evidence, storage and religious precincts within and around the defended settlement. This range of material makes the site useful for asking how urban life was organised, rather than focusing only on a famous encounter with an invading ruler.

Excavation changes the scale of the story

Ca’ Foscari University’s account of the Italian Archaeological Mission describes work at Barikot beginning in 1984. It distinguishes the lower town, investigated over many seasons, from later work on the acropolis. In its account, the lower settlement includes an Indo-Greek defensive phase, while the hill preserves later religious and defensive structures.

The university describes a substantial temple podium associated with the Shahi period, decorated architectural remains and a later guardtower. These features make the acropolis a sequence of transformations rather than a single monument. Earlier deposits may also be disturbed or reused by later construction, so an object’s presence in fill must be interpreted through its archaeological context.

The Tentative List submission separately highlights an early apsidal Buddhist shrine and its successive remodelling. The importance is not merely an early date. It is the opportunity to examine how a religious building developed within an urban setting, alongside other activities and structures.

A town is more than its most celebrated building

Pottery, tools, production debris and small objects help explain activities that monumental architecture alone cannot reveal. Their value depends on recorded provenance: where they were found, in which layer and in relation to which structures. A beautiful object without context may be harder to connect with urban life than an ordinary fragment carefully documented during excavation.

This is why an archaeological plan needs a time dimension. Two adjacent walls can belong to different phases; an open space can acquire a building; a residential area can take on another function. Colour-coded phases or clearly dated plans can communicate these changes more honestly than a single reconstructed panorama.

Barikot’s inclusion on Pakistan’s Tentative List in 2026 should also be described accurately. It is part of a proposal process, not inscription as a World Heritage property. The significance of the excavated evidence does not depend on confusing those statuses.

The site offers a powerful way to think about continuity: not as an absence of change, but as repeated occupation, adaptation and rebuilding. Its streets and acropolis make a long-lived town visible precisely because archaeology can separate the different moments that now share the same ground.
