Butkara I: a stupa built through successive phases

Butkara I preserves repeated enlargement around a continuing sacred centre. Its successive stupa phases show why the history of a monument is not always a story of demolition and replacement.

At Butkara I in Swat, rebuilding did not simply erase what came before. The great stupa developed through successive enlargements that enclosed earlier forms. Its history is therefore nested within its fabric: a continuing sacred focus received new architectural bodies rather than being repeatedly abandoned for an unrelated monument elsewhere.

Research published in Problems of Chronology in Gandharan Art describes five main building periods, corresponding to the original construction and four subsequent reconstructions of the great stupa. It credits Domenico Faccenna's carefully recorded excavations of 1956–62 with establishing the long sequence. Counting those periods accurately matters; five phases do not mean an original building followed by five additional enlargements.

Continuity made through change

An enlargement can preserve the location and meaning of a monument while changing its size, surface and relationship to nearby structures. This is a different model of continuity from leaving every stone untouched. At Butkara, the earlier centre remained important even as later work transformed its appearance.

The surrounding precinct developed too. The federal archaeological catalogue describes the great stupa among smaller votive stupas, columns and other religious structures, with schist paving and a rectangular water tank on the eastern side. The main monument was part of an accumulating environment rather than a solitary object on an empty platform.

For someone reading the site, this creates a challenge. Features visible beside one another today may belong to different episodes of building. A complete-looking plan can conceal chronological complexity. The excavated relationships between foundations, surfaces and additions are what allow a sequence to be reconstructed.

Materials also changed

The specialist chronology study discusses a shift towards plastic materials and associated techniques during later phases, including the use of stucco in architectural and sculptural work. This should not be reduced to a simple story of artistic decline from stone to something less valuable. Different materials support different processes of modelling, repair and alteration.

The study of a material change asks what was replaced, what was retained and how new work related to existing images. Those questions can reveal practical decisions and changing artistic habits without assuming that every phase had the same resources or intentions.

The image of a stupa

A separate study by Luca Maria Olivieri examines an incised slab from Butkara I showing a tall, towered stupa. Such an object adds another kind of evidence: architecture represented in an image. It should not automatically be treated as an exact portrait of the surviving great stupa, but it can contribute to comparisons across Buddhist visual traditions.

The Department of Archaeology notes that sculptures from Butkara are held in Swat Museum. Moving between site and museum helps reconnect architectural setting with displaced objects. The strongest interpretation keeps the phases visible rather than compressing them into a single construction date. Butkara's enduring identity was made through repeated acts of building, donation, adaptation and care.
