How a site plan changes what visitors see

A site plan is a selective argument in lines, not a transparent view of the past. Its key, phases and purpose determine how it should be read.

From above, a ruin can become wonderfully legible. Rooms connect, passages acquire direction and scattered walls form an apparent whole. Yet a site plan is not a window through time. It is a drawing made for a purpose, using conventions that decide what a reader can see.

Find the key before finding the building

The Archaeology Data Service asks deposited maps and plans to document their layers and drawing conventions. Historic England's recording manual likewise treats conventions as part of archaeological documentation. Without that information, a broken line can be mistaken for a surviving wall, or an inferred continuation for an observed one.

A useful first reading therefore begins outside the monument's outline. Check the scale, orientation, date and legend. Ask whether the plan records exposed remains, reconstructs a phase or guides visitors through a modern site. These purposes can overlap, but they are not identical.

A visitor route, for example, may cross an area differently from an ancient passage. A path included for access should not silently become evidence about historical circulation. The drawing needs to distinguish what it explains about the past from what it provides for the present.

More than one time on one sheet

Plans often bring together remains from several phases. Unless those phases are separated, a reader may imagine all the structures functioning at once. An apparently straightforward doorway might belong to an alteration, while a nearby wall predates the room it now appears to enclose.

This is an interpretive problem, not a reason to distrust drawings. A well-labelled composite plan can be extremely useful. It becomes misleading only when its composite character disappears from the explanation.

At complex sites such as Taxila or Moenjodaro, the best question is not “Where is the one correct plan?” but “Which part of the site, and which question, does this plan address?” Different scales can legitimately show different information. A regional map cannot carry every masonry junction; a detailed trench plan cannot substitute for a wider landscape.

Digital presentation adds another responsibility. Cropping away a legend or shrinking labels until they cannot be read removes part of the argument while leaving the lines apparently authoritative. An attractive reproduction should retain the information needed to interpret it.

Reading a plan carefully changes the experience of a site. Empty areas become unrecorded, unexcavated or intentionally omitted spaces rather than proof of ancient emptiness. The drawing becomes a companion to observation, not an instruction to see more certainty than the evidence contains.
