The Indus script: why short texts resist easy answers

Short inscriptions offer patterns without an agreed translation. Reading Indus signs responsibly means separating what an object shows from what a proposed decipherment claims.

An Indus inscription can be immediately recognisable without being readable. Its signs invite comparison: an angular shape returns, a sequence resembles another, or an animal appears beneath a line of marks. Recognition, however, is not translation. A convincing account must explain how a proposed meaning was reached and whether the same method works beyond the example chosen to illustrate it.

Three different questions

What signs occur together? What kind of communication did they perform? What language, if any, did a particular sequence record? These are related questions, but answering the first does not automatically settle the others. In their 2009 study, Rajesh Rao and colleagues examined sequential patterns using a Markov model. Their work investigated statistical structure; it did not supply a generally accepted reading of individual inscriptions.

The difficulty is partly the material available. The study identifies brevity, the absence of a known bilingual and uncertainty about the underlying language as obstacles. A very short sequence offers few opportunities to test a proposed grammar. Assigning a familiar word to one sign may feel persuasive, yet several incompatible explanations can fit an isolated example.

The object still speaks

This does not make an inscribed object archaeologically silent. Kenoyer and Meadow's study of material excavated at Harappa between 1986 and 2007 places inscriptions within documented find contexts. Their approach demonstrates the value of asking where an object was recovered and what kind of object carried the marks, even when the marks cannot be translated.

For an archive reader, the practical distinction is between an observation and its interpretation. “This sign appears twice” reports something checkable. “This sign names a king” introduces a linguistic and historical claim. The second needs additional evidence, not simply confidence or a visually attractive diagram.

A useful decipherment proposal should therefore explain its sign readings consistently, identify exceptions and test previously unexamined inscriptions. A claim that changes its rules whenever a new object appears cannot be assessed in the same way as one that makes clear, repeatable predictions.

At Harappa or Moenjodaro, uncertainty should sharpen attention rather than end it. Examine the relationship between inscription, image and surface. Ask whether a photograph shows an original object or a reproduced impression. Then keep the unresolved language question visible. The achievement of Indus communication does not depend on supplying a modern sentence where the evidence does not yet support one.
