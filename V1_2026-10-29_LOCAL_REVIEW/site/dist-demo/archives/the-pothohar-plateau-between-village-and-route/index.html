Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The Pothohar plateau between village and route

Forts, settlement traces and a pass show how Pothohar can be read between local occupation and movement across a wider plateau.

Pothohar is often introduced as a landscape between larger destinations. That description can make its villages and smaller remains seem like intervals on somebody else’s journey. A more useful approach begins with occupation: places where people lived, worked, gathered and controlled access, as well as routes through which they moved.

The plateau does not present one uniform historical surface. Different kinds of evidence—settlement mounds, forts, religious buildings and road remains—belong to different periods and levels of preservation. Their proximity is worth investigating, but it cannot by itself make them part of a single system.

Enclosure and surrounding settlement

At Sangni, the federal inventory records a compact fort with corner bastions and a defined entrance. Dhan Gali is described as a ruined enclosure built in small brick and mortar, with a tentative Mughal attribution. These records supply useful architectural observations while leaving important questions open. A fort’s outline alone cannot identify all the people who used it or establish the extent of its authority.

The difference between a fort and the wider place around it is equally important. Walls concentrate attention, while less conspicuous occupation can disappear from a visitor’s mental map. A comprehensive account would ask about nearby settlement evidence and later uses, rather than treating everything outside the enclosure as empty background.

A route has a material history

The catalogue’s record for the Margalla Pass and Grand Trunk route introduces movement at another scale. It describes surviving road fabric and discusses historical associations. Such an entry should not be read as proof that every famous traveller used the exact surviving alignment. Roads can be repaired, widened, diverted and renamed; their history is rarely a single line maintained without change.

Putting a pass and a fort on the same map therefore creates a research question, not an automatic answer. Were their periods of use contemporary? What other approaches existed? Did the fort actually control movement along that route, or is the connection only a modern visual impression?

The landscape of recording

The Urban Unit’s Rawalpindi Division heritage report describes a planning method combining field visits, survey forms, digital recording and consultation. It also recognises that conditions have changed since earlier archaeological surveys. The inventory is consequently a record of investigation as well as a catalogue of places: its coverage depends on where researchers looked and when they recorded it.

This is particularly relevant to modest sites. A ruined wall or spread of pottery may receive less attention than an intact monument, despite its value for understanding settlement. Absence from a convenient visitor list does not establish absence of history.

Pothohar can thus be explored through two linked questions: how were particular places occupied, and how were they connected? Moving between village, enclosure and route makes the plateau a subject in its own right, without turning its many histories into a generic gateway between better-known regions.
