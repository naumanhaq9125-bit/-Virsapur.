The Sulaiman foothills as an archaeological borderland

A small recorded settlement on the Sulaiman foothills illustrates why borderland archaeology needs precise chronology and attention to local terrain.

An archaeological borderland is not necessarily a thin strip between two self-contained cultures. Along the Sulaiman foothills, the more useful questions concern relationships among slopes, passages and adjoining plains. A modern provincial boundary can help locate a record, but it cannot define the full range of movement or interaction in an earlier period.

The area also demonstrates why small archaeological sites deserve attention alongside prominent sacred places. Their physical modesty can conceal the importance of the questions they raise about settlement, material use and the history of survey.

A recorded site at Khalath

The federal catalogue describes Khalath as a small ruined settlement with a partly visible stone wall, a platform, ceramics and stone-working material. It links the record to a 2019 study by Zakirullah Jan and colleagues of sites along the Koh-e-Suleiman foothills in Dera Ghazi Khan. The study’s introduction identifies its focus as the late Neolithic settlements documented within a wider survey that also recorded other periods.

The catalogue’s dating fields require care. Its broad numerical chronology does not sit comfortably with all the material labels in the description. Rather than selecting whichever date sounds more impressive, the responsible reading separates the observed remains from the chronological interpretation and refers the discrepancy back to the original research.

What surface evidence can tell us

The description nevertheless contains meaningful distinctions. A built wall is evidence of construction; ceramics may help identify activities or periods; stone tools and cores raise questions about manufacture and use. Those materials should not be merged into a single imagined household scene unless their association has been demonstrated.

For example, finds visible across a site may come from more than one occupation or from disturbed deposits. A precise account needs to know where material was recovered and whether it came from a surface survey or an excavated context. These are not technical distractions: they determine what can be said about the people who used the place.

Different histories in one foothill region

The shrine of Sakhi Sarwar belongs to a very different historical and social context. Its presence in the same regional atlas should broaden the range of questions, not imply a direct continuity from the prehistoric settlement. Archaeological remains and a sacred place can share a landscape while requiring different kinds of evidence and different forms of respectful engagement.

This distinction also prevents the region from becoming merely a passage between better-known centres. A borderland has places of residence, devotion and work, not just movement through it. Their histories cannot be reconstructed from a line joining distant cities.

The Sulaiman foothills therefore reward a deliberately local scale of discovery. Begin with a named site and what has actually been recorded there; then consider its terrain and possible connections. Such an approach preserves the significance of the region while leaving genuine chronological and interpretive uncertainties open to further research.
