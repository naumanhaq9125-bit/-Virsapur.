Hafiz Hayat: reading a shrine as an ensemble

Hafiz Hayat’s shrine near the University of Gujrat is an ensemble of religious, residential and gathering spaces. Its significance extends beyond a single tomb.

A shrine is often introduced by the name of the person buried there. At Hafiz Hayat, near the University of Gujrat, that introduction is necessary but incomplete. The place is an ensemble: tombs, a mosque, a baradari and other buildings occupy a historic mound. The relationship between these elements offers a fuller subject than an isolated photograph of the principal grave.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums records several tombs and water-related features as well as religious and residential structures. The architectural heritage project Naqoosh-e-Javidan describes brickwork, lime plaster and fresco painting, and provides a plan of the complex. Its interpretation associates the site with the Jahangir period. That is a broad attribution rather than a secure date for every surviving wall.

Reading across the spaces

The first useful act is to locate the buildings in relation to one another. Which space opens toward another? Where is movement channelled through a doorway, and where is it dispersed across open ground? A plan can reveal these relationships more clearly than a sequence of picturesque close-ups. Photographs then add the qualities that a plan cannot: height, surface, shadow and the experience of approaching.

The baradari is important within this reading because it should not disappear behind the more immediately recognisable mosque and tomb. Likewise, a structure interpreted as a residence or sarai raises questions about the activities that accompanied religious devotion. The alternative labels should remain visible where the evidence is uncertain. Calling a room a school, guesthouse or kitchen without supporting evidence would create a false impression of a completely understood institution.

Painted plaster also deserves attention as part of a building rather than a separate artwork. A fragment may help explain the visual character of a room, but it does not necessarily recover its entire decorative scheme. Recording its position, scale and relationship to an opening makes it more informative than an isolated image of colour.

A layered place beside a modern institution

The proximity of the university creates a particularly useful opportunity for architectural learning. The site can be approached through measured observation, material study and archival research, with each discipline addressing a different question. A building survey, for example, could distinguish changes in masonry without pretending that every change can immediately be dated.

The heritage project recommends careful archaeological investigation before conservation. That sequence is important. Repair decisions can erase evidence if an uncertain arrangement is made to look confidently complete. An intervention should therefore be judged not only by how tidy the result appears, but by what historical information it retains and how clearly new work is identified.

Hafiz Hayat rewards an account that moves between the individual building and the whole setting. The tomb gives the complex a focus; the surrounding structures explain that the place had a wider spatial life. Preserving that relationship is the difference between conserving a collection of objects and understanding an ensemble.
