Archaeological survey versus excavation

Survey and excavation answer different questions. Understanding their methods prevents a mapped site from being mistaken for a fully excavated settlement.

A site can be archaeologically known without having been extensively excavated. It may have been identified through a systematic surface survey, mapped through visible features or investigated by a limited programme of testing. Those distinctions should survive when research is condensed into a heritage description.

Different questions, different evidence

The US National Park Service's explanation of site discovery distinguishes surface inspection, remote sensing, archival work and forms of testing. These methods can reveal where evidence is concentrated and help define the questions a later investigation might address. Excavation is not the automatic final step for every place identified.

Survey often concerns relationships across an area. Excavation investigates deposits and features in the portion of ground actually opened. Neither method supplies an unrestricted view of the past. A surface pattern may be affected by visibility and later disturbance, while a trench offers detail from a necessarily limited location.

Imagine a mound surveyed through scattered pottery and visible walls. That evidence may justify recording a settlement, but it does not establish the plan of every buried building. Conversely, a carefully excavated room cannot by itself describe the entire settlement around it. These are examples of scale, not claims about a particular Pakistani site.

Why excavation creates an archive

Oxford's recording guidance stresses that excavation removes the deposits it investigates. Information missed during that process cannot simply be recovered by returning the soil to its earlier position. Recording is therefore part of the investigation itself, not an administrative task added afterwards.

A useful report tells readers what was surveyed, what was excavated and how much remains unexamined. It should identify the methods used and the limitations of the resulting coverage. A map filled with confident outlines is not a substitute for that explanation.

For heritage discovery, this matters when comparing sites with very different research histories. One may have many published trenches; another may be represented by survey observations. The difference in documentation should not be mistaken for a difference in historical importance.

Nor should incomplete excavation be treated as an invitation for visitors to investigate independently. The point of understanding methods is to appreciate why controlled research and preservation matter, not to encourage collecting or digging.

A good atlas can make these distinctions visible through clear notes. “Surveyed”, “tested” and “excavated” describe different kinds of knowledge. Retaining those words gives readers a more accurate account of both what archaeology has established and what a landscape still holds beyond the available record.
