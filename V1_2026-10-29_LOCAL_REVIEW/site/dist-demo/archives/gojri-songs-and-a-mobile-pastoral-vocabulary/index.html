Gojri songs and a mobile pastoral vocabulary

Gojri songs can illuminate livelihood and movement, but language should not be reduced to a single nomadic identity.

A song about movement does not prove that every speaker of its language moves with livestock. Gojri offers a good reason to resist that shortcut. Language connects people whose work, residence and relationship to seasonal travel can differ considerably. Reading its oral literature only as the voice of an imagined, uniformly nomadic population would conceal the very diversity that makes it interesting.

Wayne Losey’s 2002 study of Gojri writing distinguishes nomadic pastoralists, settled agricultural communities and people practising seasonal movement. Its discussion of Hazara also differentiates particular pastoral groups and patterns of contact. These are observations from a situated study, not a timeless classification of every Gojri-speaking household. Occupational labels and language names cannot simply stand in for one another.

What a pastoral word can carry

For a listener, vocabulary connected with animals or terrain is an invitation to ask precise questions. Does a term distinguish an animal’s age, a kind of pasture, a stage of a journey or a relationship between people? Does the singer use it literally, affectionately or as a comparison? An English gloss such as cattle or mountains might be convenient while removing the distinction that gives the verse its force.

Those possibilities should be investigated through an actual recording and a knowledgeable speaker, not supplied from imagination. A useful annotation would connect a word to its place in the performance, retain the speaker’s explanation and distinguish that explanation from the editor’s inference. The same expression in conversation might do different work from its use in a refrain.

Losey also describes Gojri literary activity around a circle established at Nawanshahr near Abbottabad in the 1960s. That evidence places writing and organized literary exchange alongside oral transmission. It challenges a false opposition between a mobile people who supposedly only sing and settled institutions that supposedly produce literature.

Listening as a shared research task

The Forum for Language Initiatives reports participation by Gojri community representatives in its 2023 ethnomusicology workshop alongside several other language communities. The event concerned the skills needed to document indigenous music. It is evidence of active work on representation, not proof that a single workshop exhaustively documented a repertoire.

A strong song record needs more than a language label and an attractive landscape image. Ask who performed it, whether the words are their composition or an inherited version, what occasion shaped the performance and how the performer wishes to be credited. Record separately any restrictions on circulation. An accessible recording is not automatically an invitation to detach a singer’s voice for a tourism montage.

The most revealing comparison may be between two performances rather than two dictionary entries. A change in emphasis, audience or location can alter what listeners recognize in familiar words. Following those differences keeps livelihood within the story without turning livelihood into a compulsory identity. Gojri then appears as a language used by people making choices, not as a decorative soundtrack assigned to a single way of life.
