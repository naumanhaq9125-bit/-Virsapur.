Noorjehan Bilgrami and the documentation of ajrak

Noorjehan Bilgrami’s ajrak research shows how a craft can be documented through writing, film and attention to the sequence of making.

A finished piece of ajrak can be admired without revealing the decisions that produced it. Repeated patterns conceal repeated acts of judgement: aligning a block, preparing a resist, washing cloth and assessing colour. Noorjehan Bilgrami’s contribution to craft documentation is to direct attention from the attractive surface towards a process, and from a generic textile label towards knowledge held by makers.

Sahapedia’s contributor record links her research to the book Sindh jo Ajrak and subsequently to the documentary Sun, Fire, River, Ajrak: Cloth from the Soil of Sindh. A related paper appears in the proceedings of the Textile Society of America’s 2000 symposium. These are different forms of record, intended for different kinds of encounter; they should not be treated as interchangeable evidence.

Recording a sequence

Bilgrami’s illustrated Sahapedia account identifies processes including steaming, preparation of a mud resist and resist printing. Its captions distinguish stages rather than presenting all visible activity as simply dyeing. That precision is important. A description that collapses the sequence into one colourful photograph loses the relationship between preparation, protected areas and later colour development.

Film can potentially preserve movement and timing that a still image cannot. A book can place terms, diagrams or comparisons side by side. A conference paper can advance an argument and expose it to specialist discussion. This article has not viewed the documentary itself, so it does not claim that a particular action or named maker appears in it. The documented existence of the film is nevertheless part of the history of how ajrak has been communicated.

The 2000 paper’s abstract also proposes connections between ajrak, ancient material culture and patterned textiles found farther west. Such comparisons are a research question, not automatic proof of an uninterrupted technique stretching across millennia. A similar motif may travel, recur or receive a new interpretation. Establishing continuity requires evidence of materials, methods and intervening examples, not resemblance alone.

Documentation is a relationship

The distinction matters because craft histories can unintentionally give an ancient object more authority than a living practitioner. A maker’s vocabulary and explanation deserve careful recording in their own right, even when a proposed archaeological connection remains uncertain. Conversely, an account of inherited practice is not a substitute for identifying the evidence behind a remote historical claim.

For future documentation, useful questions include who names each stage, whether terms vary between workshops, and how makers want their contributions credited. Recording permission and the intended use of photographs should be agreed rather than assumed. These are recommendations for responsible research, not claims that this article has conducted new interviews.

Bilgrami’s body of documentation offers a starting point for that work. It makes ajrak legible as accumulated practical knowledge while leaving room to examine how research itself selects, arranges and interprets. The next step is not merely to reproduce its most memorable images, but to retain the specificity of process and the agency of the people who continue to make the cloth.
