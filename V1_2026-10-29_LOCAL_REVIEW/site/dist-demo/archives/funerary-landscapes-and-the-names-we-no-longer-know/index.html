Funerary landscapes and the names we no longer know

Unknown names do not make a cemetery historically empty. At Makli and Chaukhandi, materials, inscriptions and the relationships between monuments preserve different kinds of evidence.

A named royal tomb offers a convenient story: a ruler, a date and a monument. A grave whose occupant is unknown can seem less legible, but it is not without history. Its material, position, construction and relationship to neighbouring graves may preserve evidence even when the person’s name has been lost or has not yet been securely identified.

This is a useful way to approach the funerary landscapes of Makli and Chaukhandi. They should not be treated as identical cemeteries, nor reduced to a collection of photogenic carvings. Each has its own setting and evidence.

A monument without a secure name

UNESCO’s account of conservation work at Makli in 2025 describes attention to the Shaikh Jyio Tomb and a nearby unknown tomb associated with the Samma period. The unidentified monument was documented, assessed and investigated before work proceeded. A missing personal name did not make its structure or conservation needs less significant.

The example separates two kinds of knowledge. Researchers may have evidence for a broad period or building technique without knowing who was commemorated. Conversely, a traditional name may survive while the date of the visible fabric remains uncertain.

An honest archive keeps those levels distinct. “Unknown” should identify the particular gap, not suggest that nothing can be said. Nor should a plausible name be inserted merely to make a place page feel complete.

Decoration is evidence, not a biography

The federal catalogue describes Chaukhandi’s carved stone graves and the motifs used in its interpretation of male and female commemorations. Such descriptions provide a starting point for studying how identity was represented. They do not automatically reveal the occupation, rank or life story of every individual.

A horseman or weapon motif may have a commemorative role without proving that the person below performed a specific deed. Jewellery carved on stone is not an inventory of possessions recovered from a burial. To turn either into a personal biography would require further evidence.

The carvings still deserve close attention. Recording their location, relationship to inscriptions and differences between monuments can support comparison without forcing a confident story onto each image.

The cemetery is more than its largest buildings

UNESCO’s Makli description connects the necropolis with the historical importance of Thatta and identifies monuments associated with several kinds of social figure. The better-known structures help orient a visit, but they should not make smaller markers disappear from the record.

Paths, groups of graves and changes in construction can reveal relationships at a scale larger than an individual tomb. A survey can record those relationships even where a visitor cannot read an inscription. Such documentation must be undertaken without moving stones or treating fragile surfaces as seats or climbing points.

A cemetery also concerns the dead and those who continue to care about them. Lack of a legible name does not remove the obligation to behave respectfully.

The most revealing encounter may therefore begin with a modest question: what is actually preserved here, and what kind of evidence could answer the next question? That approach leaves uncertainty visible while recognising that unnamed people and monuments remain part of the historical landscape.
