Khwaja Ghulam Farid and the vocabulary of Rohi

Rohi in Khwaja Ghulam Farid’s poetry is both a named landscape and a changing field of longing, weather and encounter.

Rohi is not an empty stage in Khwaja Ghulam Farid’s poetry. The name carries a landscape into the language of longing, but its emotional character changes. Dry ground can become a place of difficult wandering; rain can alter the scene; the same desert can be imagined as a meeting place. Reading those changes is more useful than assigning Rohi one fixed symbolic meaning.

Shahzad Qaiser’s translation and commentary, Understanding Diwan-i-Farid, makes several such contrasts accessible to an English reader. In the selected passages considered here, separation appears through movement across desert and rocky ground, while a rainy season transforms dryness into growth. Elsewhere Rohi becomes the desired setting for an encounter with the beloved. These are brief examples from a much larger collection, not a claim to summarise its whole vocabulary.

A place name within a poem

The important feature is the relationship between surroundings and the speaker’s condition. Rain is not only weather information, and distance is not only a measurable interval. Yet the material setting is not disposable. The force of the image depends on knowing something of thirst, seasonal change and movement through difficult terrain.

Qaiser places translation beside commentary and gives the passages a spiritual interpretation. Readers should keep those layers distinguishable. The English wording is a translation; the accompanying explanation is an interpreter’s account of meaning. Neither should be silently converted into a direct autobiographical statement about where Farid stood on a particular day or what route he followed.

That distinction becomes especially important when literary material is linked to a heritage map. Kot Mithan and the physical landscape of Cholistan can provide orientation, but a poetic reference does not establish coordinates for an episode. The text may bring several forms of distance together: geographical separation, emotional absence and a spiritual search.

A vocabulary that continues to travel

The Poetry Translation Centre supplies a useful modern comparison. Its poem You Deserted Me in Rohi is by Shakir Shujaabadi, translated through a workshop involving Nukhbah Langah. The accompanying note identifies Rohi as an important symbol in Farid’s Saraiki mystical poetry. This is evidence of a continuing vocabulary, not permission to attribute the newer poem to Farid.

The distinction is easy to lose when recordings, translation pages and social posts travel without their credits. Keeping the poet, translator and performer visible protects both the historical writer and the later artist. It also allows readers to notice continuity without pretending that all uses of a familiar image are identical.

Farid’s Rohi is consequently best approached through a named edition and a small number of closely read passages. Follow how the landscape changes with the voice, and resist turning every image into a ready-made moral. The result is a more attentive literary geography: one in which a real region remains present, while poetry changes what distance, weather and belonging can mean.
