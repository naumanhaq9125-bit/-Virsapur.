Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The Indus delta and the changing idea of a port

Excavation at Banbhore shows why identifying a historic port requires more than matching a ruined city to today’s shoreline.

A ruined city beside a creek does not explain its former harbour at a glance. The coastline may have changed, channels may have shifted, and later buildings may cover earlier activity. Banbhore is particularly valuable because its excavations show how an apparently simple question—where was the port?—can remain open even when a settlement’s wider maritime importance is clear.

The archaeological project places Banbhore on the northern bank of Gharo Creek, well inland from the present shoreline. Its account includes a fortified area and extensive remains outside it. This broader setting matters: a port is not only a gate or quay, but a relationship among water access, settlement and activities on land.

Names are hypotheses too

The Pakistani-Italian research team discusses Banbhore’s proposed identification with places named in historical texts, including Daybul or Debal. The authors acknowledge continuing debate. Similarity of location and evidence of trade are important, but neither automatically settles the relationship between an excavated site and every ancient place-name associated with it.

Keeping that distinction visible avoids a circular argument in which a name is used to prove the interpretation that originally produced the name. Archaeological evidence and written accounts can illuminate one another without being forced into premature agreement.

Excavation can revise a persuasive feature

Eveha’s report on the French mission’s 2015 work offers a concrete example. A wall previously considered in connection with a possible harbour location was re-examined. The excavators interpreted it instead as two phases of masonry associated with a small building and reinforcement along a change in slope. They concluded that the original port location remained uncertain.

The same campaign recorded alternating alluvial and sandy deposits, with traces of relatively short occupations in some levels. These observations linked settlement activity with changing water conditions. The report also distinguished earlier occupation from the construction and alteration of the visible fortification. A standing wall therefore cannot be assumed to represent the beginning of the place.

A port is a relationship, not a silhouette

For a reader, these findings suggest a different way of looking at the delta. Instead of searching only for a recognisable harbour shape, ask which evidence supports a relationship with water: deposits, structures, artefacts and the changing levels at which people occupied the ground. Each contributes something different.

The distinction is especially useful when modern photographs show dry ground where an account discusses river activity. The mismatch is not necessarily an error; it may be the historical question. Yet explaining it requires dated evidence, not a general assumption that every coastal ruin was abandoned because the sea withdrew.

Banbhore’s value lies partly in this capacity to revise expectations. Its port history becomes more interesting when uncertainty is retained: a city whose connections can be investigated, a delta whose form has changed, and an excavation record that shows how interpretations improve when earlier assumptions are tested.
