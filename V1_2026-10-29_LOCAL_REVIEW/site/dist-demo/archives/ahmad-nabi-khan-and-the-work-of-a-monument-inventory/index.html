Ahmad Nabi Khan and the work of a monument inventory

Ahmad Nabi Khan’s documented publications show why inventory work matters, and why an older catalogue is a starting point rather than a final authority.

A heritage inventory can be easy to overlook because its entries are short and its labour is dispersed. Yet the ability to identify a modest tomb, find its bibliography or distinguish it from a similarly named building depends on such work. Ahmad Nabi Khan’s name appears repeatedly in Pakistan’s monument records, making his publications a useful route into the practice of documenting heritage.

The federal catalogue entry for the tomb of Nawab Bahadur Khan in Lahore cites his 1987 publication Archaeological Sites and Historical Monuments Protected Under the Antiquities Act, 1975. The entry records an octagonal plan, brick construction and a later use as a theatre. These are different kinds of information: form and material describe a building, while a change of use opens a historical question.

What a short record makes possible

Even a concise description can direct attention towards evidence that a visitor might otherwise miss. An octagonal platform is a feature to locate and compare. A reported later use suggests documents to seek. A named publication gives the researcher a route beyond the current webpage. The value lies not only in the facts listed, but in making further checking possible.

That does not mean every field on the digital page was supplied by Khan in 1987. Coordinates, administrative categories and website labels may have been added later. The publication date should therefore remain attached to the cited source, not transferred indiscriminately to all present metadata. Nor does a protected-monument inventory necessarily represent every place considered culturally important by local communities.

The Sindh Exploration and Excavation Branch’s research-library catalogue documents a broader range of work under Khan’s name, including publications concerned with Multan, mosque architecture and archaeological meetings. Japan’s CiNii author record also connects the name to museum and art publications. These bibliographical records show the range of subjects available for study; they are not evidence that this article has read every listed volume.

An inventory remains open to revision

Older descriptions may preserve observations of features that have since changed. They can also contain uncertain dates, inherited interpretations or language that needs reconsideration. The appropriate response is neither unquestioning repetition nor dismissal. Compare the record with later studies, identify the source of a correction and retain the earlier account as part of the documentation history.

For a digital atlas, that means keeping a stable identity for a place while allowing its description to improve. A revised name should not silently create a duplicate. A new coordinate should be supported by site evidence. A changed interpretation should be distinguishable from the simple correction of a typographical error.

Khan’s contribution can therefore be approached through the continuing usefulness of documented work rather than an unsupported biographical tribute. An inventory gives future researchers something to question, compare and extend. Its legacy is strongest when the chain from physical place to description to publication remains visible, allowing each new generation to build on evidence rather than begin again from an unattributed paragraph.
