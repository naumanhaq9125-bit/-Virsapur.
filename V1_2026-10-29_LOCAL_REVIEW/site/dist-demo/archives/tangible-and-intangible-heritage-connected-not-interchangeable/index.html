Tangible and intangible heritage: connected, not interchangeable

Buildings, objects, skills and performances belong to connected histories, but they require different forms of description and care.

A musical instrument can be held in a collection. The knowledge required to make and play it cannot be understood simply by measuring the object. This is a useful starting point for distinguishing tangible and intangible heritage. The distinction concerns what is being described and sustained; it does not divide culture into two unrelated worlds.

The 2003 UNESCO Convention identifies practices, expressions, knowledge and skills recognised by communities as forms of intangible cultural heritage. It also acknowledges associated instruments, objects and spaces. The relationship is therefore present in the definition itself. A building may provide a setting for a practice, and an object may be essential to it, without either being identical to the living knowledge involved.

Different questions, connected evidence

Consider a hypothetical embroidered textile. An object study might document its fibres, stitches, dimensions, condition and ownership history. A study of living practice would ask who teaches the technique, how terms are used, how work is valued and how makers decide to continue or change it. These enquiries can inform one another, but an answer to one is not automatically evidence for the other.

A historic example in a display case does not establish that a technique is still practised in exactly the same way. Equally, a contemporary maker’s variation does not make the work culturally meaningless because it differs from a museum specimen. A guide that presents only an unchanged ancestral pattern may miss experimentation, changes in materials or the maker’s own account of what matters.

UNESCO’s discussion of safeguarding distinguishes supporting transmission from freezing practice into a fixed form. It emphasises the recognition and involvement of the communities concerned. In editorial terms, this means that a published photograph of a performance is not a substitute for asking practitioners how they describe it or what conditions apply to its documentation.

Choosing the right kind of care

The practical implications differ. A damaged object may need a conservator’s examination. A threatened skill may need time, income, teaching opportunities or access to materials. A building repair could support a gathering, but it cannot by itself guarantee that the knowledge shared there will continue. These are illustrative possibilities, not a universal programme for every tradition.

When Virsapur links an object, a place and a story, the links should make those relationships easier to explore. They should not imply that ownership of an object confers authority over a community’s practice. Nor should a place description speak for everyone who uses it. The strongest account allows the material evidence and the people involved to remain distinct, connected and open to further understanding.
