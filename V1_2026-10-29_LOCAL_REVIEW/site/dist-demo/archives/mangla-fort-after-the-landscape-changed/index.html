Mangla Fort after the landscape changed

Mangla Fort belongs to an older river landscape now experienced beside major water infrastructure; the two histories should be connected without being conflated.

Mangla Fort and Mangla Dam share a name and a landscape, but they are not one historical project. The fort belongs to an earlier pattern of occupation along the Jhelum; the dam transformed the wider setting in the twentieth century. Reading them together can reveal change, provided the modern water landscape is not projected backwards into the fort’s origins.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes the fort beside the river, with an irregular enclosure formed from adjoining polygonal sections. Dressed sandstone, substantial walls and numerous narrow openings are prominent in its account. The main entrance is placed in the eastern wall and associated stylistically with the Sikh period. The record does not establish a single exact year in which every surviving part was built.

The catalogue also preserves local accounts of religious occupants and temples within the enclosure. These should remain attributed traditions. Without independently identified structures or inscriptions, the recollections cannot be converted into a confident plan of four named temples.

A later infrastructure landscape

WAPDA records that construction of Mangla Dam began in 1962 and was completed in 1967. It describes the project as a major water and power undertaking on the Jhelum and records later raising work. These are separate historical layers from the fort. The reservoir’s presence in a contemporary photograph does not prove that the earlier builders designed their position beside an identical body of water.

This distinction is especially important when a place is photographed primarily for its views. Water can make a ruin appear timeless, yet the apparent natural setting may itself be the result of a comparatively recent engineering intervention. A heritage interpretation should help the viewer recognise that change rather than conceal it.

Read the enclosure at its own scale

The fort’s irregular shape raises questions about how walls respond to terrain. Its openings and entrance invite a closer look at movement and observation. Those are architectural questions that can be asked without inventing a siege narrative or assigning a function to every damaged room.

Comparing an older plan or photograph with a current one would be particularly valuable here. The comparison should identify its dates and viewpoints, because a change in camera position can look like a change in the building. Likewise, a survey’s description of missing roofs or damaged masonry should be presented as a dated observation, not silently updated to the present.

Mangla also encourages a distinction between shared geography and shared identity. The fort, dam, reservoir and nearby settlements each have their own histories. Linking them on an atlas can make their relationships visible, but combining them into one catch-all attraction would lose the specificity that makes those relationships meaningful.

The result is a more layered way to see the place. The fort remains an architectural subject in its own right, while the modern water infrastructure explains why its surroundings are encountered differently today. Neither history needs to erase the other.
