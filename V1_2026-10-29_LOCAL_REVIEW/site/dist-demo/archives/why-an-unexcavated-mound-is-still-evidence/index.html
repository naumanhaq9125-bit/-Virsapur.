Why an unexcavated mound is still evidence

A mound can preserve valuable evidence before excavation. Surface survey and geophysics ask different questions, and their results should guide careful research rather than turn an archaeological place into a treasure target.

An unexcavated mound is not an empty page. Its shape, surface material and relationship to the surrounding landscape can already provide evidence. What it does not provide is unrestricted permission to imagine the buildings, people or events beneath it. The challenge is to ask questions that the available methods can answer without pretending that investigation has already revealed everything.

Surface survey offers one starting point. Sidra Gulzar’s study of Ganweriwala explains how systematic examination of a site can record landscape, settlement form and the distribution of material. Such work can help researchers plan later investigation. Ganweriwala has now been excavated; its earlier survey remains a useful example of knowledge produced before trenches were opened.

Seeing a pattern without removing a layer

A concentration of pottery or building material can identify an area worth closer study. It does not automatically mark the precise boundary of an ancient house or workshop. Material may have moved through erosion, cultivation or later activity, and different parts of a surface may be unequally visible.

Recording those conditions is therefore part of the evidence. A map of finds without information about how the survey was conducted can suggest a precision it does not possess. Repeated observations and a documented collection strategy make comparison more meaningful.

What geophysics measures

Historic England explains that the choice of geophysical technique depends on the expected target, its depth, the area to be covered and local conditions such as soils, geology and vegetation. There is no single instrument that reveals every kind of archaeological feature equally well.

Oxford’s introduction makes another essential point: geophysical plots represent measured physical properties, such as electrical resistance or magnetism. They are not direct photographs of the past. A striking pattern may suggest a wall, ditch or other feature, but interpretation has to consider alternative explanations and the limits of the survey.

An apparently blank result is equally easy to overread. Failure to detect a feature with one method does not establish that no archaeology exists. The question is whether the chosen technique and conditions were suitable for the kinds of remains being sought.

Preservation is an active decision

Leaving deposits in place can preserve relationships that future research may examine with better methods or different questions. Excavation is not the only meaningful sign that a site matters. Documentation, monitoring and protection from disturbance can be substantial forms of archaeological work.

For a heritage atlas, the term unexcavated should therefore describe a dated research status, not a promise of hidden treasure. It should never encourage casual digging or collecting. Related sites may illustrate different stages of investigation rather than all sharing the same status.

A mound’s value lies in the information it contains and the questions it can help answer. Recognising that value before excavation is one of the clearest ways to move from curiosity about ancient objects toward care for archaeological evidence.
