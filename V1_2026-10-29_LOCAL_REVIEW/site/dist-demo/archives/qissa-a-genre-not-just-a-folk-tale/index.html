Qissa: a genre, not just a folk tale

Qissa connects storytelling, print and performance, but its scope changes across literary settings. Punjabi and Urdu sources warn against a single narrow definition.

Translating qissa simply as folk tale can make the term sound smaller and more uniform than the works it describes. A qissa may be encountered in writing, through performance or in a remembered retelling. Its form and cultural setting deserve attention before it is assigned to a familiar English shelf.

Farina Mir’s study of colonial Punjab examines qisse as a lively literature of epics and romances, connecting print, performance and ideas of belonging. Her research asks how Punjabi literary practice flourished despite the limited official patronage available to it under British rule. The important contrast is between institutional marginalization and cultural activity—not between supposedly oral people and literate elites.

Musharraf Ali Farooqi’s introduction to Fabulous Machinery for the Curious, a collection of translated Urdu qissas makes a different caution. It notes that qissa in Urdu can cover both short and long narratives, in prose as well as verse. A definition drawn from one Punjabi corpus should therefore not be imposed automatically on every Urdu work bearing the term.

Genre is a way of asking questions

A useful genre label helps readers notice conventions. Does the story announce a narrator? How are dialogue and description handled? What kind of attention does it ask from a listener or reader? Those questions are more productive than assuming that every qissa has the same length, metre or relationship to historical events.

Print adds another set of clues. A title page, an illustration, an editor’s introduction or a publisher’s series can frame the work for an audience. A performance may frame it through an opening address or the occasion on which it is heard. Both are part of the story’s circulation, even when neither appears in a short plot summary.

A familiar romance also makes it tempting to treat different tellings as copies of one ideal original. Comparison can instead ask what each version does with inherited material. A changed emphasis on a character, an expanded exchange or a new setting for performance may be central to the work’s purpose rather than evidence of decay.

Keep the archive specific

For a catalogue, record the author or attributed author where known, the language, the edition and the medium being described. If a performer retells a printed work, identify that relationship when the evidence supports it. If the connection is uncertain, uncertainty is more useful than an invented chain of transmission.

The term folklore should not function as permission to omit individual makers. A story’s wide circulation does not mean that every performance or translation is anonymous, unowned or available for unrestricted reuse. Nor does an association with a place verify every narrated event as local history.

Understanding qissa in this way enlarges the encounter. We can appreciate the pleasure of a story while also examining its language, social world and routes between listeners and readers. The label becomes an opening into those relationships, not a small box that removes the work from literature as soon as it is called traditional.

References

  1. The Social Space of Language: Vernacular Culture in British Colonial Punjab — Farina Mir / University of California Press (accessed 2026-10-22)
  2. Fabulous Machinery for the Curious: The Garden of Urdu Classical Literature, introduction — Musharraf Ali Farooqi / University of California Press (accessed 2026-10-22)
