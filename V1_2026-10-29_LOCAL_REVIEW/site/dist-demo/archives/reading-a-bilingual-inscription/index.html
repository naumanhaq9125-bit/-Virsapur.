Language, script and the evidence for a bilingual inscription

Two scripts do not automatically mean two languages. Ashokan rock edicts and a documented Indo-Greek coin show how to ask the right questions.

Before calling an inscription bilingual, ask two different questions: what language is being expressed, and what script is used to write it? A language can be written in more than one script, while a single script can serve more than one language. Counting visually different letter forms is therefore not enough to establish bilingual communication.

The Ashokan rock edicts linked from this article offer a useful warning. Shahbazgarhi’s Tentative List description identifies a Prakrit text written in Kharosthi. Prakrit and Kharosthi are not the names of two languages in that description. One identifies the language, the other the writing system. A reader should not turn that pair of terms into a claim that the monument carries two parallel translations.

A small object offers a comparison

The British Museum’s coin IOC.104, attributed to Menander I, records a Greek inscription on one side and a Kharosthi legend on the other. Its catalogue describes a wheel and a palm frond among the designs. The object was collected at Begram in present-day Afghanistan; it is a comparative example from the wider region, not a newly identified Pakistani monument.

Even here, the catalogue’s script description is a starting point. To compare the actual messages, a reader needs an edited reading and identification of the language represented by each legend. The presence of two writing systems does not, by itself, tell us whether their wording is equivalent, whether titles differ or how a contemporary viewer understood them.

Read the relationship between the texts

For a securely identified bilingual inscription, begin by locating the two texts on the object. Are they placed side by side, on opposing faces or in different parts of a building? Are they contemporary, or could one have been added later? Those questions connect linguistic interpretation with the object’s material history rather than treating words as detached statements.

Next compare what an editor says is actually present. A readable letter, a restored gap and a disputed reading carry different levels of certainty. An English translation may smooth all three into a confident sentence unless its notes are consulted. Two fluent translations can also hide differences in titles, names or formulae that matter to the historical argument.

A bilingual text can suggest an effort to address different readers, but it cannot establish that every inhabitant of a region knew both languages. Nor does the language of a ruler’s proclamation necessarily describe ordinary conversation. Claims about education, identity or administration require additional evidence beyond the inscription’s mere existence.

The reward for this slower approach is a more interesting encounter. Instead of treating multilingual writing as a slogan about cultural fusion, we can examine a particular act of communication: who commissioned it, what was made legible and how its forms were arranged. The first step remains modest and precise—name the language and script separately, then show the evidence for calling a text bilingual.
