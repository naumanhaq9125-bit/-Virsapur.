Objects & Material Culture

An Indus seal as a made object

An Indus seal can be examined as a worked material before anyone attempts to explain its signs.

A seal is easily reduced to its picture: an animal, a row of signs, an intriguing composition. Yet its surface also belongs to a manufactured object. Before asking what an inscription means, we can ask what was cut, what was heated and which observations belong to this particular specimen. That change of attention brings an otherwise anonymous maker into view without inventing a name or translating an unread text.

Harappa's published photographic record includes an instructive contrast. Jonathan Mark Kenoyer identifies an unfired rectangular steatite seal beside a fired and glazed example, both assigned to the later Harappa Phase. The same commentary distinguishes another fired and glazed seal from the preceding phase. These are not interchangeable illustrations of one timeless technique: their material condition and archaeological dating are part of the evidence.

From soft stone to finished surface

Kenoyer's workshop study explains that steatite could be shaped while relatively soft and subsequently transformed by high-temperature firing. This gives the finished object a history that its present hardness alone does not reveal. The study also concerns faience, a different manufactured material; a pale or glazed appearance therefore should not replace an actual material identification.

Imagine documenting a seal for an exhibition. A face-on photograph records the composition, but a second view of its edge could help readers understand thickness and damage. A scale prevents a small carving from being mentally enlarged into a monumental relief. A material description tells us something that colour reproduction cannot. These are different kinds of information, and none needs to compete with the visual pleasure of the image.

An individual button seal provides a useful anchor. The Harappa Archaeological Research Project records H2000-4432/2174-3 from the Trench 54 area as fired steatite bearing four concentric-circle designs. Keeping that identifier with the image allows an editor to distinguish this find from superficially similar pieces. A generic caption such as “ancient seal” would discard that connection.

Making, use and meaning are different questions

Manufacturing evidence does not automatically establish how long a seal was used, who controlled it or what every mark signified. A proposed sequence of carving and firing is a statement about production. A claim about an owner's occupation requires another kind of support. An interpretation of the signs requires something different again. The confidence attached to one conclusion cannot simply be transferred to the others.

For a visitor, the most rewarding exercise is to keep those questions alongside one another. What can the photograph show? What has an excavator recorded? What has a materials specialist established? What remains an interpretation? Looking this way does not make the object less evocative. It replaces an imagined certainty with a more tangible achievement: a person selected a material and made a small surface capable of carrying an exceptionally deliberate design.
