Khaplu Polo Ground as a historic public place

Khaplu’s polo ground shows how an open space can carry architectural, sporting and communal heritage without a monumental roof or façade.

An open ground can be as culturally specific as a palace. At Khaplu, the polo ground belongs in a heritage account because it is a defined place for gathering and performance, not simply an unbuilt gap beside an important building. Its meaning depends on the relationship between the playing surface, its edges, the surrounding town and the people who use it.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums records the ground south of Khaplu town, near the palace, and attributes its construction in 1890 to Raja Ali Sher Khan. Its account also distinguishes an older ground near the Hachi settlement, describing a shift in community use after 1972. That distinction is important: a tradition can continue while its principal venue changes.

These catalogue dates should be understood as the record’s historical account, not evidence that every boundary or surface visible today has remained unchanged since the nineteenth century. Open spaces are maintained, adapted and sometimes reorganised, just as buildings are.

A ground becomes a gathering

Abdul Hameed’s illustrated account of the 2017 Jashn-e-Ghanche describes polo alongside other sports, music and dance at Khaplu’s festival. His report places the ground beside the former royal palace and records participation by teams from different parts of Baltistan. It provides a dated example of the space in use, rather than an abstract assertion that culture happens there.

The account’s value lies in that specificity. It shows how one event brought competition and performance together. It should not be turned into a promise that the same programme, teams or dates recur every year. A historical description of a festival is not a current events calendar.

A government development programme later listed improvement of the boundary wall at Khaplu’s Shahi Polo Ground. The budget entry is evidence of an identified maintenance project, not proof that every proposed work was completed. It nevertheless reminds us that the edges of an open place require care too.

Tangible space, living practice

Polo itself cannot be preserved by conserving a wall alone. Equally, the activity needs somewhere to take place. This makes the ground a useful example of the relationship between tangible heritage and living practice: neither is adequately represented if the other disappears from the account.

For interpretation, a photograph of an empty field and a photograph of a match answer different questions. The first can show boundaries and relation to neighbouring buildings; the second can show how people occupy the space. Neither should stand alone as a complete history, and images of participants deserve the same care over context and permission as other cultural documentation.

Khaplu’s polo ground expands what a visitor might recognise as heritage. The palace may draw attention first, but the neighbouring open space introduces another scale of social life. It is a place where architecture includes the arrangement of an absence: room left for movement, spectatorship and shared occasions that cannot fit inside a monument.
