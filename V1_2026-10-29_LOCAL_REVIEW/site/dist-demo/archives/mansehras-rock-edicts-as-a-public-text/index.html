Mansehra’s rock edicts as a public text

Mansehra’s rock edicts turn an existing landscape into a medium for imperial communication. Their script, placement and comparison with other versions reveal more than a general claim to ancient writing.

At Mansehra, three large boulders carry a form of architecture without a building. Their surfaces were made into a medium for the edicts of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka. The result joins text, rock and route: a political message placed in a landscape rather than confined to a manuscript that could be moved elsewhere.

Pakistan's Tentative List description dates the inscriptions to the middle of the third century BCE and identifies fourteen major edicts written in Kharoshthi, from right to left. The federal archaeological catalogue likewise treats the boulders as a related group. The physical carrier is therefore essential to the site's identity; a printed translation explains the words but cannot replace their setting.

A message beside movement

The UNESCO-hosted submission places the edicts beside an old route connecting the Peshawar region with northern areas and Taxila. This location invites questions about intended audiences. People moving through a route encountered a marked place; the inscription did not have to travel to them in the same way as a document.

That does not prove universal literacy or that every passer-by read the text directly. Public communication can involve readers, listeners, interpreters and repeated explanation. The surviving words establish that a message was inscribed, but the full history of its reception requires evidence beyond the stone itself.

The edicts concern Ashoka's understanding of dharma, described in the Tentative List account as righteous conduct or law. A careful interpretation distinguishes the ruler's declared principles from independent evidence about how society actually behaved. An official statement is a source for political intention and representation, not a transparent description of everyday life.

Script, language and comparison

Kharoshthi is the script: the system of signs in which the text was written. Identifying a script is not the same task as translating the language or explaining a historical term. Keeping those stages separate makes the work of reading ancient inscriptions more intelligible.

The University of Oslo's Bibliotheca Polyglotta places Mansehra within a comparative set of Ashokan rock-edict records, alongside locations such as Girnar, Kalsi and Shahbazgarhi. Such a framework allows versions to be studied in relation to one another rather than assuming every inscription is an identical copy. Its record for the thirteenth edict explicitly identifies the Mansehra version.

Recording a vulnerable surface

The British Library's catalogue includes a historical image record for Mansehra's edicts IX–XI. A photograph can preserve information about a surface at a particular moment, but its usefulness depends on knowing what it shows, when it was made and how it relates to the actual rock.

Visitors should never trace, rub or wet an inscription to make its letters easier to see. The desire to read must not damage the evidence being read. Mansehra rewards a slower encounter: first the boulder in its setting, then the marked surface, and finally the scholarly work that makes the text accessible. Together, those scales reveal an ancient act of communication still open to careful study.
