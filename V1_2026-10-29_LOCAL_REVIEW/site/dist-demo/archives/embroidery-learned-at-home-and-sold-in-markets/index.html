Embroidery learned at home and sold in markets

Embroidery can connect household learning to market income, but its value cannot be understood without the maker’s time, choices and position in the supply chain.

A piece of embroidery can move from a household to a market without carrying the name of the person who stitched it. Along that journey, an account of family skill can become a sales description while hours of work disappear from view. Understanding the craft requires attention to both learning and exchange: how someone acquires competence, and how that competence becomes income.

Learning does not happen in one way

World Bank research on low-income urban women’s work includes accounts of skills learned within families and the practical importance of work arrangements compatible with domestic responsibilities. Embroidery appears within a wider discussion of livelihoods, not as a single experience shared by every woman. Household learning can be valuable without making the household the only appropriate place for work.

A 2017 Balochistan Review study of home-based embroidery workers similarly connects craft income to household circumstances and choices. Its participants should not become a composite imaginary artisan whose story supposedly represents an entire province. Their evidence is strongest when its setting and limits remain visible.

For future documentation, asking who taught a stitch is only a beginning. A maker may also wish to discuss how she developed speed, learned to interpret an order, selected materials or decided which work to accept. These questions recognise judgement and experience, rather than reducing expertise to an inherited female instinct.

The route to the buyer

The ILO’s Pakistan’s Hidden Workers draws on research conducted in two Karachi locations in 2016. It examines home-based work within wider supply chains. Its local findings cannot serve as current national wage statistics, but they challenge the assumption that working at home means working outside an economic system.

WIEGO’s research also records irregular orders and intermediary relationships in home-based production. The existence of an intermediary is not, by itself, an explanation of every outcome. A useful account asks who supplies materials, agrees a rate, checks the work and bears the cost when an order changes or is rejected.

Consider a hypothetical embroidered panel sold for a known price. That figure alone does not reveal the maker’s earnings. Material costs, unpaid preparation, delivery arrangements and the division of payment would all need to be established. An attractive retail price tag cannot substitute for that information.

Make room for the maker’s account

A consent-based interview should let the maker decide whether to be named, photographed or represented through her work alone. It should also distinguish her words from a retailer’s description. A family connection may be part of her identity, but it need not be the headline of her professional story.

The resulting account can recognise pleasure, ambition and resourcefulness without hiding difficult conditions. It can also acknowledge that someone may choose another livelihood. Preserving embroidery should not mean asking its makers to accept any terms offered in the name of tradition. The craft’s cultural value and the worker’s economic choices belong in the same conversation.
