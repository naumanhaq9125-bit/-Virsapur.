Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

Thar’s sacred and fortified places

Gori’s Jain temple, Naukot and Umerkot reveal different sacred and fortified landscapes within Thar rather than one uniform desert culture.

Thar is often represented through a single visual vocabulary of sand, colour and remote monuments. Its built heritage is more varied. Gori’s Jain temple, Naukot Fort and Umerkot Fort differ in function, material and political setting. They can be compared within a wider region without being made to tell one uniform story about desert life.

Pakistan’s Nagarparkar Cultural Landscape submission places Jain temples within a network of settlements, religious spaces and water features. It is on UNESCO’s Tentative List, not the World Heritage List. Its landscape approach is useful because a temple’s significance extends beyond an isolated carved doorway. Approaches, associated structures and relationships between settlements also matter.

A sacred interior organised in stages

Gori’s official inventory describes a sequence of pillared spaces leading towards the shrine, with stone construction and marble details. It also records an inscription associated with repair in 1715. The repair date is particularly revealing: the temple had a history of maintenance, not only an initial moment of construction. A surviving sacred building can preserve changes within a religious tradition as well as evidence of its original commission.

Reading the temple through its successive spaces avoids reducing it to decoration. A threshold, a column and a covered chamber participate in the experience of approach. Their religious significance should be explained through appropriate Jain sources and community knowledge, rather than guessed from their resemblance to features in another tradition. Shared materials do not make dedications interchangeable.

Fortification and residence

Naukot’s catalogue presents a different architectural arrangement: a brick enclosure with a defended entrance, associated with the Talpur Mirs and used as both stronghold and residence. Its walls organise a protected interior, whereas the temple’s sequence organises a sacred approach. Both use enclosure, but enclosure serves different purposes in each setting.

Umerkot’s record recounts successive political possession and describes a substantial rectangular fort with corner bastions. The important point for comparison is not that every surviving wall belongs to its earliest attributed ruler. Reoccupation and repair can change a fort while preserving its strategic or commemorative identity. A dynasty name alone cannot date all of its masonry.

These forts should not automatically be included within the boundaries of the Nagarparkar Tentative List property. A regional article and an international designation have different scopes. The wider Thar context makes comparison possible; it does not create a single protected ensemble out of every place discussed.

Difference within a region

The contrast between temple and fort helps resist two simplifications: that the desert was empty except for a few monuments, or that all its communities shared one unchanged culture. Buildings imply different networks of devotion, authority, labour and settlement. Their histories also extend beyond the people whose names appear in catalogues.

A thoughtful reading of Thar therefore preserves distinctions while exploring connections. Gori asks about sacred space and continuing care; Naukot about the relationship between residence and defence; Umerkot about successive claims to a stronghold. Together they offer a region with several histories, not a single picturesque desert past.
