Peshawar Museum: how a collection frames Gandhara

Peshawar Museum’s Gandhara collections are more than a sequence of beautiful sculptures. Their acquisition histories, reserve holdings and scholarly study shape what visitors can learn from them.

A museum does not simply gather objects; it gives them relationships. Placing sculptures together can make gestures, materials and narrative scenes easier to compare. It can also separate those objects from the buildings and deposits in which they were found. Peshawar Museum offers a useful setting in which to think about both possibilities.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums records the museum’s opening in 1907 in the Victoria Memorial building. Its holdings extend beyond Gandhara sculpture to other categories including coins, manuscripts, weapons and dress. Gandhara is central to the museum’s public identity, but it should not erase the range of historical material held there.

The collection behind the display

Tahir Saeed’s 2023 study focuses on a preaching Buddha image in the reserve collection associated with the former Sub-Regional Office at Peshawar. The article explains that this collection includes material received through several routes, including excavations, exploration, gifts, acquisitions and confiscations. These are not equivalent forms of provenance.

An excavated sculpture may be connected to a recorded architectural context. An object seized from illicit circulation can retain artistic and historical importance while lacking that context. The distinction is not a ranking of beauty. It affects the questions a researcher can answer securely and the uncertainties a label should communicate.

Saeed also traces changes in institutional custody, including the transfer of relevant federal holdings to the provincial directorate after the Eighteenth Amendment. The history of an object does not stop when it enters a museum. Registration, custody, storage and renewed study become further parts of its documented life.

Looking at a gesture

The article examines the Buddha’s preaching gesture, known as dharmacakra mudra, and uses formal comparison to discuss the sculpture. Such close study gives the viewer something more precise to notice than a general impression of serenity. The position of hands, treatment of clothing and relation of figure to support can become evidence.

Comparison nevertheless has limits. Similar appearance does not by itself prove a shared workshop or findspot. An interpretation should distinguish what is visible on the object from what is inferred through comparison. That distinction becomes especially important where excavation information is missing.

What the gallery cannot show all at once

A published study of a reserve object also reminds us that the visible gallery is not the complete collection. Storage is not necessarily a sign that an object is unimportant; research, conservation and display planning involve different requirements. Conversely, publication does not establish that a particular sculpture is currently on public view.

The most rewarding museum visit can therefore combine attentive looking with attention to the label. What is the object called? Where was it found? How certain is its date, and how did it enter the collection? Peshawar Museum’s significance grows when these questions accompany admiration. They restore some of the relationships that a case or pedestal cannot communicate by appearance alone.
