Sacred sound is not always music for an audience

The same sound can be heard as worship, recitation or performance. Heritage listening begins by asking how participants understand it, not by assigning every voice to a concert category.

A visitor hears a trained voice, a repeated phrase or an organised melody and may immediately call it a performance. That description can be useful, but it may also place the visitor at the centre of an activity that is not being undertaken primarily for an audience. The people involved may understand the sound through worship, recitation, learning or service.

This is not an argument against appreciating musical skill. It is an invitation to identify the purpose and terms of the particular practice before turning it into a heritage soundtrack.

The words are part of the action

The SGPC’s Sikh Rehat Maryada describes kirtan through the singing of scriptural compositions and sets out expectations for its congregational setting. That institutional account makes the relationship to scripture explicit. The melody cannot be understood fully as an independent decorative layer.

For a visitor at a gurdwara such as Panja Sahib, this means that listening should not begin with a request for a favourite song or an assumption that the gathering operates like a concert. Current guidance from the institution determines how a visitor may observe or record. The code is a religious source, not a timetable for the site.

The distinction also affects cataloguing. Identifying a recording only as “traditional music” can hide the text and the purpose that participants consider central. Where the composition can be reliably identified, that information belongs beside the performers and the occasion.

A recording changes the encounter

The Folkways description of Meraj Ahmed’s qawwali distinguishes shrine-related performance from commissioned gatherings. Even within one broad tradition, setting affects the organisation of the music. A commercially available recording may preserve significant artistry while offering only a partial view of the live relationship.

Zulfiqar Ali Qureshi’s study of Shah Latif’s vaaee examines both shrine practice and mass media. The comparison suggests another question: what happens when a recurring devotional practice is presented to listeners who encounter it through broadcasting rather than participation at its established setting?

The answer need not be that one form is authentic and the other false. The useful account identifies the change in medium and the people responsible for the version being heard.

Listen without assigning a private experience

A listener can describe a response from the group, a change of pace or a return to particular words. They cannot know from sound alone whether an individual is experiencing consolation, concentration, grief or something else. Those meanings require testimony, and testimony remains the account of its speaker.

At a khanqah, shrine or gurdwara, silence and waiting may also matter. A recording that removes every interval can make an activity seem more continuous or more theatrical than it was. Good documentation notes editing rather than presenting the result as an unaltered encounter.

Sacred sound can be aesthetically powerful without existing for a visitor’s enjoyment. Respectful listening allows those facts to coexist. It attends to the text, the setting and the participants, and accepts that some moments are better understood by being present than by taking a recording away.
