Recognising a bodhisattva through attributes

Identifying a bodhisattva depends on combinations of attributes and context, especially when hands or other diagnostic details are missing.

A halo alone does not give a sculpture its name. Neither does jewellery, an elaborate hairstyle or a serene expression. Identifying a bodhisattva involves a combination of features, the relationship to other figures and the scholarship attached to a particular work. The process is most revealing when the catalogue explains the reasoning rather than offering only a confident title.

The University of Missouri's Museum of Art and Archaeology presents a schist Maitreya from Gandhara, numbered 84.66. Its explanation draws attention to the flask held in the figure's left hand as an identifying attribute. The museum associates Maitreya with the future appearance of a Buddha. Here the named identity is connected to a specific surviving feature, not to a general impression of spirituality.

When the attribute is missing

The University of Sydney's study of the so-called year 5 schist Buddha triad presents a more qualified case. It identifies the figure to the Buddha's right as most likely Maitreya, discussing the hairstyle and a missing hand that probably held a water pot. The difference between a surviving vessel and a reconstructed one is crucial. Both can participate in interpretation, but not with identical evidential force.

The same study discusses the figure on the opposite side through another attribute: a small Buddha represented in the headdress. This illustrates why a careful description should preserve the actual arrangement. “Two attendants” may be adequate for an initial orientation, but it loses the details through which a more specific identification is argued.

A reader does not need to memorise an entire iconographic dictionary before looking. Start by noticing what is visibly present and then compare the catalogue's explanation. If the interpretation relies on a damaged area, ask whether it is supported by a surviving trace, a close comparison or an older record. These are productive questions rather than challenges to the value of specialist knowledge.

An identification is not a universal rule

A single example should not become an automatic recognition test applied to every sculpture. Regional traditions, periods and changing scholarly interpretations can complicate a seemingly simple association. A feature that supports an identification in one context may be insufficient in another. The relevant comparison is not merely any image with a similar silhouette.

Digital presentation can help by pairing an overview with a clearly identified detail. Cropping to the hand or headdress should leave readers a route back to the complete composition. Otherwise the attribute becomes detached from the very context that gives it significance.

Recognising a bodhisattva is consequently less like solving a visual password than following an argument. A strong account distinguishes observation, comparison and conclusion. It can name Maitreya where the evidence supports that identification, retain “probably” where it matters, and leave an unresolved figure unnamed. This makes the encounter more precise without reducing the work to a checklist of symbols.
