Bhera and the memory of a river town

Bhera’s river setting, altered gates and repeatedly rebuilt mosque show why a town’s age cannot be read from a single origin story.

Bhera’s history is often compressed into a claim about exceptional antiquity. A more revealing starting point is its position beside the Jhelum and the different kinds of movement associated with it. Rivers, roads, gates and markets do not all preserve the same chronology. Their relationship can explain a town without requiring every visible building to belong to its earliest remembered phase.

The federal inventory places Bhera on the river’s left bank and describes routes associated with salt traffic branching towards Lahore and Multan. It also repeats an identification with the capital of Sophytus, a contemporary of Alexander. The first is a geographical and route-history proposition; the second is a much more demanding ancient identification. It should not be treated as established merely because it appears beside a modern place name in a catalogue.

Gates that remember rebuilding

The same record describes an eight-gated walled town associated with Sher Shah Suri and notes substantial nineteenth-century reconstruction of gateways. That combination matters. A gate can preserve the memory and location of an older approach while containing later fabric. Calling it old does not answer which part is old, when it was repaired or how its surrounding street changed.

Rather than seeking a completely untouched town, look for differences between the passage, its façade and the buildings that now meet it. A rebuilt arch may retain a route even where an earlier wall has disappeared. Conversely, an impressive façade does not prove the survival of a complete defensive system. These are questions to investigate through plans and dated photographs, not conclusions available from appearance alone.

A mosque with more than one date

Jame Masjid’s inventory associates its foundation with Sher Shah and reports a 1541 date on the entrance. It separately records rebuilding under Qazi Ahmed-ud-din Bugvi in 1858 and repair under Qazi Zahoor Ahmed Bugvi in 1926. The account also preserves the name of mason Muhammad Asif. These details turn a simple foundation story into a history of continued investment and skilled work.

The present building therefore cannot be described responsibly as an unchanged sixteenth-century object. Its domes, halls, painted plaster and institutional spaces need to be understood through their individual phases. The association with an early patron remains meaningful, but it is not a date stamp applied to every wall. Later rebuilding is part of the monument’s significance rather than an embarrassment to be omitted.

Bhera’s river-town identity emerges from this layering. Routes can persist while gateways change; a religious institution can endure through major reconstruction; an ancient name can remain debated while later history is well documented. These are not competing versions from which one must choose the most romantic. They describe different kinds of continuity. Keeping them distinct allows the town’s memory to remain expansive without making its evidence appear more certain than it is.
