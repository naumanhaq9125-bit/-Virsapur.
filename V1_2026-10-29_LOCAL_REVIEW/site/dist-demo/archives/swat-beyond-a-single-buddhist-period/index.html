Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

Swat beyond a single Buddhist period

Swat’s archaeology includes urban settlement, Buddhist precincts, early Islamic buildings and later timber architecture, with different chronologies at each site.

Swat’s Buddhist monuments are central to its archaeological importance, but they are not the whole of its history. Settlements, production areas, mosques and later timber buildings belong to the same valley without belonging to one period. A fuller picture emerges by comparing sites and phases rather than arranging the region around a single cultural high point followed by disappearance.

The Khyber PATH research project at Ca’ Foscari describes Barikot through ten broad occupation phases extending from about 1200 BCE into the pre-modern period. Its account distinguishes the acropolis from the lower city and bases its sequence on archaeological contexts and radiocarbon evidence. This is a settlement history as well as a history of religious monuments.

Different parts of a town can change differently

At Barikot, the project describes changes in the use of defensive walls and the eventual abandonment of the ancient lower city. Such changes should not be converted into a claim that all life in Swat ended at the same moment. A hilltop, a town below it and neighbouring settlements can follow different trajectories. Regional history needs those differences rather than a single date imposed across the valley.

Butkara-I offers a different archaeological focus. Its inventory describes a central stupa surrounded by smaller votive structures and associated buildings, with a paved setting and finds now represented in the Swat Museum. A religious precinct concentrates evidence for worship and patronage. It cannot by itself stand for every domestic activity or social group in the surrounding landscape.

Early Islamic evidence is archaeological too

The Swat Museum project’s account of Udegram discusses an inscription recording a mosque foundation in 1048–49 CE, associated with the Ghaznavid commander Nushtegin. Excavation revealed the mosque and neighbouring settlement. This is a material and epigraphic history of early Islamic occupation, not simply a later chapter added after the archaeological story has ended.

The same account places the mosque on an earlier terrace with older masonry. Reuse of a location does not mean every earlier structure remained active, nor does it demonstrate that religious change happened uniformly across the valley. It shows why a site’s physical sequence is indispensable: a later building may occupy a landscape already shaped by centuries of construction.

Kalam’s wooden mosque introduces another scale and material tradition. The official catalogue describes a timber structure with a central chamber, veranda and carved geometric and floral decoration. It belongs to a later inhabited setting, not to the same archaeological phase as Butkara or the same building campaign as Udegram.

Swat is therefore not best understood as a succession of sealed cultural compartments. Places were occupied, altered, abandoned, revisited and maintained in different ways. Reading Barikot, Butkara, Udegram and Kalam together keeps both change and continued inhabitation in view. It also gives the valley’s later architectural traditions their own significance, rather than treating them merely as a background surrounding a more important ancient past.
