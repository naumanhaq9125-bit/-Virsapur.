Architecture & Built Heritage

Historic Bhera beyond a single gate

Bhera’s heritage is an urban network of gates, streets and reused buildings. Understanding that network is more useful than reducing the town to one landmark or a disputed gate count.

Bhera is best understood as an urban ensemble rather than a collection of unrelated monuments. Gates, streets, houses and religious buildings form a network, and the meaning of any one element depends partly on the others. A gate photographed without the street behind it becomes a facade. Seen in its urban setting, it is also an entrance, a route marker and a point where the town’s edges have changed.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums places Bhera on the left bank of the Jhelum and describes its association with routes toward Lahore and Multan. Its account also notes substantial rebuilding of gates in the nineteenth century. That matters because the historical foundation of a walled town cannot be used as the date of every structure visible there today.

Why counting gates is not enough

Different inventories do not always count the same things. The national catalogue refers to eight gates, while the Urban Unit’s 2022 heritage plan lists nine, including an Inner Lahore Gate. The difference should be explained through the scope and naming of each inventory rather than silently converted into a single unquestioned total. An inner gateway and an outer entrance may belong to different levels of an urban system.

The Urban Unit records several nineteenth-century dates and distinguishes surviving, altered and lost gateways. Those categories are more informative than a simple checklist. A missing gate can remain significant in a street name or route, while a standing gate may contain substantial later work. Neither is adequately described by the binary labels original and destroyed.

The same report records a former gurdwara used as an imambargah, with local testimony about its condition around Partition. This is evidence of changing use, not permission to assume that every feature belongs to the building’s first function. The account should retain the difference between the physical record and what a caretaker recalled hearing from earlier generations.

The city between the monuments

Urban heritage includes the connections between celebrated buildings. Street width, the alignment of an entrance and the scale of adjoining construction influence how a place is experienced. A conservation project focused only on isolated fronts can miss these relationships. Equally, a plan that treats present residents and businesses as visual clutter misses the fact that the town continues to function.

Punjab’s 2025–26 tourism programme listed an unapproved proposal for heritage conservation in Bhera. That planning record signals institutional interest, but it does not establish a completed transformation. Any account of subsequent work needs its own dated evidence.

For an archive or a careful walk, Bhera suggests a sequence rather than a single destination: establish an entrance, follow a street, notice a change of use and return to the wider plan. Its appeal lies in the accumulated relationships of a living town. A decisive photograph of one gate can introduce that story, but it should not be allowed to stand in for the whole.
