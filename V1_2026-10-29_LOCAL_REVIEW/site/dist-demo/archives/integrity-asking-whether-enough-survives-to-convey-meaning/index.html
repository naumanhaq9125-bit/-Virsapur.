Integrity: asking whether enough survives to convey meaning

Integrity concerns the survival and relationships needed to express a place’s significance; it is not a synonym for untouched or perfectly preserved.

A monument may retain a striking facade while losing the relationships that explain it. Conversely, a ruin can retain enough evidence to communicate an important history without looking complete. Integrity helps describe this problem. It directs attention to the survival, extent and condition of the elements needed to convey a place’s significance rather than asking only whether the most photogenic building remains standing.

UNESCO’s discussion of integrity distinguishes the presence of necessary elements from their condition and exposure to threats. The enquiry is therefore both spatial and material. Does the identified property contain the features required to explain its significance? Are those features sufficiently intact, and what processes may undermine them? A boundary and a condition report answer different parts of the same enquiry.

Enough of what, for which story?

Consider an imaginary water-powered workshop. If the significance being explained concerns a production system, preserving a machine but removing its water channel may leave a serious gap. If the claim concerns the design of a particular machine, the assessment may focus differently. The example shows why integrity cannot be judged without first stating what the place is significant for.

The question is not whether every trace of every period survives. Archaeological places are often fragmentary. What matters is whether the surviving evidence can support the particular argument being made. A glossy reconstruction cannot automatically repair a loss of evidence, just as weathered masonry does not automatically mean that all meaningful relationships have been destroyed.

UNESCO’s Taxila account illustrates why threats may extend beyond the immediate appearance of a ruin. Its integrity discussion identifies environmental pressures and activities in the surrounding valley as relevant to the property. The lesson for a general reader is not to diagnose those pressures from a photograph. It is to recognise that assessment needs a broader field of evidence than a single well-framed image.

Different from authenticity

Integrity and authenticity can overlap in discussion, but they ask different questions. A surviving feature may be credibly documented while the wider system to which it belonged is severely reduced. Another place may retain a broad arrangement but contain poorly explained replacement work. An account should describe those conditions separately rather than compress them into the vague claim that a site is well preserved.

For Virsapur, a useful integrity description should identify the relevant elements, explain the scale of the property and cite the assessment being summarised. It should not become an unsupported condition score. Readers gain more from knowing what survives and why it matters than from receiving an unexplained badge of completeness.
