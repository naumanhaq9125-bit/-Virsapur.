Pahari and Pothwari: names, varieties and self-identification

Pahari and Pothwari labels connect linguistic varieties, places and self-identification; careful description leaves room for those meanings to differ.

A language name can identify speech, place, ancestry or a relationship with other speakers. Sometimes it does several of these things at once. Pahari and Pothwari are therefore best approached through the people and varieties being described, rather than as labels whose meanings can be settled by choosing one spelling.

This is not a reason to avoid description. It is a reason to make description more precise: who uses the name, for which variety, in what context?

Naming is part of the evidence

Farah B. Nazir’s 2014 thesis on Potwari discusses the use of Pahari, Potwari and Mirpuri among speakers and in research. Her study focuses on a community in Leeds with migration connections to Mirpur. She explains the terminology chosen for that study while acknowledging the variety of names speakers themselves employ.

The point is not that every label means exactly the same thing. Nor does one researcher’s choice settle the preferred name for every community. The account shows that language naming has a social history, including the circumstances of migration and the audiences to whom speakers explain themselves.

An archive can preserve this complexity by recording a contributor’s own term alongside a broader catalogue category. The catalogue can then help people find related material without silently replacing the name used in the recording.

Study a variety closely

Awais Hussain’s 2024 research on negation identifies its principal data as the Chakswari variety and its migrant community. It examines how negative markers function within sentences. This is a tightly defined linguistic inquiry, not an attempt to make every speaker fit a single uniform standard.

That local focus is a strength. A grammatical claim becomes more useful when readers know which speech it describes. The fact that a feature appears in one study does not establish that every related variety uses it in precisely the same way.

It also illustrates why a discussion of language and dialect cannot be settled by prestige. A variety does not need a large publishing industry to possess systematic grammar. Conversely, similarity between neighbouring forms of speech does not determine how their speakers must identify politically or culturally.

Avoid making a map do too much

A place-name can help orient a reader, but it should not be converted automatically into an exclusive language boundary. People move, families maintain connections across places and speakers may use different names in different settings. A migration destination can become a significant site of literary or linguistic activity in its own right.

For a public heritage record, uncertainty should be specific rather than evasive. “The speaker calls this variety Pahari” conveys useful information. “The terminology is disputed” is less useful unless the competing usages and their contexts are explained.

The aim is not to invent a compromise label that everybody must accept. It is to make related materials discoverable while respecting the particular names, voices and research scopes they contain. Pahari and Pothwari then become openings into a complex linguistic world, rather than a naming argument that prevents readers from encountering it.
