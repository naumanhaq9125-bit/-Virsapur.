Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Taxila is eighteen places, not one stop

Taxila’s eighteen mapped components tell connected but different stories. Settlements, monasteries and later remains should be read together without turning them into one timeless city.

Taxila is a name large enough to hide several different places. A visitor may use it for the museum, a particular monastery, an ancient city or the modern town. In heritage terms, it is a serial property: separate archaeological components are recognised together because their combined evidence tells a larger history. Virsapur’s atlas keeps eighteen component locations within that single canonical property.

UNESCO’s description includes a cave, early settlement sites, Buddhist religious complexes and later Muslim remains. The result is not one frozen moment of civilisation. It is a landscape in which settlement, patronage and religious use changed over a long period. Keeping the components distinct is the first step toward understanding their connections.

Different cities, different questions

Saraikala, Bhir, Sirkap and Sirsukh represent different parts of the settlement sequence. They should not be imagined as neighbourhoods constructed at the same time around a single centre. A comparison between them is historical: it concerns changing forms of settlement and the evidence preserved at each location.

At Sirkap, the planned street arrangement and the placement of buildings along it offer questions about urban organisation. The museum’s dedicated account places the city within a sequence of political and cultural changes. That specificity matters more than assigning the entire Taxila landscape one adjective such as Greek, Buddhist or Kushan. Those labels refer to different kinds of connection and different periods.

Monasteries are not smaller cities

Mohra Moradu provides another scale of evidence. Its documented religious architecture belongs to the monastic landscape, with a different organisation and purpose from a settlement street. Comparing a monastery with a city can illuminate relationships between religious institutions and surrounding populations, but resemblance in masonry does not make their functions identical.

The museum helps reconnect objects with these settings. A sculptural panel is more informative when its recorded provenance is retained than when it is presented simply as an example of Gandharan art. The object’s style, findspot and original architectural context are related questions, not substitutes for one another. Where a context is uncertain, that uncertainty should remain visible.

Reading the whole without losing the parts

A useful approach to Taxila is therefore to select contrasting components and ask what each contributes. One may clarify a settlement plan, another a religious arrangement, another a later phase of the valley’s occupation. The point is not to collect eighteen identical stops. It is to understand why a dispersed group can tell a story that no single monument could carry.

On a map, the components remain separate points; in interpretation, they remain connected evidence. That balance prevents two common mistakes: reducing Taxila to one famous monastery, and dissolving the individual sites into an undifferentiated ancient city. The serial property is strongest when its diversity stays legible.
