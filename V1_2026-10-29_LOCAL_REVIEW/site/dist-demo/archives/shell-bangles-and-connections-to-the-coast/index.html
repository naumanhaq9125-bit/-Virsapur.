Shell bangles and connections to the coast

Shell bangles connect inland archaeological finds to marine materials, but workshop waste is needed to understand where and how they were made.

A pale curved fragment from an inland settlement may preserve a connection to the sea. Yet the connection is not a complete itinerary. A shell bangle can demonstrate the use of marine material without revealing whether a whole shell, a partly worked ring or a finished ornament travelled to the place where it was found. Those alternatives imply different distributions of labour.

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer's study of Indus shell industries identifies several species used in ornament production. It also explains a limitation that is easy to overlook: a small finished fragment may have lost the anatomical features needed for a confident species identification. The surviving object can therefore be recognisably shell while its more precise biological attribution remains uncertain.

Looking at what was cut away

The study reconstructs bangle manufacture using working traces and discarded pieces. Saw cuts, shell anatomy and unfinished forms help distinguish stages that a polished ring can conceal. Kenoyer's research at Balakot adds a coastal production context to the broader comparison. Together these investigations encourage us to treat manufacturing waste as evidence, not merely as the unattractive remainder of a collection.

Suppose a hypothetical inland excavation yields only finished bangles. It would be reasonable to ask how the ornaments arrived, but premature to announce a local bangle factory. If another context contains consistently related blanks and cutting waste, the production question changes. The importance lies in the combination of finds and their recorded setting, not in the simple number of attractive objects recovered.

This distinction also changes how we imagine exchange. A map line from coast to city is a summary of a relationship, not necessarily a direct journey by one merchant. Material could have changed hands, form and value along the way. Unless a particular route is independently documented, an illustration should leave the intermediate stages open rather than give them invented certainty.

Ornament does not explain its wearer

A bangle's shape invites comparison with objects worn today. Comparison can make an unfamiliar fragment intelligible, but it cannot by itself establish an ancient wearer's marital status, community or ritual obligations. Contemporary meanings belong to particular people and settings. They should not be projected backwards simply because both objects encircle a wrist.

For an exhibition, a useful arrangement might place an accurately identified finished fragment beside a documented working piece and a diagram of the relevant shell anatomy. The purpose would not be to reconstruct a universal Indus workshop, but to make one supported manufacturing argument understandable. Each object would retain its own location, period and collection identifier.

Shell bangles thus open two different histories. One concerns the movement of material between marine environments and inland settlements. The other concerns skilled transformation: understanding an irregular natural form well enough to cut a desired object from it. Keeping both in view gives the fragment more depth than either an isolated jewellery label or a sweeping claim about ancient trade can provide.
