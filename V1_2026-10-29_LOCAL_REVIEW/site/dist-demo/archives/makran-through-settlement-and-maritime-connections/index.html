Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

Makran through settlement and maritime connections

Makran’s archaeology links inland oasis settlement with coastal resources and wider exchange, without requiring one continuous ancient sea route.

Makran’s heritage is often approached from the sea. Its archaeological history also depends on inland valleys and oases. Settlement near Turbat, sites in the Dasht landscape and later buildings at Gwadar belong to different periods and systems of connection. Bringing them into one regional account is useful only if those differences remain visible.

The French Archaeological Mission in Makran investigated this relationship through survey and excavation in collaboration with Pakistan’s Department of Archaeology and Museums. Its published institutional overview places Miri Qalat and Shahi-Tump at the centre of a regional sequence reaching back into the fifth millennium BCE. The project combined settlement archaeology with studies of materials, environment and coastal resource use.

An oasis has its own history

At Shahi-Tump, the mission distinguishes built occupation from later cemetery phases. Its account discusses pottery, metalworking and ornaments in relation to wider regional connections. These are not simply exotic objects proving that traders passed through. Their archaeological contexts help reconstruct how people lived, made things and treated the dead within an inland setting.

The difference between a settlement and a cemetery is crucial. Material from a grave belongs to a particular act of deposition; material in a floor or workshop has another relationship to activity. Combining everything under a single site date can conceal changes in use. Makran’s significance lies partly in the sequences that make such distinctions possible.

Coast and hinterland are related, not interchangeable

The federal inventory places Sutkagen-Dor beside the Dasht river plain on a sandstone outcrop. That location invites questions about approaches, water and regional movement. It does not, by itself, demonstrate where the shoreline lay during a particular occupation phase or prove that the site functioned as a harbour. Palaeoenvironmental evidence and excavated material are needed to establish those propositions.

The mission’s wider research identifies connections with the Iranian plateau, the Indus region, Central Asia and Oman. Such connections need not represent one unchanging route. Objects, techniques and raw materials can move through several linked exchanges. A similarity between distant assemblages is evidence to investigate, not a complete itinerary for an individual traveller.

A much later maritime chapter

Gwadar’s Omani-period fort belongs to another historical framework. Its catalogue identifies a substantial rectangular building associated with Omani rule. It can introduce later political relationships across the sea, but it should not be used to imply direct continuity from a Bronze Age exchange network. The same coastal region can participate in very different systems centuries apart.

Makran becomes more interesting when inland settlement is not treated merely as a supporting corridor to a port. Oases, river plains and coastal communities each contribute evidence with their own chronology. A regional narrative can connect them while remaining precise about what is known. That produces a history of changing relationships between land and sea, rather than a single ancient route drawn confidently across a modern map.
