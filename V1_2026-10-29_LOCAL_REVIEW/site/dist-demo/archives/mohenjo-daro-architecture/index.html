Mohenjo Daro Architecture

Mohenjo Daro’s architecture is best read through materials, levels and connections—not through confident labels for buildings whose original names are unknown.

The architecture of Mohenjo Daro is not confined to one monumental building. It lies in the repeated joining of wall, opening, platform, stair and drain. Looking at these relationships offers a more useful introduction than assigning every large room a grand title. A building can be carefully planned and socially important even when its original name and function are unknown.

UNESCO identifies the site as a major surviving example of an early planned city, distinguished by brick construction and an extensive urban layout. This broad significance should not flatten the remains into a single moment. The visible city is an archaeological selection: excavated structures, surviving fragments and the interventions that allow them to remain exposed.

Reading height in low walls

Stairs are especially valuable because they point toward spaces that no longer survive in full. Harappa’s architectural documentation discusses upper courts and floors, while also showing how the interpretation of an impressive house can vary. A suggestion that a building was a palace or temple is not the same thing as an inscription identifying its occupants.

Think of each stair as a surviving connection rather than a complete answer. It establishes movement between levels; it does not by itself recover the roofing system, the activity upstairs or the authority of the person who used it. This distinction keeps reconstruction anchored to the structure rather than to modern expectations about ancient power.

Buildings changed their surroundings

A digitised archaeological account held by IGNCA describes a building whose earlier entrance was blocked, another opened, and later work raised above older surfaces. Access to a well subsequently required descending from a higher lane. The example is a reminder that relative levels are historical evidence. What seems awkward in a ruin may record adaptation to an altered setting rather than poor original design.

When looking at a plan, ask which elements actually belong together. A straight wall meeting another wall does not necessarily prove they were constructed simultaneously. An entrance can be cut after a room is built; a raised floor can change the experience of a doorway without moving its walls. Architecture includes such revisions, not just the first design.

Mohenjo Daro rewards this patient attention. Its importance is not dependent on recognising a royal residence or imagining a single architect directing the entire city. Materials and connections show how built space was organised; alterations show that it continued to be negotiated. The ruins are most revealing when treated as evidence of construction and change, with uncertainty made visible rather than concealed behind familiar labels.
