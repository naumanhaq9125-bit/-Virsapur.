An oral history is a relationship, not just a recording

An oral-history interview depends on consent, interpretation, preservation and return—not merely on capturing a clear recording.

A recorder can capture a voice without capturing the relationship that made the conversation possible. Oral history begins before the first question and continues after the file is saved. The narrator needs to understand the project, the interviewer needs to explain the proposed uses, and both need a realistic account of what will happen to the recording.

The Oral History Association’s best-practice guidance identifies preparation, interviewing, preservation and access as connected parts of the work. It calls for documented informed consent and, where possible, an opportunity for narrators to approve the oral history before public release. These are not merely administrative steps added after a compelling interview has been obtained.

Agree on the conversation’s future

An interview for a private research project and an interview intended for an unrestricted website create different expectations. A participant should not have to discover the difference after publication. Explaining where material may appear, who will hold it and what review is possible helps make the agreement meaningful.

Language adds another layer. A narrator may choose expressions whose significance is not fully carried by a short English translation. Keeping the original recording and documenting translation decisions allows future listeners to understand what has been changed. A polished quotation should not silently merge the narrator’s words with the editor’s explanation.

Memory also has its own character. An oral account can reveal how a person understands an experience, including emphasis, uncertainty and later reflection. It should not automatically be treated as a precise chronological register. Where a historical claim requires corroboration, other evidence can be consulted without dismissing the narrator’s perspective as worthless.

Returning something useful

The recording’s destination should be planned, not improvised once storage becomes inconvenient. The Oral History Association advises selecting a repository capable of preserving the material and respecting the agreed conditions. For a small local project, that principle raises practical questions about copies, file formats, contact details and who will manage access if the original interviewer leaves.

Returning a recording or transcript can also allow participants to use the work themselves. A file that exists only in an inaccessible institutional account may preserve information while failing to serve the people who contributed it. The form of return should be discussed rather than assumed: a digital link is not useful to everyone in the same way.

For heritage storytelling, the most valuable interview is not necessarily the one that produces the most dramatic extract. It is one conducted through a clear and respectful process, with its context and conditions retained. The resulting record can then support future understanding without reducing the person who spoke to a source of quotations.
