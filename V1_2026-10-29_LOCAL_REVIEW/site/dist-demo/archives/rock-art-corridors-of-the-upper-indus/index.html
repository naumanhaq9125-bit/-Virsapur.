Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

Rock-art corridors of the upper Indus

Upper Indus rock art records many acts of marking across a landscape. Inscriptions and images illuminate movement without making every maker a foreign traveller.

Rock art beside the upper Indus can make movement visible without drawing a complete route on a map. Images and inscriptions occur at particular surfaces, but their makers did not all leave the same kind of evidence. Some recorded names or religious acts; others left marks whose circumstances remain uncertain. A corridor is therefore a useful way to think about connections, not a single explanation for everything engraved along it.

Many kinds of encounter

Jason Neelis’s research presentation places inscriptions and rock art within networks of movement through the upper Indus region. It distinguishes major routes from smaller connecting paths and considers both local inhabitants and people passing through. This avoids the familiar shortcut in which every unusual image becomes the work of a distant traveller.

The written evidence includes several scripts and languages. Identifying a script can help frame a question about chronology or communication, but it does not automatically identify the birthplace of its writer. Someone could use a learned language without having travelled from the place most associated with it.

Images require a similar caution. A stupa drawing, an animal and an inscription on neighbouring rock may belong to different moments. Physical proximity invites comparison; it does not establish that all were made by one group or as a single composition.

Reading is an argument

Thomas Höllmann’s study of Chinese inscriptions illustrates the interpretive work involved. His discussion of a Haldeikish inscription proceeds from photographs and considers readings of names, titles and a reference to Wei. The name of a dynasty is not enough by itself to provide an uncontested date, because more than one historical polity used that designation.

This is a useful model for an archive caption. It should separate what can be seen, what a specialist reads and what that reading may imply. A proposed identification can be interesting without being presented as certainty. Translation should not erase the difficulty of the original evidence.

Sites, not an undifferentiated gallery

The official catalogue records locations including Hodar, Thalpan and the Sor Das group in Rondu. Keeping these names and geographic relationships distinct helps readers understand the distribution of evidence. Combining their images into a single decorative montage would conceal the individual surfaces and settings that make them informative.

The history of research also deserves precision. Karl Jettmar’s introduction acknowledges earlier knowledge of rock carvings before the systematic investigations begun in 1979. A modern research project should not be described as discovering a landscape that local people and earlier observers already knew.

For public interpretation, the strongest invitation is to look closely without intervening. Marks should not be traced, wetted or altered to make a photograph clearer. More importantly, the archive should preserve uncertainty in its language. Upper Indus rock art is compelling because it records many encounters across time; reducing those encounters to one effortless Silk Road story would make the evidence less, not more, remarkable.
