Bilot Sharif Temples and the stone-building tradition

Bilot Sharif’s temples belong to a regional stone-building tradition whose chronology cannot be reduced to one dynasty. Their materials, arrangements and changing scholarly labels reward careful comparison.

Bilot Sharif’s stone temples invite a closer look than the familiar label ancient ruins allows. The Department of Archaeology and Museums records temple groups within a fortified setting and describes the use of dressed stone and kanjur, a comparatively workable sedimentary material. Material and arrangement offer a route into the buildings even when a complete historical narrative is unavailable.

A visitor can begin with straightforward questions. How does the entrance relate to the chamber? Which surfaces carry architectural mouldings? Where does one kind of masonry meet another? These observations are more useful than assigning every visible element to the same ruler simply because a regional dynasty is well known.

A regional tradition, not one repeated design

Michael Meister’s work on temples along the Indus places Bilot within a broader architectural discussion. The recurring forms matter, but so do differences between buildings. Comparison is most productive when it identifies a particular feature rather than treating all temples in the region as interchangeable examples.

A separate excavation report by Meister, Abdur Rehman and Farid Khan provides an instructive comparison from north Kafirkot. There, investigation distinguished earlier temple construction from later enlargement. The authors describe changes from kanjur elements to limestone additions, modifications around an entrance and evidence for more than one building phase. They also argue against assigning the entire regional tradition exclusively to the Hindu Shahi period.

Those findings belong to the excavated northern site. They should not be copied into Bilot’s record as if the same trench had been opened here. Their value is methodological: they demonstrate why standing appearance and a single dynastic label may conceal a more complicated sequence.

Reading stone without overreading it

A change in masonry can indicate an alteration, but it does not identify the person who ordered it. A blocked opening may reflect repair or changed use; its purpose requires contextual evidence. Architectural interpretation becomes stronger when it keeps these steps separate: observation, comparison, proposed explanation and supporting evidence.

The names and letters applied to individual structures also require care. An archaeological catalogue may group a selection of buildings without providing a complete inventory of every surviving feature. Before comparing plans from different publications, the reader should check that their labels refer to the same structure. Otherwise, an apparent disagreement may simply be a mismatch in naming.

More than a skyline of towers

Photography can help make these distinctions visible. A distant view establishes the ensemble, while an oblique view may reveal an entrance or a change in wall treatment. Close details should remain connected to the building they belong to. An isolated ornamental fragment is visually appealing but tells little about spatial organisation on its own.

Bilot’s importance lies in the opportunity to read architecture as evidence of making and remaking. The temples deserve individual identities within a shared tradition. That approach preserves their complexity and makes comparison with other Indus monuments meaningful, without turning a regional resemblance into an invented common history.
