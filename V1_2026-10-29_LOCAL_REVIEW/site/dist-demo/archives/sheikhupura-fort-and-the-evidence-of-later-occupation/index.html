Sheikhupura Fort and the evidence of later occupation

Sheikhupura Fort contains more than a Mughal origin story. Later residences, painted interiors and political imprisonment are integral to its architectural history.

Sheikhupura Fort is often introduced through Jahangir, but its architecture cannot be understood by stopping at a Mughal foundation. Later residential buildings, painted rooms and changes in political use are part of the site’s substance. To remove those chapters from the account would be to make the fort historically simpler than the surviving evidence allows.

The archaeological catalogue associates the main gateway and enclosure with the Mughal period while assigning much of the internal construction to later Sikh occupation. It records a residence associated with one of Ranjit Singh’s queens and floral frescoes within an upper room. The exact attribution of that room is presented cautiously in the source and should remain so. A plausible identification is not the same as an inscribed one.

An enclosure with several interiors

The fort’s outer wall establishes a strong boundary, but the buildings inside were not necessarily conceived as a single project. A useful architectural reading asks how one structure meets another. Does a wall continue uninterrupted? Has an opening been blocked? Does a staircase serve one building or link several? Such relationships can help reconstruct change without assigning every surface to a dynasty on appearance alone.

Conservation architect Saba Samee’s presentation for the Commonwealth Heritage Forum emphasises the site’s range of architectural and decorative material, including frescoes and carved timber. Her account treats the fort as a layered place spanning several periods. This is an important alternative to selecting one imagined golden moment and making all later work disappear from view.

Painted interiors are especially vulnerable to being detached from their context in public presentation. A floral panel may be beautiful, but its position within a residence helps explain how it was experienced. The room’s scale, its openings and its relation to neighbouring spaces should accompany the detail whenever possible.

Residence and confinement

The fort is also associated with the imprisonment of Maharani Jind Kaur during the nineteenth-century struggle over power in Punjab. This chapter changes the emotional register of the place. A royal building could serve as a setting for residence and later as an instrument of confinement. Those uses should not be collapsed into a picturesque story of courtly life.

It is equally important not to assign a particular surviving room as her prison without specific evidence. Historical association can be secure at the scale of a site while remaining uncertain at the scale of an individual doorway or chamber. Good interpretation explains that difference instead of manufacturing a more dramatic visitor route.

Sheikhupura Fort therefore calls for a form of conservation that preserves evidence of change. Its value is not only in the oldest masonry or the finest painted surface, but in the relationships between them. A public account that follows those relationships can show how architecture outlives its first patrons, acquires new meanings and carries histories that are not all celebratory.
