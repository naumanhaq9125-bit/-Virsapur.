Great Bath of Mohenjo-Daro

The Great Bath combines precise masonry with an unresolved social purpose. Its steps, ledges and waterproofing explain more than an easy comparison with a modern swimming pool.

The Great Bath is one of Mohenjo Daro’s most recognisable spaces, yet recognition can be misleading. Its name encourages an immediate comparison with a swimming pool. The archaeological evidence offers something more particular: a carefully constructed tank, controlled approaches, surrounding spaces and a demanding effort to keep water where its builders wanted it.

Harappa’s archaeological description gives approximate dimensions of twelve metres by seven, with a depth of about 2.4 metres. Staircases descend from opposite ends. A ledge beside the water allowed movement near the foot of the steps without immediately entering the deeper tank. These details make the structure intelligible as an arrangement for bodies and movement, not just a rectangle seen from above.

Keeping water in, letting it out

The construction used closely fitted brickwork with gypsum plaster and a bitumen layer for additional waterproofing. The distinction between ordinary masonry and a watertight enclosure is important. A wall can remain standing while leaking badly; containing water demands attention to joints and surfaces as well as strength.

The published archaeological descriptions also identify an outlet connected to a substantial drain. Filling and emptying belong to the same problem of use. A tank that could only be filled would be a different kind of installation from one whose contents could be discharged. The material evidence for managing water is firmer than any reconstruction of the exact ceremony that may have taken place around it.

A nearby well has been proposed as a supply source, but the Harappa account notes that no inlet drains have been found. It is therefore better to describe possible supply arrangements than to invent a complete hydraulic circuit. Even an exceptionally well-known monument can retain such basic uncertainties.

What happened around the tank?

Rooms and colonnaded areas formed a larger architectural setting. Their presence encourages questions about preparation, waiting, supervision and movement between spaces. Those questions are useful precisely because they are not already answered by the modern name. A plan shows where a doorway lies; it does not tell us who was permitted to cross it or what words were spoken there.

Ritual bathing is a common scholarly interpretation of the Great Bath. It should remain an interpretation, rather than becoming a claim that a particular later religious practice has been identified in the third millennium BCE. Similar use of water in different periods does not by itself establish direct continuity of belief.

Seen within the wider system studied by Michael Jansen, the tank also belongs to a city with many installations for obtaining, using and disposing of water. Its monumentality should not isolate it from those more ordinary provisions. The Great Bath brings technical skill and questions of collective life together. Its surviving fabric tells us a great deal about making a water-filled space, while leaving the social meaning of that space open to further evidence.
