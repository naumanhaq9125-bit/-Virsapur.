Khojak: a pass transformed by a tunnel

Khojak’s tunnel changed how a mountain barrier was crossed, while archival photographs preserve a different view of the engineering project.

A pass and a tunnel solve the problem of crossing a mountain in different ways. At Khojak, the railway did not simply improve a path over the ridge; it established a new relationship between the line’s approaches, the rock through which it passed and the settlements connected by it. The tunnel is best understood as part of that system rather than as a length record.

The federal catalogue places it on the Quetta–Chaman railway between Shelabagh and Sanzala and records completion in 1891. At roughly 3.9 kilometres, it is a substantial engineering work, but its historical significance is not dependent on whether it occupies a particular place in a changing list of longest tunnels.

Crossing beneath the barrier

The distinction between a road over a pass and a railway through a tunnel concerns more than distance. A railway requires an alignment suited to its operating conditions, together with approaches and supporting infrastructure. The tunnel entrance is therefore only the most visible part of a larger decision about movement through the landscape.

The historical account reproduced by the Indian Railways Fan Club Association discusses the project in relation to the Chaman extension. Read alongside the catalogue, it places the tunnel within a particular late nineteenth-century railway undertaking. It should not be used to imply that the line crossed into Afghanistan or that every earlier proposal was completed.

An entrance in the photographic record

The British Library holds a photograph catalogued as the entrance to the Khojak tunnel, attributed to Frederick Bremner and dated to the 1890s. Its collection context is also recorded: it survives within the Jones collection rather than as an anonymous illustration detached from provenance.

A separate reproduced Bremner image is identified as construction work on the Shelabagh side in 1889. Together, the records indicate that the project was photographed during and after construction. Their catalogue dates and descriptions are evidence, although close visual comparison requires examination of the images themselves.

What the engineering view leaves open

Such photographs can help document changing fabric, but they cannot provide a complete social history. The organisation of labour, working conditions and supply arrangements require records beyond an entrance view. Dramatic stories attached to the tunnel should likewise be checked against contemporary evidence before being repeated as fact.

The same distinction applies to present-day discovery. Historical railway significance is not a statement about current train services, permission to enter railway property or safe access to a tunnel. Those are separate, time-sensitive questions.

Khojak rewards attention to the relationship between visible and hidden infrastructure. The portal gives the railway an architectural face, while the passage through the mountain and its approaches explain the transformation. Reading the tunnel through dated records and its wider alignment makes it a specific historical project, not merely a picturesque opening in a hillside.
