Punjabi across more than one script

Shahmukhi and Gurmukhi make Punjabi visible through different writing systems; reading across them involves more than changing a font.

Two readers may understand the same spoken Punjabi sentence yet find each other’s books difficult to read. Language and script answer different questions. The language concerns vocabulary, grammar and ways of speaking; the script supplies a visible system for writing them. Punjabi’s association with both Shahmukhi and Gurmukhi makes that distinction especially important.

A change of script is therefore not necessarily a translation into another language. At the same time, reading across scripts is not merely a matter of replacing a typeface. The characters, conventions and decisions about representing sound have to be understood.

A small letter can reveal a large problem

A 2019 Unicode proposal by Lorna Priest Evans and M.G.Abbas Malik addressed the representation of a Punjabi retroflex lateral sound in Arabic-script writing. It compared the relevant Gurmukhi character with proposed Shahmukhi representation and discussed a publisher’s workaround. The proposal also presented historical manuscript evidence rather than relying only on a newly designed symbol.

The example is useful because it shows several kinds of work meeting on a printed page: a speaker distinguishes a sound; an editor decides how to write it; a font must display the chosen character; and software must preserve it as text. A page may look correct to a casual observer while still failing one of those requirements.

It would be misleading to turn this single proposal into a claim that Punjabi had no writing tradition before digital standardisation. The problem concerns particular representation and technical support, not the existence or legitimacy of the language.

Crossing the script barrier

Research by Saini, Lehal and Kalra on Shahmukhi-to-Gurmukhi conversion describes the use of a language corpus and statistical information to address text without diacritical marks. That is an important clue: conversion can require informed choices, not a simple one-to-one character substitution.

Punjabi University’s 2007–2008 report also lists work on transliteration tools and a Gurmukhi/Shahmukhi presentation of the Puratan Janam Sakhi. These are documented attempts to widen access across writing systems. They do not establish that every automated conversion is accurate, or that readers using either script necessarily share identical literary interests.

For an archive, the practical implication is to retain the source text alongside a clearly identified converted version. If an editor resolves an ambiguous word, that decision should not be hidden inside an apparently mechanical output. A competent reader of the destination script needs to review the result.

More than a choice between two labels

A script field can help a reader find material, but it should not substitute for information about the work itself. Author, edition, place of publication and language variety remain relevant. Two books in the same script can differ profoundly, while versions in different scripts may preserve a close textual relationship.

The reward of reading across Punjabi’s scripts is not the elimination of difference. It is access to a wider literary conversation. Treating both systems as serious ways of writing, while making the process of conversion transparent, allows that conversation to expand without pretending that typography alone can do all the work.
