Khewra Salt Mines

Khewra is both a geological exposure and a working industrial landscape. Its underground rooms reveal extraction, structural judgement and the labour behind an everyday material.

A visit to Khewra begins with salt, but the mine is also a place to think about work. The chambers are spaces made by removing a material that is ordinarily encountered in a packet, a kitchen or a market. Seeing that material still embedded in the earth changes its scale: an everyday commodity becomes a geological body, an industrial resource and an engineered interior.

The Jhelum district administration locates the mine in Pind Dadan Khan tehsil, at Khewra in Punjab’s Salt Range. The Pakistan Mineral Development Corporation identifies it as one of its operating salt mines. These two facts are a useful foundation. Khewra is not simply a decorative underground attraction, nor is the accessible visitor area the whole of the working mine.

Leaving material behind

PMDC describes the extraction system as room and pillar. In this method, the space opened for extraction is understood alongside material deliberately left to support the workings. The apparently empty chamber and the surviving mass are parts of the same engineering decision. Removing more is not automatically better: the relationship between opening and support is central to reading the interior.

The operator records several salt seams and colours ranging from pale and transparent material to pink and red. Those variations are part of the physical deposit, but colour alone does not establish a product’s purity, origin or health effects. The most useful on-site observation is how bands and masses appear in the exposed workings, not what a souvenir label promises.

A history larger than a discovery story

The district’s tourist account repeats the familiar story of Alexander’s horses licking salt-bearing stones. It is a memorable piece of local narration, but that page does not supply contemporary evidence establishing the event. The history of a resource should not depend on a single dramatic moment of supposed discovery. Knowledge held by local populations, changing extraction practices and commercial organisation are different questions requiring their own records.

Khewra also belongs to a wider regional geography. PUNJMIN’s account of rock salt identifies other mining locations in the Salt Range, including Warcha and Kalabagh. This context prevents one famous mine from standing in for every deposit and every working practice in the region. Shared material does not imply identical industrial histories.

Reading an industrial interior

For the heritage observer, distinguish the exposed geology, the excavated workings and later visitor installations. A carved display may explain a material or celebrate a landmark without being an ancient feature. A tunnel’s scale may reflect extraction and transport requirements rather than the proportions of a public room.

Most importantly, remember that the mine was made legible through human labour and continuing maintenance. Its heritage lies not only in attractive colours but in the relationship between material, judgement and work. That perspective gives Khewra a place in cultural history alongside forts and temples: it records how people have transformed the ground beneath them into a resource and a built environment.
