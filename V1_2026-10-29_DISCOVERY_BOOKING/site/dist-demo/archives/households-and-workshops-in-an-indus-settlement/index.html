Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Households and workshops in an Indus settlement

Craft debris helps identify production, but it does not automatically reveal a factory. Chanhudaro offers a starting point for distinguishing work processes from assumptions about labour.

A finished bead is evidence of an accomplished object. A partly worked stone, a drill or a scatter of production waste can answer a different question: how was the object made? For an Indus settlement, distinguishing those questions is essential before a cluster of finds is confidently labelled a workshop.

Reconstruct a process, not a modern workplace

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer's study of Indus stone beads places Chanhudaro's bead workshop within the history of archaeological research. It also explains why recording should consider material, shaping, drilling, surface treatment, wear and later alteration. Classification by appearance alone can conceal differences in how an object reached its surviving state.

The implications extend beyond beads. A production sequence has stages, and different stages leave different traces. Finding material from one stage does not establish that every other stage occurred in the same room. Nor does a concentration of finished products necessarily identify a place of manufacture; it could prompt questions about storage, exchange or later disposal.

Consider the distinction between a room's last contents and its full history. A space used for work might later be cleared or filled with rubbish. A household might undertake a specialised task alongside other activities. These are analytical alternatives to investigate, not descriptions that can be assigned to every Indus house.

Why comparison needs restraint

Ethnoarchaeological work on bead-making at Khambhat provides a way to study the relationship between activities and the traces they leave. Such comparison is useful because it asks what waste, tools and working arrangements look like together. It does not establish that ancient workers had identical households, employment relationships or social identities.

Calling a site a factory can import assumptions about permanent employees, central management and separated domestic space. Each assumption needs its own evidence. A more careful account can identify specialised production while leaving the organisation of labour open where the archaeological record does not decide it.

For visitors reading a plan of Harappa or Chanhudaro, this changes the emphasis. Instead of looking only for an impressive room, ask how the excavation linked particular finds with particular surfaces and phases. Were objects recorded together, or assembled later under a broad site name? Does the interpretation concern making, using or discarding?

The most informative workshop story is not necessarily the grandest. It may describe a single operation clearly, distinguish debris from finished goods and explain why the relationship with neighbouring domestic space remains uncertain. That is a richer account of skilled labour than turning every concentration of craft material into the same modern industrial scene.
