Sacred Heritage

Religious heritage and the politics of naming

A sacred place can carry several names without having several identities. Recording aliases carefully can improve discovery while preserving religious affiliation and historical distinctions.

A place name is not merely a label attached after the history has been written. It can preserve a religious relationship, a personal name, a local pronunciation or the priorities of a later catalogue. Choosing one spelling for a map is useful; treating that choice as the only legitimate identity of the place is not.

The problem becomes especially visible when a religious building is described through a broad category that obscures its affiliation. Calling every non-Muslim sacred structure a temple may make a list shorter, but it makes the history less precise.

One person, more than one name

The ASU Asian Art History record identifies the Gujranwala memorial associated with Atmaramji as a shrine of Acharya Vijayanandsuri. It explains that the two names refer to the same Jain religious figure. This is a useful case of an alias making a connection rather than creating a second monument.

A searchable archive can retain Atmaramji as a familiar name while also recording Vijayanandsuri and the term samadhi. The relationship should be explained explicitly. Otherwise, readers may assume that a shrine, a samadhi and a memorial under another personal name are three different places.

The ASU account also distinguishes the figure’s Jain institutional history. That specificity matters more than forcing the monument into a generic architectural category. Similarities with other memorial buildings do not make its religious affiliation interchangeable.

Names can outlive a use

A building may continue to be known by its former sacred name after another use begins. Conversely, a later administrative name can become more familiar locally than an older dedication. Neither circumstance permits the archive to erase the other period.

The SOAS survey of Jain heritage demonstrates the importance of documenting institutions and funerary monuments as well as major temples. Its research scope provides a reason to look beyond familiar catalogue labels, not a licence to relabel a building without evidence.

At Gori, the established temple name provides an entry point into a specific Jain complex. A transliteration variant should be linked as an alternative spelling where verified, not treated as a fresh place simply because a search engine returns it separately.

Preserve the source of each name

A practical record can distinguish a current institutional name, a historical name and a locally reported alias. It should state where each came from and avoid turning an unverified suggestion into the main title. Recording the original script, when securely read, can reduce confusion introduced by transliteration.

Honorifics deserve similar care. They can be meaningful to a community without functioning as a separate personal name. Removing them everywhere may flatten the source; including every variant without explanation may make the record difficult to understand.

Naming also affects whose history becomes discoverable. A reader searching for Jain heritage should be able to find a Jain memorial even if an older inventory used an imprecise category. The solution is a transparent correction with retained provenance, not a silent replacement that makes the earlier description impossible to trace.

Good names do two things at once: they help people find the place and help them understand why it has been known in different ways.
