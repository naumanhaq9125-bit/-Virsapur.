Swat’s terraces and archaeological visibility

Barikot’s slopes and terraces reveal why what archaeology can see depends on reuse, erosion and where excavation takes place.

A terrace in an archaeological landscape is not automatically a field, and a hilltop is not a single-period monument. At Barikot in Swat, slopes, platforms and successive buildings show how the visibility of the past depends on reuse as well as survival. The most conspicuous remains may occupy a surface created for an earlier purpose.

Luca Maria Olivieri’s study of Barikot’s Shahi landscape describes a Hindu temple built on a monumental terrace previously associated with a Buddhist sacred area. The important point is not simply that one religious phase followed another. The material setting itself was reused and altered, creating relationships that only careful excavation can distinguish.

Height is not a date

It is tempting to read an elevated structure as the oldest or most important element of a site. Archaeology does not permit that shortcut. A high platform can be a later intervention, while earlier remains survive beneath or beside it. Equally, material found lower on a slope may have moved from above rather than marking an undisturbed occupation.

Ca’ Foscari’s account of work at Barikot distinguishes excavation in the lower town from investigation of the acropolis. It describes complex stratigraphy on the steep slope and notes earlier material incorporated into later fills. That distinction helps explain why an artefact’s age is not necessarily the date of the deposit in which it is recovered.

Architecture connects levels

Elisa Iori’s research on Buddhist buildings at Barikot examines the relationship between lower open courts and raised, formerly roofed platforms. Steps connected spaces with different arrangements and uses. The elevation was therefore part of architectural organisation, not just a response to scenery.

Such evidence encourages a more precise way of looking at a ruin. Rather than imagining an entire lost building from one wall, begin with the relationships that survive: a threshold, a stair, a court and the level to which it leads. These can support interpretations of movement even when roofs and upper walls are gone.

Excavation shapes what becomes visible

An archaeological plan is also a record of where work has taken place. Unexcavated ground should not be interpreted as empty ancient space, while an exposed area may bring together remains from several phases for study. The viewer needs a phased explanation to understand which features actually belonged together.

This is especially important in a landscape as visually powerful as Swat. The green valley and prominent hills can make the setting seem immediately comprehensible, while the chronology remains complex. A scenic panorama is a useful introduction, not a substitute for the excavation record.

Barikot’s terraces consequently offer more than attractive vantage points. They show how people built upon earlier surfaces and how researchers separate those interventions. Reading the site through levels, connections and documented phases makes its visible remains richer without pretending that the whole past is already exposed to view.
