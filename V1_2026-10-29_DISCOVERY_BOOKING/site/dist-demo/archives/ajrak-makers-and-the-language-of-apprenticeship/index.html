Ajrak makers and the language of apprenticeship

Ajrak teaching moves between workshops and classrooms. Its continuity depends on recognising makers as teachers, with knowledge that exceeds a printed pattern.

An ajrak can be photographed in a moment; learning how to make one is a different undertaking. The finished cloth reveals pattern and colour, but not every decision that produced them. For an archive concerned with living heritage, the relationship between maker and learner deserves attention alongside the textile itself. A pattern catalogue cannot stand in for a teacher.

The maker as educator

In a small qualitative study published in 2021, education researcher Aruj Khaliq interviewed people involved in teaching craft heritage in Pakistan. One participant, Sindh artisan Muhammad Ghulam Abbas, described invitations to teach ajrak in educational institutions. The account places a practising maker in the role of an educator, rather than treating craft expertise merely as material for someone else’s lesson.

Khaliq’s discussion also considers learning through sustained guidance and the connection between craft and other subjects. It is a study of selected perspectives, not a national survey demonstrating how every ajrak workshop trains its apprentices. That distinction leaves room for different teaching arrangements and for artisans who do not work with formal institutions.

A British Council programme guide offers another specific example: it identifies Ghulam Abbas Soomro as a master block printer from Bhit Shah and describes engagement with textile and fashion education. This is evidence of a named practitioner’s educational work. It should not be expanded into a claim that every student encounter provides a full apprenticeship.

Demonstration is only a beginning

Consider the difference between watching a printed impression and learning to judge it. In a hypothetical teaching exercise, a learner might compare two attempts, explain where the repeat fails, and try again with feedback. The educational value lies partly in being able to recognise and discuss a mistake. A successful souvenir print, by itself, tells us much less about what was learned.

An archive interview could therefore ask who taught a maker, which tasks a beginner is allowed to attempt, and how competence is recognised. These are proposed questions, not answers supplied on behalf of practitioners. Local terms should be recorded with their speaker and context instead of being presented as a universal vocabulary for all Sindh workshops.

Recording without replacing

Noorjehan Bilgrami’s documented work includes the book Sindh jo Ajrak and the film Sun, Fire, River, Ajrak. Their existence shows that recording the craft has taken several forms. Neither a book citation nor a film title, however, authorises an archive to reproduce a maker’s designs or disclose workshop knowledge without agreement.

Good documentation should distinguish the teacher, the workshop, the person recording the lesson and the person making the object. It should also ask what may be shared publicly. A maker can welcome visitors while declining to reveal a technique or have a learner photographed. Respecting those choices keeps apprenticeship a relationship between people, not simply an attractive story attached to cloth.
