Mohenjo Daro Residential Lanes

Mohenjo Daro’s lanes reveal a city that was planned, used and altered. Thresholds and blocked drains tell a more intricate story than the familiar grid diagram.

A city plan makes Mohenjo Daro look orderly from above. A lane introduces a different scale: a doorway only a few steps away, a wall that interrupts a view, a narrow passage between larger buildings. These spaces are not incidental gaps left over after monuments were built. They are the places where movement between buildings becomes visible in the archaeological record.

Harappa’s documentation of the DK-G area describes streets and buildings aligned broadly north–south and east–west, but also notes adjustments as new construction appeared. In the photographed example, a corbelled covering over a drain was eventually obstructed as adjoining streets accumulated material. The grid was therefore not a timeless diagram. Its elements belonged to successive acts of construction, use and alteration.

Looking at the entrance

An educational reconstruction preserved in the Australian National University’s Basham Collection presents rooms around a courtyard and entrances facing a lane rather than the principal street. It is useful for imagining a relationship between outside movement and an interior household space. It is not a measured record of every house, and its sweeping description should not be mistaken for proof that all residents lived alike.

Instead, treat the entrance as a question. Does it face a broad route or a smaller passage? Is the threshold at the same level as the surrounding surface? Can someone entering see directly into the next space? Such observations help explain how a building organised movement without requiring an invented account of the family who occupied it.

The missing vertical city

The low walls visible today can encourage an entirely horizontal reading. Documented stairways remind us that movement also ran upward. Kenoyer’s archaeological captions discuss access to upper courts or floors in particular buildings. That evidence adds height to the streetscape, but does not justify completing every missing roof or assigning each room a fixed purpose.

The most useful mental reconstruction is consequently selective. Imagine an upward route where a stair supports it; recognise an entrance where an opening is documented; distinguish a surviving surface from a modern path. Leave the unsupported details unfilled rather than supplying a complete theatrical scene.

Mohenjo Daro’s lanes offer an alternative to the image of a perfectly controlled ancient city. Their interest lies in the tension between a recognisable framework and local change. A blocked drain, a shifted wall or an altered approach can matter as much as a straight street. Read at walking pace, the city becomes a record of decisions made over time, not merely an early demonstration of the ruler and the right angle.
