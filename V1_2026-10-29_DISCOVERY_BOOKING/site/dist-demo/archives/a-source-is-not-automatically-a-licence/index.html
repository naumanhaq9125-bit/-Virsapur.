A source is not automatically a licence

Crediting a photograph identifies its source; permission to reuse it requires a separate rights check and compliance with the applicable terms.

A heritage photograph may be easy to find and difficult to reuse responsibly. It can appear on a museum website, in a government catalogue or on a public social-media account without offering a licence for another organisation’s publication. A source link tells readers where the image was found. It does not, by itself, establish permission to copy it.

The distinction is especially important when a photograph documents a historic building. The building’s age and the photograph’s rights are different questions. A modern image of an ancient object should not automatically be treated as an ancient image. Likewise, a public institution’s role as custodian does not mean that every photograph on its website carries identical reuse terms.

Read the terms attached to the actual file

Creative Commons provides several licences with different conditions. Its guidance distinguishes attribution, sharing adaptations under specified terms, non-commercial restrictions and limits on distributing adaptations. Seeing a CC label is therefore only the beginning of the check. The exact licence and version need to be identified, together with the person or organisation making the rights statement.

For example, the CC BY 4.0 deed permits sharing and adaptation under its stated terms, including credit, a licence link and an indication of changes. It also warns that the licence may not supply every permission required for a proposed use. This article does not resolve privacy, personality rights or other legal issues for any particular photograph; those may require separate advice.

An editorial record should keep the creator’s name, the original file page, the selected licence and the date of verification. It should also record changes such as resizing or cropping. A downloaded filename alone is a poor rights record because it can become separated from the page that explained the conditions.

Availability is not clearance

Consider an image copied into a research folder while a writer investigates a site. Its presence there means it was available for reference, not that it passed a publication check. If the rights remain unknown, the publishing system should preserve that uncertainty rather than infer approval from the file’s existence.

The same applies to a credit that says only image courtesy of the internet. It neither identifies a creator nor explains the basis for reuse. A properly credited but unlicensed copy can still present a problem; a licensed image with missing required credit can also fail its conditions.

For a cultural platform, a restrained no-image treatment is preferable to an undocumented assumption. Rights work is not separate from the institution’s respect for culture. It recognises photographers and other creators as participants whose labour deserves the same care that the platform gives to the subjects they depict.
