Transliteration is not translation

Changing a script is not the same as conveying meaning in another language. Manuscript titles and catalogue records make the distinction visible.

A heritage label can show an unfamiliar name in familiar letters without explaining what the name means. That operation is transliteration: representing writing through another script. Translation addresses meaning in another language. The two often appear together, which makes them easy to confuse, but they solve different problems for a reader.

The Library of Congress maintains ALA-LC romanization tables for numerous writing systems, including Urdu. These are structured conventions for rendering non-Roman writing in Roman letters. Their existence also explains why an academic catalogue may spell a name differently from a tourist sign: a particular system is doing specific work, rather than merely decorating the word with unusual marks.

One manuscript, several descriptions

Consider the Library of Congress record for a manuscript of Amir Khusraw Dihlavi’s Khamsah, held by the Walters Art Museum. The record identifies Persian as the language and nastaʿliq as the writing style. It also explains Khamsah as a quintet. The Roman-letter title and the English explanation perform different tasks; neither converts the manuscript itself into an English text.

The catalogue cautiously associates this copy with late-sixteenth-century Lahore. Its description of a particular manuscript should not be confused with the date of the author’s composition. Here, too, preserving the distinctions between a work, a copy and an English catalogue entry prevents an apparently small label from carrying a large historical error.

Sound is a third question

Pronunciation introduces another layer. A system designed to represent letters consistently is not necessarily a pronunciation guide for an English-speaking visitor. The reader needs to know what its signs mean. Conversely, an informal spelling intended to help someone say a name may omit distinctions useful for finding the original written form.

This matters when searching archives. If a catalogue uses a formal romanization and a family history uses an everyday spelling, the two may refer to the same person or work. A search aid can connect those forms without claiming that one is a translation of the other. Keeping the source spelling visible makes the connection inspectable.

Translation requires additional decisions that an alphabet table cannot make. A title may carry a literal sense, a conventional English rendering and associations with a literary genre. A translator has to decide what an intended reader needs and where an explanation belongs. Replacing every culturally specific word with a short English equivalent can make a sentence fluent while obscuring the distinction the author wanted.

A well-prepared heritage entry therefore separates the original form, its chosen transliteration, a translation where appropriate and any pronunciation assistance. It names the system or translator when that information is available. This is not an exercise in making labels cumbersome. It lets a visitor know whether they are seeing the name, an attempt to say it, or an interpretation of its meaning—and follow the record back to its source.

  2. The Khamsah of Amir Khusraw Dihlavi, Walters W.624 — Library of Congress / Walters Art Museum (accessed 2026-10-22)
