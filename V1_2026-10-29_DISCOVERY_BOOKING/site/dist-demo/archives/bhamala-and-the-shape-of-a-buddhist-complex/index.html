Bhamala and the shape of a Buddhist complex

Bhamala’s cruciform stupa is the centre of a larger Buddhist complex. Excavation evidence connects its distinctive plan to chapels, monastic spaces and a history of continued use.

Bhamala's main stupa is memorable for its cruciform plan, but its shape becomes more meaningful when the surrounding complex is allowed into the picture. Smaller stupas, image chapels, monastic spaces and later excavation discoveries show that this was not one isolated monument. It was an organised setting for a Buddhist community whose use of the place developed over time.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums locates Bhamala in the hills near Khanpur and describes a square-based stupa with projecting stairways on four sides. The projections produce the characteristic cross-shaped plan. That geometry is more informative than comparisons with distant monuments that merely happen to look similar from above.

A centre with surrounding activities

The federal catalogue records smaller votive stupas and image chapels around the main court, with a monastic complex to the south. These elements establish relationships between a principal devotional focus and other spaces. Their distribution can help explain movement, proximity and differences in scale within the sacred precinct.

The plan should not be imagined as fully visible in one historical instant. Archaeological sites often contain structures from successive phases, and an exposed arrangement can bring those phases together in a way that no original visitor experienced. Establishing which walls and floors belong together requires recorded excavation, not visual symmetry alone.

What renewed excavation changed

Abdul Samad's report on the January–March 2013 field season describes work in the western part of the complex and an extension that drew attention to another phase of occupation. The team used systematic recording of deposits and relationships to begin refining the chronology. Training students and staff formed part of the programme alongside the recovery of objects.

The report records terracotta and stucco sculpture and copper coins provisionally associated with the fourth and fifth centuries CE. It uses these findings to argue for activity extending beyond an earlier, narrowly framed understanding of the site. The important point is not simply that more objects were found; the relationship of finds to deposits changed the historical questions that could be asked.

Form is evidence, not a complete explanation

A cruciform stupa can be compared with other Buddhist architectural forms, but shape alone does not identify every ritual performed there. Likewise, an image chapel provides evidence of a devotional setting without preserving all the words, gestures and people that animated it.

Pakistan submitted Bhamala as a separate Tentative List property in 2026. That nomination-stage recognition should be distinguished from World Heritage inscription, and geographical association with Taxila should not be used casually to redraw another property's boundaries.

For the visitor, a rewarding sequence is to understand the principal stupa's outline, then trace how the surrounding spaces relate to it. Finally, consider the excavation record that places those relationships in time. Bhamala's distinctive form is the beginning of discovery; the wider complex and its stratified evidence make that discovery historically substantial.
