Khem Singh Bedi Haveli: painted rooms and public memory

The painted rooms of Khem Singh Bedi Haveli bring architectural space and religious imagery together. Their interpretation requires care about both subject matter and changing condition.

Khem Singh Bedi Haveli at Kallar Syedan is distinguished by painted surfaces that belong to an architectural setting, not a detached gallery collection. The archaeological catalogue records frescoes across the facade, veranda pillars and interior rooms. It also describes courtyards, projecting windows and several levels. The paintings and the spaces that hold them need to be read together.

The house is associated with Khem Singh Bedi, the nineteenth-century Sikh religious and social figure. In a 2021 study, art historian Muhammad Asghar examined its paintings through field research and the interpretation of religious imagery. His published abstract identifies subjects connected with Sikh, Hindu and Islamic traditions. That is a more useful starting point than assuming that a house associated with one community contains only one visual tradition.

Images in domestic space

The position of a painting affects how it is encountered. A figure above an opening is seen differently from one at eye level, and a sequence across a veranda differs from an isolated panel in an enclosed room. An adequate record should therefore include the wall, the opening and the surrounding decoration, not only a tightly cropped figure.

Asghar interprets the imagery in terms of cultural syncretism and the patron’s religious outlook. That interpretation should be attributed to the researcher. It is not the same as a surviving statement by the patron explaining every painted subject. The distinction allows an engaging argument to remain open to discussion rather than hardening into a claim about someone’s private intentions.

Individual identifications also require care. Clothing, attributes, inscriptions and comparison may support a reading, but a confident social-media caption is not sufficient evidence. Where a figure has not been securely identified, describing what is visible can be more honest and more useful than naming a deity or ruler prematurely.

A record of condition has a date

The catalogue’s positive description of the paintings’ condition cannot be treated as a timeless assessment. The 2021 study’s abstract, by contrast, expresses concern about deterioration. These sources belong to different moments and may have assessed different aspects of the building. Their contrast is a reason to seek dated photographs and surveys, not to choose whichever description produces the more dramatic story.

Documentation can preserve information about a painting without physically preserving the painting itself. Both are needed. A photograph records a surface at one moment; conservation requires an understanding of the material, its support and the causes of change. Digital colour correction should not be mistaken for recovery of the original scheme.

The haveli’s public value lies partly in this encounter between image, room and historical interpretation. Its paintings offer evidence of a varied visual world, while its architecture shows how that world was placed within a home. Keeping the two together gives the site a depth that the label colourful mansion cannot convey, and makes careful, contextual recording an essential part of its future.
