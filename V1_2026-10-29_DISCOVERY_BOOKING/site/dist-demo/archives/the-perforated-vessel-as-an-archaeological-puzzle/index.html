The perforated vessel as an archaeological puzzle

Perforated Indus vessels have prompted several functional explanations. Their holes are clear evidence; a single universal purpose is not.

The holes are the first thing one notices. A perforated Indus vessel appears to announce that something was meant to pass through its walls. That observation is useful, but incomplete. What passed through, under which conditions, and in combination with what other equipment? A striking shape can generate a strong hypothesis without establishing a single answer.

In commentary accompanying a National Museum of Pakistan example, Jonathan Mark Kenoyer discusses straining, the preparation of beverages and dairy processing among the possibilities considered for different perforated vessels. He also points to residue research suggesting varied uses. The careful reading is not that archaeologists have discovered three interchangeable names for the same function, but that the objects need to be studied individually and comparatively.

The vessel around the vessel

Some published examples were associated with larger containers. That relationship is essential to understanding proposals involving straining. An isolated perforated object may not represent the complete apparatus. A reconstruction should therefore show which components were found together, which are supplied by comparison and which remain hypothetical. Otherwise a persuasive illustration can hide the weakest part of the argument.

Size also deserves attention. A small perforated form and a tall cylindrical jar may belong in the same broad visual family without performing exactly the same task. Grouping them for study is a starting point. It should not remove differences in their dimensions, openings, bases or recorded associations.

The National Museum in New Delhi's educational booklet invites readers to consider possible uses for a perforated jar. The wording preserves an important space for inquiry. A classroom can compare proposals without pretending that a successful guess has recovered the original owner's intention. The exercise becomes stronger when pupils explain which feature supports their answer and what further evidence would change it.

Testing a suggestion

For example, a hypothetical filtering proposal should address more than the presence of holes. Would the proposed material escape through them? Does the explanation require a lining or another container? Is there evidence for those additions in the relevant context? These are practical questions, not proof that any particular ancient vessel was a filter.

Likewise, a reconstruction that successfully performs a task demonstrates possibility rather than exclusivity. A container can work in more than one arrangement. Evidence of ancient use must come back into the discussion before a modern experiment is turned into a definitive label.

This is why the perforated vessel deserves more than a caption calling it an archaeological mystery. That phrase can imply that nothing useful is known. In reality, manufacture, form and context offer specific information, while function remains open to investigation. A good account identifies both. The holes cease to be a puzzle awaiting one dramatic solution and become an invitation to follow how archaeologists compare explanations, reject weak ones and retain uncertainty where the evidence genuinely requires it.
