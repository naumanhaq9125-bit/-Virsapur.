Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Akra: archaeology in the Bannu basin

Research at Akra places early settlement growth in the Bannu basin before Achaemenid expansion. Its mounds complicate histories that explain change only through outside empires.

Akra invites a change of starting point. Instead of asking first which empire reached the Bannu basin, we can ask what was happening there before imperial expansion. Archaeological research makes that question possible. The settlement is not merely a backdrop for a political history written elsewhere; its own sequence can test that history.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes a group of four mounds near Bharat village, southwest of Bannu. A group of mounds should not automatically be imagined as a single town occupied in exactly the same way throughout its existence. Differences between areas may matter as much as the outline of the whole site.

Growth before the imperial explanation

Peter Magee and his colleagues’ 2005 study reports results from three seasons of fieldwork ending in 2001. Their central conclusion is that settlement growth at Akra began early in the first millennium BCE, several centuries before Achaemenid expansion into the region. They also identify material connections extending toward Central Asia and peninsular India.

This does not mean that later imperial connections were irrelevant. It means that an empire cannot simply be assigned responsibility for processes already under way. The order of events matters. If settlement expansion precedes a proposed cause, the explanation needs revision rather than a more dramatic retelling.

The Bannu Archaeological Project’s later synthesis places Akra within a wider programme of fieldwork undertaken between 1985 and 2001. Its published description presents a long but discontinuous sequence extending from the first millennium BCE into the early second millennium CE. Discontinuous is an important qualification: a long overall date range is not evidence of uninterrupted occupation in every excavated area.

Reading connections without drawing an empire

An object resembling material from another region can raise several possibilities. It might have travelled, been copied locally or belong to a wider shared technical tradition. Determining which explanation fits requires attention to manufacture, composition and context. A line drawn between two points on a map is a useful question, not a complete account of exchange.

Akra’s value lies partly in this tension between regional development and long-distance relationships. Neither isolation nor total dependence is a satisfactory default. The basin can be understood as a setting where people made choices, adapted techniques and participated in connections whose significance changed over time.

A mound is more than a label

For an atlas reader, the most useful introduction is therefore not a single dynasty attached to a pin. It is a sequence of research questions: which part of the settlement was investigated, how was it dated, and what supports the interpretation of its material?

Those questions make Akra more interesting, not less accessible. They reveal a place capable of correcting an inherited story. Preserving the distinctions between surveyed ground, excavated deposits and historical interpretation allows the Bannu basin to emerge as an active part of South Asian history rather than an empty space between better-known centres.
