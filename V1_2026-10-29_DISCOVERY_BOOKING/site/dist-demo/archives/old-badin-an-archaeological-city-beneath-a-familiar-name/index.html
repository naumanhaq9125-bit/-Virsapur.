Old Badin: an archaeological city beneath a familiar name

The ruins of Old Badin require a careful distinction between documented survey evidence and broad claims about antiquity. Their value does not depend on assigning them a Bronze Age origin.

Old Badin is an archaeological place-name within a district whose modern name can make the distinction easy to miss. The ruins should not be treated as a synonym for the entire present town or for every ancient site in the surrounding area. Identifying the specific recorded remains is the first step toward understanding what is known about them.

The national archaeological catalogue describes an old town in ruins and notes pressure from modern construction. It also makes a broad claim connecting the place to the Mature Harappan period. That claim needs closer scrutiny. A primary survey summary published in Pakistan Archaeology records Old Badin as site 87 and assigns it to the medieval category. The table’s key defines that category as the eighth to eighteenth centuries.

What a survey actually establishes

The 1972 report summarises a southern Sindh survey carried out by the Department of Archaeology. Its classification is evidence about the material or historical assessment available to that survey. It is not a complete excavation sequence. A medieval surface classification cannot prove that no earlier deposits exist, but neither can an unsupported statement of great antiquity establish those deposits.

This distinction gives Old Badin a useful place in a public discussion of archaeology. A settlement can be important without belonging to the earliest period a writer can name. Later towns have their own histories of occupation, production, movement and change. Adding thousands of years to an account does not make it more meaningful if the evidence has not been demonstrated.

The ruins also appear in a protected-site list reproduced in a Sindh development project’s environmental documentation. That listing confirms their recognition as a heritage concern. It does not supply a detailed plan, a foundation date or a complete account of their destruction. Different kinds of records answer different questions.

A city beneath a familiar label

For further research, the most productive starting points would be the original survey documentation, a clearly defined site boundary and dated records of visible remains. Where modern building has affected an archaeological area, knowing exactly what was observed and when becomes particularly important. A general photograph of Badin cannot substitute for that record.

Historical names may also move between an older settlement and a later town. The persistence of a name is significant, but it does not guarantee that the occupied area remained unchanged. Establishing continuity requires a combination of maps, texts and material evidence rather than a shared label alone.

Old Badin’s story is therefore still a research question, but not an empty one. There is a documented archaeological identity, a survey classification and a recognised need for protection. Presenting those securely allows future findings to refine the account. It also gives readers something valuable now: an example of how a heritage archive can distinguish evidence from repetition while taking a less celebrated urban site seriously on its own terms.
