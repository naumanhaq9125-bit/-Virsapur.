Hinidan Tombs: recording a vulnerable monument group

Hinidan’s carved tombs show why an old inventory, a stylistic comparison and a current condition record must be kept separate.

Hinidan is a useful place to learn how heritage knowledge is made—and how easily old observations can be mistaken for current facts. Its decorated funerary monuments have appeared in archaeological writing for more than a century. Yet the existence of a long bibliography does not mean that the date, identity and present condition of every tomb have been settled.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes numerous carved graves and a varied vocabulary of headstones and raised forms. Some details are especially distinctive, including projecting elements compared in the catalogue to the handles of a bier and floral motifs on the stonework. These are features worth documenting carefully. They are not, on their own, proof of how a burial was conducted beneath the monument.

The dates do not neatly agree

The official listing assigns a broad medieval chronology, while older scholarship and subsequent discussion place particular Hinidan examples later. J. Ph. Vogel examined the cemetery for the Archaeological Survey of India in the early twentieth century. Later research preserved in the Ca’ Foscari University repository discusses examples attributed to the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, including carved weapons and riders.

These differences should remain visible rather than being silently resolved into one confident date. A style can persist; parts can be replaced; a cemetery can grow over generations. None of those possibilities should be asserted for a particular tomb without evidence, but all explain why a single site-wide date may be inadequate. A securely read inscription tied to a specific monument would carry a different evidential weight from a general comparison of form.

The historical accounts also preserve names attached to graves through local tradition. Such traditions belong in the record with their attribution intact. They should not be converted into verified genealogies merely because an early survey printed them.

Recording vulnerability without inventing a condition survey

An old description of good preservation cannot certify how the cemetery looks today. Conversely, the fact that a monument is exposed outdoors does not justify a claim that it is currently collapsing. A useful modern record would pair dated overall photographs with details of the same identifiable stones, allowing change to be assessed rather than guessed.

For a carved group such as Hinidan, the sequence of stones and their relationships are crucial. A detached panel photographed alone may preserve a motif but lose the evidence of where it belonged. Recording a tomb’s orientation, dimensions, inscriptions and position within the cemetery would make future comparisons much more reliable than collecting isolated close-ups.

Hinidan’s appeal is not simply its ornament. It reveals the distance between recognising a beautiful object and understanding a funerary landscape. An atlas can help bridge that distance by linking the place to its survey history, showing where dates remain contested and refusing to present an old inventory as a live inspection. That kind of precision gives the monuments more substance, not less mystery.
