Sacred Heritage

Jain heritage in Pakistan beyond one temple

Pakistan’s Jain heritage includes temples, commemorative structures and former community institutions. Recognising those differences prevents a diverse history from being reduced to one monument.

Gori Temple is an important point of entry into Pakistan’s Jain heritage, but it cannot stand for the entire subject. A temple, a memorial to a religious teacher and a former school preserve different kinds of history. Bringing them into one conversation should reveal their relationships without flattening their distinctions.

The SOAS exploratory survey was designed to document a much wider field than famous temple remains. Its research programme included built and funerary heritage, manuscripts, libraries and community institutions. That breadth matters because a religious community’s presence is not recorded only in the places where a visitor expects to find monumental worship.

Beyond the temple category

A published field report from the project distinguishes several forms, including temples, dadabaris, sthanakas and samadhis, alongside schools and other institutions. It also notes problems of identification. A building described casually as Hindu may require closer investigation before its Jain affiliation is recognised.

This is not an invitation to relabel structures from appearance alone. Rather, it shows why inscriptions, documentary records, community histories and architectural evidence should be considered together. An unfamiliar image or a changed use can complicate recognition. A modern sign may preserve a useful name without settling every question about the building’s earlier function.

At Gori, SOAS field documentation records the organisation of halls and surrounding cells, while the federal catalogue describes the mandapas and materials. These provide a more concrete starting point than a claim that all Jain temples share one appearance. The two accounts are evidence about this complex, not a template to impose on every site.

Commemoration is a different question

The archive also links readers to the Atmaramji Shrine record. Its inclusion alongside Gori should prompt a distinction between a temple complex and a place associated with religious commemoration. A full account requires secure identification of the person remembered and the institution’s history; similarity between names is not sufficient.

That caution is particularly important in searchable archives. The same or similar personal name can belong to people of different periods and traditions. An attractive biography found online cannot be attached to a monument merely because the spellings match. Until the connection is demonstrated, the narrower description is the more informative one.

Institutions after displacement

The SOAS project situates its work in a history affected by Partition and migration. For a heritage reader, this raises questions about how records and memories travel when communities move. A building may remain in Pakistan while relevant photographs, family papers or institutional recollections are held elsewhere.

Its later history also deserves attention. A change of use, a repair, or the work of a present caretaker can be documented without erasing the earlier affiliation. Equally, recognising that affiliation does not require inventing an uninterrupted ritual life.

A useful Jain heritage archive would therefore connect places with kinds of evidence: inscriptions with readings, photographs with dates, and institutional names with corroborating records. Gori remains compelling, but the larger story becomes visible only when the less conspicuous structures and records are allowed to matter too.
