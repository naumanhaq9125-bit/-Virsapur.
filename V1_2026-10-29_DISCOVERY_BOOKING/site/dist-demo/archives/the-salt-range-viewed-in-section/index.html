Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The Salt Range viewed in section

Geology, hilltop architecture and water give the Salt Range several distinct timescales that should not be compressed into one ancient landscape.

Seen in section, the Salt Range is a place where different kinds of time become visible together. Rocks belong to geological histories; a ridge acquires its present form through uplift and erosion; buildings occupy selected positions within that terrain. Calling the whole view ancient conceals the distinctions between those processes.

The Geological Society’s account explains the range through the continuing collision of the Indian plate with Asia. Sedimentary layers have been folded and uplifted, while salt at depth has helped layers detach and move relative to the underlying crust. This is not a claim that the hills and every rock within them are the same age.

Layers before landmarks

A 2026 United States Geological Survey data release addresses age constraints on the Salt Range Formation using samples from named localities. Its existence is a reminder that even a familiar geological label is subject to investigation. The date of a formation, the timing of mountain building and the age of a temple are not interchangeable measurements.

For heritage interpretation, this matters because stone is not simply a neutral pedestal. Terrain influences where an approach is possible and what can be seen from a site. Yet a dramatic hilltop position does not by itself explain why a particular building was founded there. Geological context can frame the question without replacing historical evidence.

Buildings occupy particular terrain

The federal catalogue places Nandana’s remains within a constrained hill setting and records several kinds of surviving structures. At Katas Raj, the ensemble includes temples, water and later buildings. These are different relationships with land: an elevated position and approach in one case, a group organised partly around a pool in the other. Neither can stand for every settlement or religious site in the range.

Malot offers another architectural focus within this regional comparison. Connecting it with Nandana and Katas on an atlas should invite a study of distinct sites, not imply a single construction campaign or identical religious history. Their shared landscape is a reason to compare them, not permission to erase their differences.

Three scales of looking

A useful first scale is the wider landform: ridge, slope, valley and water. The second is the site’s placement within it: approach, enclosure, orientation and relationship to nearby habitation. The third is the material detail of a particular wall or carved element. Moving between these scales helps avoid mistaking a close-up of stone for an explanation of the whole landscape.

It also suggests what a photograph cannot establish. Similar-looking stone need not come from the same quarry; a nearby cliff does not prove a building’s material was extracted there. Such connections require identification and evidence.

The Salt Range is compelling precisely because its histories do not fit one clock. Reading the landscape in section allows geological depth, architectural decisions and present settlement to coexist without turning them into an undifferentiated story of antiquity.
