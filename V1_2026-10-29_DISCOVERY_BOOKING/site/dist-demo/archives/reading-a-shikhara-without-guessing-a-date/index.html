Reading a shikhara without guessing a date

A shikhara belongs to an architectural composition, not a ready-made date label. Salt Range temple research shows the value of regional comparison and careful attention to surviving fabric.

The rising superstructure of a temple can dominate its appearance so completely that the rest of the building becomes a supporting detail. The term shikhara helps describe an important part of temple architecture, but it should not become a shortcut that assigns a date or dynasty from a silhouette alone.

A regional sequence, not an isolated shape

Michael W. Meister's study of temples along the Indus presents the Salt Range material through an integrated architectural and archaeological investigation. He identifies a distinctive regional tradition and examines its relationship with wider architectural forms. The argument depends on a corpus of buildings, not one tower recognised from a distance.

His discussion of Malot is especially useful for understanding comparison. The temple's relationship with architectural traditions in Kashmir is considered through specific formal features and historical context. That is more precise than treating every temple in the region as an interchangeable instance of a broad style.

Later scholarship on the Salt Range also discusses how earlier surveys and classifications shaped the field. Architectural terms have research histories. A label can be useful while still needing explanation about which features it groups together and which differences it leaves visible.

Read from the base to the top

A temple's plan, wall articulation, projections, mouldings and surviving superstructure contribute to its interpretation. Looking only at the highest element can conceal the evidence offered by lower parts of the building. A damaged top may also distort the impression of the original profile.

A careful description should identify what survives. Does the account concern intact masonry, fragments, an early photograph or a scholarly reconstruction? These sources can be combined, but their different status should not disappear when a drawing is reproduced.

Chronology requires a further step. A formal resemblance may support a comparison, yet resemblance alone does not establish the exact date of construction. Inscriptions, archaeological context and a reasoned regional sequence can strengthen or revise an attribution.

Avoid the timeless temple image

Sites such as Katas Raj, Malot and Harnoli should not be collapsed into one undated visual tradition simply because they are all described as temples. Their individual histories, conditions and available documentation differ. A heritage category is a starting point for discovery, not a conclusion about chronology.

The same care applies to religious continuity. A surviving form does not automatically establish that use was uninterrupted, nor does damage prove that all associations with the place ended. Architectural and community histories need their own evidence.

For a visitor, a useful exercise is to move attention away from the summit. Notice how the entrance, walls and base organise the building before returning to the tower. Then compare those observations with a published plan or study.

The shikhara becomes more informative when it is restored to the composition around it. Vocabulary should help readers see relationships and ask better questions, not supply an instant date that the building itself has not been shown to support.
