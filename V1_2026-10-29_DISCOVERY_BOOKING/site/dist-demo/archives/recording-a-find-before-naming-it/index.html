Recording a find before naming it

An attractive name is not the first piece of information a find needs. Position, association and an honest description allow later interpretations to be tested.

“Royal”, “ritual” and “ceremonial” are powerful words. Attached too quickly to an archaeological find, they can make an object memorable while hiding how little is known about its use. The first task is less glamorous and more valuable: preserve the information that will allow an interpretation to be tested.

Description before identification

An observation should be distinguishable from a proposed explanation. Material, shape, dimensions and visible traces can be described without first deciding that an object was an amulet or a tool. An interpretation can then be added with its supporting argument and its degree of confidence.

The National Park Service's explanation of archaeological context includes an object's relationships in space, time and association with other evidence. A find's importance therefore does not reside solely in its appearance. The place and circumstances of recovery can change the questions it is capable of answering.

Imagine a small perforated object. Without context, its opening might suggest suspension, attachment or another function. Recorded beside related material in a secure deposit, it may support a more specific argument. This is a hypothetical example of interpretation, not an identification of a particular Harappan artefact.

The record should survive a change of mind

Oxford's field guidance emphasises consistent identifiers and thorough documentation. The practical reason is that an object, its bag, its drawing and its written description need to remain connected. If a later specialist revises the identification, those connections should not be lost.

A good archive can therefore preserve both the earlier label and the revised one. The change becomes part of the research history. Quietly replacing a name without recording the basis of the revision makes it harder to understand why two publications describe the same object differently.

For readers, the most useful catalogue entry answers modest questions clearly: what was observed, where was it recovered, what is inferred and who made that inference? An elaborate title is less informative if those elements are absent.

This also explains why an object should not be removed by a visitor who thinks they have recognised something important. Moving it can destroy relationships that are difficult or impossible to reconstruct from memory. Recording and reporting concerns through the responsible site authority is different from collecting.

At Harappa and other archaeological places, the ordinary-looking fragment may be informative precisely because its context survives. The discipline of recording before naming does not diminish discovery. It gives discovery a lasting evidential value, beyond the excitement of the first attractive interpretation.
