Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Harappa after the excavation trench

Harappa’s archaeology continues after excavation through the study of objects, dates and recorded contexts. Small finds can reveal activities that monumental ruins alone cannot explain.

An excavation trench is the most visible symbol of archaeology, but it is not where all the important work happens. At Harappa, the interpretation of a small bone tool, a ceramic fragment or a dated deposit can change how an entire settlement is understood. The city survives not only in exposed architecture but also in records and collections that preserve relationships no longer visible on the ground.

The federal archaeological catalogue recalls a damaging episode in the site's modern history: ancient bricks were removed for railway construction in the nineteenth century. It also identifies the excavations begun in 1921. These are different kinds of intervention. One extracted building material; the other attempted to recover evidence. Neither can be understood simply by counting how much ground was disturbed.

A find needs a context

An object removed without a record loses part of its meaning. Its material and shape may remain available for study, but its relationship to a floor, wall, burial or sequence of deposits may be irretrievable. This is why archaeological documentation is not paperwork added after discovery. It is part of the evidence itself.

Kenoyer and Meadow's account of Harappa's Ravi Phase explains how analysis of excavated materials and radiocarbon dates enabled a more detailed settlement chronology. A famous site name therefore covers a changing history, not one undifferentiated moment called the Indus civilisation. Separating phases makes it possible to ask when particular objects, practices and building traditions appeared.

Small tools, substantial questions

The authors illustrate bone points, an awl, polished rib fragments and a perforated piece. They suggest possible connections with basketry, spinning or weaving for some of these objects. The cautious wording matters. A shape can support a functional interpretation without proving one exclusive use.

Such finds redirect attention towards activities that are easily lost in monumental accounts of the past. Producing thread or working fibres requires skill, repeated bodily movements and appropriate tools. The surviving object is only one part of that process. Archaeology must reconstruct the relationship between materials and actions while acknowledging where direct evidence is missing.

A catalogue label should preserve that distinction. “Possible weaving tool” is not a weaker version of a confident but unsupported identification; it is a more accurate description of what is known. Uncertainty can indicate disciplined analysis rather than a failure to reach an answer.

The excavation has a history too

Gregory Possehl's review of research at Harappa describes the multidisciplinary programme undertaken with Pakistan's Department of Archaeology and Museums during the later twentieth century. Different specialists and institutions brought different questions to the same site. Published reports allow those questions, methods and conclusions to be revisited.

For a visitor, the museum and excavation record therefore belong alongside the mounds. Ask where an object was found, how it was dated and which interpretation its evidence actually supports. Harappa becomes richer when it is seen not as a completed picture of an ancient city, but as a place where careful study continues to turn surviving traces into historically meaningful relationships.
