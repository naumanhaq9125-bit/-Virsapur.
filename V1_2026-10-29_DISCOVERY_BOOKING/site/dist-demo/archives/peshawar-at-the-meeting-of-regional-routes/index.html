Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

Peshawar at the meeting of regional routes

Peshawar’s sarai, merchant house and fort show how movement became institutions, domestic space and political control within a city.

Calling Peshawar a crossroads is only a beginning. Routes become historically meaningful through places where people stop, transact, worship and govern. Gor Khatri, Sethi House and Bala Hisar reveal different ways in which a city accommodated movement. They also show why exchange should not be presented as a single, uninterrupted condition stretching across every period.

The federal inventory describes Gor Khatri as a complex with a layered sacred and civic history. It associates a seventeenth-century sarai with Jahanara Begum and records monumental entrances opening into a large quadrangle lined with accommodation. A sarai gives physical form to stopping: a traveller passes through a controlled entrance into a shared interior before moving on again.

The architecture of staying

That arrangement differs from a road or pass. It places movement within an institution, with spaces for entry, supervision and temporary occupation. The catalogue also records later changes of religious use. Gor Khatri is consequently not an untouched example of a single Mughal programme. Its significance includes the ways later users altered an established place.

The distinction between a traveller’s accommodation and a merchant household becomes clearer at Sethi House. The inventory records a courtyard reached through a carved entrance, coloured glass and decorated ceilings. It links the house with Seth Karim Bakhsh and places completion in 1884. The courtyard is described as a setting for hospitality and business, joining domestic space with exchange without making the building a public market.

The richness of its interior points to concentrated resources and specialised work. It does not by itself identify where every material came from or which distant city inspired each motif. Those connections need trade records and comparative study. Describing coloured glass precisely is more informative than assigning the whole house to an exotic influence on the strength of resemblance alone.

A route also requires attention to power

Bala Hisar adds the question of control. Its inventory recounts repeated destruction and rebuilding under different authorities, including later replacement of earthen walls with brick masonry. The fort’s long history should therefore not be treated as the survival of one original structure. A prominent position can retain strategic importance while its architecture changes substantially.

The three places complicate an exclusively celebratory story of cultural exchange. A sarai accommodates, a household negotiates access, and a fort asserts authority. Movement can support commerce and encounter while also being supervised, taxed or restricted. Which of these operated in a particular period requires evidence beyond the general description of Peshawar as a gateway.

Read together, the buildings make the city itself central to the route story. Peshawar was not simply a space between more important destinations. It contained institutions and households that shaped what movement meant. Its heritage is most compelling when those local arrangements remain visible alongside the wider networks to which they belonged, and when later changes are treated as history rather than as distractions from an imagined original city.
