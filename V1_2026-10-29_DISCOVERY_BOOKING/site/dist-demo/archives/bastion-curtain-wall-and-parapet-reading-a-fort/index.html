Bastion, curtain wall and parapet: reading a fort

A few precise terms make a fort easier to understand, provided they are attached to visible features rather than memorised as a checklist.

Fortification vocabulary is most useful when it describes relationships. A projecting element, a connecting wall and its protected upper edge work together. Learning their names allows a visitor to move from the statement that a fort has thick walls towards a more exact account of how its perimeter is organised.

A curtain wall is the stretch connecting defensive towers or projecting works. A parapet is a protective wall along an elevated edge, often associated with a walkway or roof. Bastion can describe a projecting defensive feature, but its shape and technical meaning vary across periods and traditions. It should not automatically evoke the angular geometry of a European star fort.

Start with the plan

The official description of Harrand Fort records an approximately circular enclosure with repeated octagonal bastions and entrances on opposing sides. Sangni's record describes a roughly square plan with projecting features and a gate flanked by bastions. These contrasting outlines immediately show why a fort cannot be understood from one wall segment.

On a plan, mark where the boundary projects and where it runs between projections. Then compare the width and form of these features. Are they regularly spaced? Do they concentrate near an entrance? Does the ground outside rise or fall? The names begin a spatial enquiry; they do not supply the explanation in advance.

Read the wall vertically

An elevation or section adds a second dimension. A high wall, its walkway and its upper protection are different elements. The familiar alternating solid and open pattern of battlements is not the entire wall, and a parapet need not always have that profile. A missing upper edge can make a surviving wall look unlike its earlier appearance.

Openings also need careful identification. A drainage outlet, a construction hole and an opening associated with defence may appear similar in a distant photograph. The shape, internal passage and documented context matter. It is better to record an unidentified opening than to label every hole an arrow slit.

Avoid a universal military diagram

UNESCO describes Rohtas as an integrated system of walls, bastions and gateways adapted to its terrain. That comparison helps readers recognise a broader vocabulary, but it does not establish that Harrand or Sangni used identical weapons, strategies or construction methods. Shared terms do not erase chronological and regional differences.

Nor should the vocabulary imply that the whole surviving fort belongs to one campaign. Repairs, rebuilding and changed use can alter an enclosure. A bastion may retain its outline while containing later masonry; a parapet may be reconstructed.

For a visit, use an authorised path and regard wall tops as restricted unless clearly opened and supervised. A low-looking parapet or broad projection is not evidence of safe access. The best result is a precise description made from plans and permitted viewpoints: what projects, what connects, what protects the edge, and which parts remain uncertain.
