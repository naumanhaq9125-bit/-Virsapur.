Gor Khatri: a place with more than one past

Gor Khatri brings deep archaeological deposits together with a Mughal sarai, religious structures and later civic uses. Its value lies in the coexistence of these layers rather than the recovery of one supposedly pure period.

Gor Khatri resists the usual habit of giving a monument one dynasty, one function and one date. In Peshawar's old city, a substantial enclosure contains standing architecture while archaeological deposits preserve evidence of earlier occupation below it. Religious associations, travel infrastructure and civic uses have each contributed to the place that survives.

The Department of Archaeology describes a sarai established under Princess Jahan Ara Begum in 1640, known as Jahanabad. Its account identifies major eastern and western gateways, an internal quadrangle and ranges of cells. These features make the enclosure legible as an organised institution for movement and accommodation, not simply an empty court surrounded by walls.

Above ground and below ground

Pakistan's 2026 Tentative List submission emphasises the combination of deep archaeological deposits and a standing architectural complex. The two require different forms of reading. Gateways and room ranges can be examined as built spaces; buried deposits depend on controlled excavation and recorded relationships.

A date assigned to an excavated layer does not automatically date the wall standing above it. Conversely, a later building does not make the earlier occupation beneath it irrelevant. Gor Khatri's significance depends on preserving the connection between these different kinds of evidence without merging them into one undifferentiated claim of antiquity.

The nomination discusses possible earlier religious associations, but possibility should remain distinct from a securely identified structure. A name in a historical account, a tradition of pilgrimage and an excavated foundation do not carry identical evidential weight.

A courtyard with changing purposes

The federal catalogue records religious transformations during the Sikh period, while the newer nomination includes colonial civic reuse and the early-twentieth-century fire brigade building. These later elements are not merely clutter obscuring the Mughal sarai. They form part of the history through which the complex remained connected to the city.

This does not mean every alteration must be valued equally or that conservation requires retaining every recent addition. It means decisions should begin with documentation and an understanding of significance. Restoring an imagined single-period appearance can erase evidence that helps explain how an urban place survived.

A place where heritage work happens

The Heritage Foundation of Pakistan records establishing a documentation centre at Gor Khatri in 2010 for work on Peshawar's historic buildings. That is a modest but revealing continuation of institutional use: the complex became a setting for recording and understanding other parts of the city's heritage.

For a visitor, begin by reading the enclosure's organisation, then ask which elements belong to which uses and periods. The gateway, cell, religious structure and civic building need not compete for attention. Each answers a different question about travel, authority, devotion or administration.

Gor Khatri is most compelling when its layers remain intelligible together. Its story is not the replacement of one authentic past by a series of distractions. It is the repeated adaptation of a prominent urban place, with archaeology and architecture preserving different parts of that continuing history.
