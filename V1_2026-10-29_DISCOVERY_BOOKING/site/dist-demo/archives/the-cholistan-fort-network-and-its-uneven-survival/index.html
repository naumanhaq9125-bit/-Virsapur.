Cultural Landscapes & Historic Places

The Cholistan fort network and its uneven survival

Derawar is only one part of Cholistan’s fort landscape. Smaller and damaged structures change how the network’s history should be understood.

Derawar’s recognisable outline can overshadow the other forts of Cholistan. Yet a network cannot be understood through its most photogenic member alone. Smaller enclosures, damaged walls and partly lost plans are evidence too. They help test whether the forts shared functions, how their forms differed, and why some survive more visibly than others.

Pakistan’s Tentative List submission, published by UNESCO in 2016, proposes Derawar and the desert forts as a related landscape. Tentative listing is not World Heritage inscription. The submission links the structures with movement, water and political control, while acknowledging that the network’s precise extent and character need further exploration. This is an argument for research and comparison, not a licence to draw a fully proven route between every fort.

Different buildings within the landscape

The federal inventory’s Marot entry, drawing on Mohammad Rafique Mughal’s work, describes a rectangular enclosure and surviving corner bastions. It also reports that much of the layout had disappeared by the documented assessment. That description is a historical condition record, not a fresh survey. Its value lies partly in the features it recorded before further change, and partly in the questions it leaves for present documentation.

Mirgarh’s entry gives a different picture: a compact square fort, an outer earthen rampart and wells inside and outside the enclosure. The catalogue associates its construction with Nur Muhammad Khan at the end of the eighteenth century. Even without accepting every measurement as a current field observation, the contrast with Marot is useful. Size, enclosure and water provision need to be compared individually rather than assumed from Derawar’s form.

Visibility is not importance

A tall standing wall offers a strong photograph. A low mound may preserve a more complex sequence underground. The difference is one of visibility, not automatically one of historical value. A damaged fort can still help establish building phases, access arrangements or the relationship between an enclosure and nearby water infrastructure. Removing loose material to make it more legible would destroy precisely the evidence needed to answer those questions.

The Tentative List account presents continued occupation as one reason for Derawar’s comparatively strong survival. That explanation should not be generalised to every site. Different materials, repair histories and local circumstances can produce very different results. Nor should the apparent absence of a wall on a visitor photograph be treated as proof that a feature never existed.

Reading the network responsibly

An effective comparison begins with modest, consistent observations: the shape of the enclosure, the position of entrances, evidence for rebuilding and the relationship to documented wells. It then distinguishes measured features from proposed connections. The resulting map will have gaps, but those gaps are more useful than an elegant line that claims certainty it does not possess.

Cholistan’s forts belong together without becoming identical. Derawar can introduce the landscape; it cannot replace it. Bringing Marot, Mirgarh and other recorded structures into the account shifts attention from a single monumental silhouette to the uneven survival of an entire historical system.
