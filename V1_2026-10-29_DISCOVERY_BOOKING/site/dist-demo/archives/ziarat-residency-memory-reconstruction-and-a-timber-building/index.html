Ziarat Residency: memory, reconstruction and a timber building

Ziarat Residency is both a place associated with Jinnah and a reconstructed monument whose material history should remain visible.

Ziarat Residency asks visitors to hold two histories together. One concerns a building associated with Muhammad Ali Jinnah’s final year. The other concerns the destruction and reconstruction of that building long after his death. Both are necessary to understand what stands in Ziarat today. Treating the monument as an untouched capsule of 1948 would obscure an important part of its significance.

The Department of Archaeology and Museums describes the Residency’s earlier role as a British administrative summer residence. Its account identifies stone construction with wooden floors and balconies, arranged over two storeys. Timber strongly shapes its appearance, but describing the whole building simply as a wooden house misses that material combination. The same catalogue connects Jinnah’s stay to the period following his inauguration of the State Bank in July 1948.

Association is not the same as burial. The Residency is a place of residence and remembrance; it should not be described as Jinnah’s tomb. Keeping that distinction clear gives the building a more precise role within Pakistan’s commemorative geography.

Reconstruction is part of the history

The Residency was attacked in June 2013. An Associated Press of Pakistan report carried by The Express Tribune records the inauguration of the rehabilitated building in August 2014 and identifies architect Nayyar Ali Dada with the project. Those dates make reconstruction a major chapter, not a minor maintenance footnote.

Sanam Maher’s contemporary reporting explores a difficult question behind the work: whether restoring the character of a cherished place is equivalent to conserving all of its surviving material. Her account describes the architect’s concern with retaining the spirit of the building, while also acknowledging earlier interventions. This is not evidence that every component visible today is original. It is evidence that the project involved choices about what continuity should mean after severe loss.

What a visitor should be able to distinguish

A clear interpretation could separate three layers: the building’s administrative origins, Jinnah’s association with it, and the later destruction and recovery. Each layer has its own documents, photographs and material questions. An old exterior photograph may help explain a reconstructed balcony, but it cannot establish the history of an object displayed inside. A room’s commemorative purpose does not automatically authenticate every furnishing.

This distinction need not diminish the emotional force of the place. On the contrary, explaining what was lost, what survived and what was rebuilt allows remembrance to be more honest. Reconstruction can itself become an important public act, provided it is not confused with an uninterrupted survival of historic fabric.

Ziarat Residency is therefore best encountered as a national memory site with a layered architectural biography. Its familiar outline carries meaning, but the story should extend beyond the outline. The building asks how a society cares for a place after damage, how it records absence, and how it makes a restored landmark intelligible to people who never saw the earlier structure.
