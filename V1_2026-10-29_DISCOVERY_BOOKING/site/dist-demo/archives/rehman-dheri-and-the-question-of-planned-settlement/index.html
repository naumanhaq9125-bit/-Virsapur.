Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Rehman Dheri and the question of planned settlement

Rehman Dheri makes early planning visible in streets and blocks, but its ground plan cannot by itself identify the people or institutions that organised the settlement.

A low mound can contain an unusually legible idea of a town. At Rehman Dheri in the Gomal plain, the arrangement of streets, lanes and blocks is central to the site's importance. The settlement asks a question that is more useful than a contest over which city came first: how much can an ordered plan tell us about the society that made it?

Pakistan's Tentative List description identifies a rectangular, walled settlement whose latest layout remains discernible on the surface and in aerial views. Streets divide the interior into regular blocks. Rain and dew can help reveal buried walls through differences in the ground. This is a plan partly exposed by the condition of the mound, rather than a complete town excavated room by room.

Order before the great cities

Archaeologist Jonathan Mark Kenoyer places Rehman Dheri among the important settlements preceding the mature Indus urban centres. His review of archaeological research highlights the University of Peshawar excavations directed by Farzand Ali Durrani, substantial mud-brick walls, planned streets and early evidence of seals and graffiti. The point is not that urban organisation suddenly appeared at one famous city. Its antecedents can be investigated in several settlements and regional traditions.

A regular street grid implies coordination, but it does not reveal the form of government. The same surviving pattern cannot tell us whether a ruler, a council, neighbouring households or several changing institutions made each decision. Nor does an enclosing wall alone demonstrate a particular war. Planning, authority and defensive intention are related questions, not interchangeable answers.

The surface is only one moment

The Tentative List account also records evidence of craft activity, including kilns and slag, and notes the limitations imposed by comparatively restricted excavation. Surface visibility therefore has a double meaning. It helps make the last settlement arrangement intelligible, while erosion exposes material that once belonged within deposits. A clear photograph can be the result of a damaging process.

Imagine two plans laid over one another: the visible pattern of the final occupation and the sequence of earlier building beneath it. They need not be identical. Establishing continuity requires dated layers, recorded wall relationships and associated finds. A neat aerial outline is an invitation to research, not a substitute for that work.

Making the site accessible without losing evidence

The introduction to Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's January 2024 environmental and social management plan treats conservation and visitor development as activities requiring impact assessment and monitoring. It describes a proposed management framework, not proof that every intended intervention has been completed.

That distinction matters for visitors and planners alike. Paths, drainage, signs and protective measures should help people understand the mound without confusing new facilities with ancient remains. Rehman Dheri's strongest story is already in its ground: an early community organised a substantial settlement, and archaeology is still determining how that organisation changed. Respecting the unanswered questions makes the visible order more interesting, not less.
