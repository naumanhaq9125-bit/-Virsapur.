Early farming in the Kachi plain: evidence rather than an origin myth

Mehrgarh is central to understanding early farming in the Kachi plain, but evidence for crops and livestock is not the same as proof of a single isolated invention. The important story is a process, not an origin slogan.

Mehrgarh’s importance is often compressed into a declaration that farming began here. That statement is too blunt for the evidence. The site in the Kachi plain is central to the study of early food production in northwestern South Asia, but the questions it raises are more interesting than a competition to identify one first place.

The published field reports bring together architecture, burials, tools, plants and animals. Yale’s archaeological index of those reports describes evidence relating to early cereals and livestock, including sheep, goats and cattle. Farming is therefore studied through several connected categories of material, not inferred from a single object that looks agricultural.

Cultivation and domestication are different questions

Cultivation concerns what people do with plants: tending, sowing or managing them. Domestication concerns changes in populations of plants or animals through their relationship with people. The two processes are connected, but evidence for one does not automatically demonstrate the timing or location of the other.

Archaeobotanist Dorian Fuller’s review emphasises the need to study the physical changes associated with domestication and the gaps in evidence for earlier foraging and initial cultivation. In its discussion of Balochistan, the review notes the importance of introduced crops and livestock alongside unresolved questions about local developments and connections farther west.

This approach avoids a false choice between complete isolation and passive borrowing. A community can adopt a crop from elsewhere while developing distinctive ways of growing, storing or combining it with other resources. Movement of a plant does not mean that the whole social system travelled unchanged with it.

What the assemblage can establish

The field-report description identifies cereal evidence including barley, with variation in the degree to which domesticated characteristics are apparent. That qualification matters. A grain is not merely a token in a list of foods; its identification, condition and archaeological context affect the conclusions drawn from it.

The same applies to animal remains. Species identification alone does not tell us whether an animal was hunted, herded or obtained through exchange. Researchers have to examine additional patterns rather than treating every familiar domestic species as proof of a fully developed farming economy.

A history of decisions and connections

An origin myth makes change appear instantaneous. Archaeological evidence instead encourages questions about repeated choices: how people obtained food, managed risk, organised storage and responded to local conditions. Different parts of that process may have changed at different speeds.

Chronology also needs to remain open to revision as better samples and methods become available. An early date repeated in textbooks should not substitute for checking which deposit was dated and how the result was obtained.

Mehrgarh is most valuable when it is allowed to complicate the story. It reveals early communities making livelihoods through a combination of practices and relationships. Understanding that process gives the Kachi plain a substantial place in the history of farming without asking the site to prove a simplified claim of exclusive invention.
