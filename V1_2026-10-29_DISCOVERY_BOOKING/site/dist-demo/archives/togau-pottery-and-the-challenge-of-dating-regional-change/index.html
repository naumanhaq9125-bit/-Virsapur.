Togau pottery and the challenge of dating regional change

Togau pottery is recognised through a history of classification and comparison. Its decorative groups are useful research tools, but uncertain contexts prevent them from functioning as a simple universal clock.

Togau pottery is often introduced through recognisable painted designs, but its real archaeological interest lies in how those designs have been classified and dated. A sequence of motifs can look like a ready-made clock. It only becomes a reliable chronological tool, however, when the material has been connected to secure archaeological contexts.

The official catalogue identifies Togau as a site in the Kalat region, named after a nearby hamlet. The relationship between a type-site and the pottery category named after it should be kept clear. The category can occur at many places; it is not confined to the settlement whose name it carries.

How the categories were built

Zahir Hussain and Kiran Shahid Siddiqui’s analytical study explains the importance of Beatrice de Cardi’s work at Anjira and Siah Damb in defining Togau groups. Decorative differences were used to distinguish stages conventionally labelled A, B, C and D. The authors also note difficulties caused by erosion and the need to combine evidence from different excavations.

Their own survey observations in Panjgur found an uneven distribution of these types: some were prominent, others scarce or not clearly identified. Such variation matters. A group missing from a surface collection does not necessarily prove that the corresponding period was absent from the settlement.

Surface visibility, sample size and the locations examined can all shape what enters a collection. A classification is strongest when its limits are stated alongside its diagnostic features. Otherwise, a useful comparison can harden into an assumption that every region followed the same sequence at the same pace.

Context is part of the object

The study also discusses Togau-type vessels in a private collection and acknowledges their likely illicit excavation. Attractive, relatively complete vessels may preserve decoration while losing the information needed to date their deposition. Recovering an object from the market cannot recover the layer from which it was removed.

A contrasting example of documentation appears in Cambridge’s Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology. Its record for worked stone from Siah Damb includes an accession number, excavation information and a period attribution linked to de Cardi’s work. The object is not pottery, but the record shows how different kinds of finds can remain connected to an investigated sequence.

A chronology open to testing

For Togau, the productive question is therefore not which motif looks oldest in isolation. It is how decorative groups relate to manufacturing methods, associated finds and independently dated deposits. New evidence may confirm parts of an established sequence while requiring other parts to be revised.

This makes the pottery more than a set of pleasing designs. It becomes evidence for how researchers construct regional histories from incomplete material. A careful public account should show the beauty of the vessels while making clear that their chronological value depends on relationships that an unprovenanced object, however impressive, cannot supply on its own.
