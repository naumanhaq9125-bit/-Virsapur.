The wells of Mohenjodaro

Brick-lined wells bring Mohenjodaro into focus as a city of repeated daily tasks, specialised construction and carefully arranged access to water.

A well is a modest-looking opening in the ground. At Mohenjodaro, it is also a way into the organisation of a city. The most revealing question is not simply how old a shaft is, but who could approach it, how water moved from it into neighbouring rooms, and what happened after that water had been used.

Archaeologist Michael Jansen’s study of the city’s water system describes hundreds of wells alongside bathing facilities and drainage. His much-cited figure of more than 700 belongs to an archaeological account of urban provision, not a promise that a visitor can identify that many exposed shafts today. Estimates of an ancient system and the number of monuments visible on a walk are different kinds of information.

A circle made from bricks

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer’s photographic documentation draws attention to wedge-shaped bricks at a large well in the HR area. Their form allows a circular shaft to be assembled from small units. Some bricks also carry grooves associated with drawing ropes. These details shift attention from the well as a picturesque ruin to the practical work of building and using it.

The same documentation distinguishes wells reached directly from a street from those within buildings. That distinction matters even without knowing the names or legal rights of their users. A doorway, passage or enclosing wall changes the route to water. An apparently simple circle therefore belongs to a larger architectural arrangement rather than standing alone as an isolated invention.

Supply is only half the story

Water brought into a house must also be handled after use. Jansen considers supply and disposal together, while a later engineering study by Nakamura and colleagues questions how the drainage evidence should be interpreted. Its abstract proposes that the drains primarily removed wastewater from paved rooms near wells. Calling every channel a sewer can conceal precisely this question about function.

For a careful reading of the site, follow relationships rather than collecting superlatives: shaft to platform, platform to room, room to outlet, outlet to lane. Where a connection is not preserved, leave it as a question. A modern photograph can show a wall very clearly while revealing little about its relationship to a surface removed during excavation.

The wells make Mohenjodaro compelling because they concern repeated actions: reaching, drawing, carrying, washing and maintaining. They suggest a city understood through use, not only through famous buildings. Their achievement is substantial without claiming that every resident enjoyed identical access, that every installation worked at once, or that an ancient system was equivalent to modern household plumbing.
