Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

Animal bones and the limits of ancient menus

Animal bones record more than meals. Species, skeletal parts, modification and deposition must be considered together before reconstructing husbandry, hunting or food consumption.

A pile of animal bones is not an ancient menu preserved in shorthand. Some remains may come from food preparation, but others can reflect craftwork, natural death, deliberate burial or later disturbance. Zooarchaeology begins by identifying the material and its context before deciding which human activities it represents.

Historic England’s animal-bone handbook sets out the importance of recording species, skeletal parts, age indicators and modifications. Cut marks can contribute evidence for carcass processing, while weathering and scavenger damage help reconstruct what happened before burial. These observations need to be considered together rather than allowing one dramatic mark to determine the whole interpretation.

What is being counted?

Fragment numbers are affected by breakage. One bone can produce many pieces, and different parts of a skeleton preserve differently. A count of fragments therefore cannot be converted directly into the number of animals eaten, still less into portions served to a population.

The handbook also emphasises that some processing activities leave more visible traces than others. An absence of cut marks is not automatically evidence that a carcass was untouched. Recording methods, preservation and the parts recovered all influence the result.

This is one reason that recovery procedures matter. If small bones are missed, the apparent balance between large animals and smaller species can change. The assemblage is not simply what once existed; it is what survived, was recovered and could be identified.

Availability is not the same as use

Richard Meadow’s discussion of regional subsistence offers a useful comparison from Balakot. He notes that fish bones and mollusc shells were uncommon in earlier levels but much more abundant in the later Harappan phase. Proximity to coastal resources did not produce an identical pattern of exploitation throughout the site’s history.

Meadow also stresses variation between settlements and phases, even where a broad agricultural foundation appears similar. Landscape creates possibilities and constraints, but people’s knowledge and decisions shape how those resources are used. An environmental map cannot by itself reconstruct a community’s diet.

Animals had lives before they became remains

Food is only one dimension of human-animal relationships. A study may also ask about management, movement, working lives or the use of non-food products. Those questions require evidence appropriate to them. Naming cattle in an assemblage does not, on its own, establish a particular dairy system or method of traction.

For early farming places such as Mehrgarh, this distinction helps keep the discussion grounded. The aim is not to project modern menus or identities backward, but to understand the relationships supported by the material.

An accessible archive article can explain the difference between identification and interpretation through a few well-recorded examples. The bones become more informative when their limitations are visible. Rather than offering a supposedly complete dinner table, they can reveal changing practices, selective survival and the work required to turn fragments into a history of human and animal lives.
