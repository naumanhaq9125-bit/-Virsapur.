Empress Market: a building still used as a market

Empress Market’s architecture and commercial life cannot be separated. Its history includes the building’s colonial origins and the consequences of later changes around it.

Empress Market is a historic building whose purpose is still legible in its name. It was designed for exchange, not simply for viewing. Its architectural form, the movement of goods and the people who buy and sell are therefore parts of the same subject. An account that removes commercial life from the picture risks preserving an image of the market while losing the reason it exists.

The archaeological catalogue dates construction to 1884–89 and records four galleries arranged to accommodate shops and stalls. The Endowment Fund Trust identifies the market within Saddar and describes a varied range of everyday commodities. These details establish a working urban place rather than a monument whose only legitimate activity is tourism.

Architecture organised around transactions

A market building has to be understood through circulation. People need to enter, compare goods, stop, turn and leave, while sellers need access to their stock. The relationship between passage and stall is therefore as important as the outer facade. A photograph taken when a space is empty may clarify its geometry but conceal the patterns through which it normally operates.

This does not mean congestion or poor maintenance should be romanticised. It means that improvements should be assessed against the building’s function as well as its appearance. A clearer view of a wall is only one possible outcome of an intervention; the effects on livelihoods, access and the distribution of activity also matter.

The Urban Resource Centre documented demolitions around Empress Market in 2018 as part of a wider record of evictions in Karachi. Its material makes it impossible to describe the subsequent visual opening around the building as a purely aesthetic event. Shops and working spaces were involved, and the people affected belong in the history of the place.

A different way to imagine conservation

Urban planner Arif Hasan proposed ways to combine restoration with continued commerce, including organised low-height shops, improved pedestrian connections and space for changing public activities. These were proposals, not a description of a completed scheme. Their importance lies in demonstrating that the debate was not limited to a choice between an obscured building and an empty foreground.

For a heritage archive, the same principle applies to images. Before-and-after photographs should be dated and explained. A newly exposed facade can appear to tell a simple story of rescue, while the caption may need to explain a much more complicated social change. The photograph is evidence of appearance, not a complete evaluation of policy.

Empress Market’s heritage value is strongest when its architecture and its use remain in conversation. The colonial building, the later commercial networks and the disputes over public space are all parts of its history. Understanding them together does not require agreement on every intervention. It requires asking a fuller question: how can a market retain significant fabric while continuing to serve the city that gives it life?
