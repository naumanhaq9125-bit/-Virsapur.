Archaeology & Ancient Civilisations

From sherd to published argument

A pottery fragment becomes evidence through a chain of recording, classification and comparison. Each step should remain visible when a published argument moves from a sherd to a wider historical claim.

A sherd is a fragment, not a miniature complete pot. It may preserve a rim, a base, part of a handle or an otherwise unremarkable piece of wall. Its contribution to a historical argument depends on what survives and on the information recorded with it.

Begin with attributes

Pottery recording guidance from the Chartered Institute for Archaeologists distinguishes contextual unit, fabric, form, decoration and other attributes. “Fabric” concerns the material body of the pottery, including its clay and inclusions; it does not mean a textile. This vocabulary allows researchers to describe comparable features rather than rely on an overall impression.

A fragment with a recognisable rim can offer different information from a plain body sherd. Decoration may support comparison, but it is not the only relevant feature. Material and manufacturing observations can complicate an identification that initially seemed obvious from colour or pattern.

The analytical advantage is that a description can be checked. Another specialist may disagree with the classification while still working from the recorded attributes. If the entry contains only a culture name, the reasoning that led to it becomes much harder to assess.

A fragment is not a vessel count

Several sherds may come from one vessel, while a small assemblage can also contain fragments of many different vessels. Counting pieces is therefore not identical to counting pots. The chosen measure needs to be stated before quantities are used to compare deposits or settlements.

This matters when an argument moves from pottery to behaviour. A higher fragment count does not automatically mean more people, more consumption or a longer occupation. Breakage, disposal and the composition of the investigated deposit all need consideration.

Reporting guidance also asks researchers to address condition and fragmentation when discussing how an assemblage formed. The published table and the interpretation should therefore belong to the same argument, rather than the table serving merely as a technical appendix no one is expected to read.

From comparison to historical explanation

A resemblance between two pottery groups may support a chronological or technological comparison. It does not independently prove migration, trade or shared identity. Those explanations require additional evidence and an account of why alternative mechanisms are less convincing.

For an archive reader encountering a sherd from a Pakistani mound, useful questions are modest and precise. Which part of the vessel survives? How securely is its context known? Is the proposed comparison based on form, fabric, decoration or several features together? What uncertainty remains?

The fragment's value is not diminished if the answer stops short of a dramatic story. A carefully documented sherd can refine a sequence or reveal a problem in an existing classification.

The route from fragment to argument should remain inspectable at every stage. Description, grouping, comparison and interpretation are connected operations, but they should not collapse into one confident label that hides how much work the conclusion actually requires.
