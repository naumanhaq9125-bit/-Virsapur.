Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan across recording and live performance

Three documented releases separate Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan’s live performance, studio collaboration and the later life of a recording archive.

The date printed on an album is not always the date of the performance it contains. Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan’s recording history makes that distinction especially useful. A live festival recording, a studio collaboration and a later archival release can reach listeners through similar streaming interfaces while preserving very different encounters with the musicians.

Real World’s Live at WOMAD 1985 is a clear example. The label identifies the performance as taking place on 20 July 1985 at Mersea Island in Essex, but dates the album’s release to 2019. Its credits describe an eight-track analogue recording, followed much later by digitisation, restoration, mixing and mastering. The familiar word live therefore names the original performance setting, not an absence of subsequent technical work.

Performance and release are separate events

The credits also make a collective process visible. Nusrat appears with his party, while the surviving sound passes through the hands of recording and restoration specialists before reaching the listener. Naming those roles does not diminish the singer’s achievement. It identifies what kind of object the album is and avoids presenting a commercial release as an untouched window onto a night several decades earlier.

Chain of Light supplies a different chronology. Real World’s announcement describes recordings made at its studios in April 1990 and scheduled for release in September 2024, after rediscovery in its tape archive. The label places these sessions alongside work on Mustt Mustt with producer Michael Brook. Traditional ensemble recording and a crossover collaboration were therefore being pursued in the same period, rather than occupying neatly separated stages of a career.

This matters when accounts divide music into a supposedly pure early form and a compromised later one. The session history supports a more complicated picture of simultaneous approaches. It does not tell us that every listener experienced them in the same way, or that one release can stand for all the contexts in which qawwali was performed.

Listening with the record in view

A useful comparison begins with the documentation: when and where was the music recorded, which performers are credited, and what happened before publication? Only then should an account move to audible features. This article has checked release records rather than conducted a close listening session, so it does not invent claims about a particular improvisation, audience response or transition within a track.

The archive has its own history as well. Tapes can remain unpublished, be rediscovered and acquire a new audience under a new title. A late release is evidence of continuing custodianship and editorial choice, not a new performance by an artist who has died.

For heritage discovery, Nusrat’s recordings are consequently more than a playlist of famous titles. They invite attention to ensemble work, institutions of recording and the changing ways a performance survives. Keeping those layers visible allows admiration to become a more precise form of listening, with the people and processes around the voice remaining part of the story.
