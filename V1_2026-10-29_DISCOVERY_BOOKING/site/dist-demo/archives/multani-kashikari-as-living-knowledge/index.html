Living Traditions

Multani kashikari as living knowledge

Multani kashikari survives through working knowledge, training and access to production resources—not through admiration for blue ornament alone.

The blue surface of a Multani ceramic object is easy to recognise before its maker is known. That visual familiarity can conceal a harder question: who is able to learn the work, practise it and sustain a livelihood from it? Kashikari is living knowledge because people continue making decisions with materials. A photograph of an old tiled building cannot answer how that knowledge circulates today.

Knowledge and access

Ayesha Aslam’s PIDE case study of Multan ceramics distinguishes workers’ vocational skills from owners’ access to physical capital. Its interviews describe learning within production settings and unequal opportunities to establish an independent business. Possessing a skill and possessing the resources to use it commercially are different conditions.

The study also records respondents’ accounts of knowledge being retained within families, including reluctance to share particular stages of production. Those testimonies reveal tensions around teaching and competition. They do not establish that every Multani family behaves alike, or that inherited knowledge necessarily prevents new entrants.

For an archive, the useful lesson is to ask more precise questions. Who taught a ceramicist to prepare a body, mix a material or judge a result? Which parts of the process are shared, and with whom? A general statement that the craft passes from father to son would erase the different answers that actual makers may give.

More than one setting for learning

The Skilling Pakistan directory lists a Government Institute Blue Pottery Development in Mumtazabad, Multan. That institutional entry establishes a named training setting, not a promise about current admissions, staffing or the availability of a particular course. Those details require a separate current enquiry.

Another form of evidence comes from practitioner presentation. Ustad Wajid Art’s workshop description outlines teaching that connects clay preparation, pattern, pigment, glaze and kiln work. It is the workshop’s own account of its offer, not an independently assessed qualification. It nevertheless shows how a maker presents ceramic knowledge as an interconnected process rather than simply a decorative motif.

These different settings need not be treated as rivals. A classroom can introduce vocabulary and observation; a production workshop can expose a learner to repeated decisions and consequences. Whether either arrangement is effective must be assessed with the people who teach and learn there, not inferred from the word traditional or modern.

Document the judgement, not only the result

A useful future interview might compare a successful object with a rejected one, if its maker agrees. Why was one retained and the other set aside? Which decision would the maker change? Such questions recognise practical judgement without publishing a technical recipe or appropriating protected workshop knowledge.

The purpose is not to turn every ceramicist into an anonymous custodian of the past. Makers may teach, experiment, restrict access, change products or leave the occupation. A responsible account gives those choices space. Multani kashikari deserves appreciation as work carried out by living people, with expertise and constraints that cannot be read from blue glaze alone.
