Architecture & Built Heritage

A market’s building and a market’s life

A market survives through trade as well as masonry. Conserving its architecture requires understanding the people and routines that animate it.

A market can be photographed as a building or experienced as a pattern of exchange. The first view notices gates, roofs and galleries. The second includes deliveries, negotiations, storage, cleaning and the repeated journeys of traders and customers. Neither is sufficient alone. The architecture and its use have histories that overlap without being identical.

Karachi's Empress Market provides a clear architectural starting point. The national inventory records a late nineteenth-century building organised with galleries and accommodation for stalls. That description explains part of the formal arrangement. It does not tell us everything about who traded there at different times or how commerce extended into the surrounding streets.

Plans show capacity, not a complete social history

A row of stalls suggests a division of space, but a plan cannot establish the goods sold in every period, the terms of tenancy or the relationships between traders. Those questions need records and testimony. A historical capacity figure should not be presented as a current count of businesses without a new survey.

Likewise, an open passage may have served customers, deliveries or both at different times of day. Understanding these rhythms requires observation with dates and consent, rather than a single crowded photograph treated as a timeless image of market life.

A bazaar need not be one monumental building

Tang Gali Bazaar in Kalabagh offers a different kind of enquiry. Its official record draws attention to verandas and wooden balconies along a trading setting. Here, the architectural interest lies partly in a sequence of edges and upper levels, rather than only in one designed market hall.

The comparison should retain that difference. A purpose-built market and a street bazaar may both support trade while arranging access, shade, storage and display differently. Neither should be treated as a more authentic version of the other simply because it appears older or more picturesque.

Improvement has consequences

Historic England's guidance on historic retail centres connects the condition of buildings and public space with the health of their uses. Its institutional context differs from Pakistan's, but the underlying question is useful: what happens to a historic market if the architecture is repaired while the activities sustaining it are displaced?

Answering that question requires listening to traders, workers, residents and customers, including people whose contribution is not represented by a shopfront. Cleaner views and restored masonry are not, by themselves, a complete measure of success. Access for deliveries, sanitation, safe movement and viable livelihoods belong in the assessment.

For a visitor, respectful attention means asking before close portraits, keeping routes clear and recognising that bargaining or handling goods is not a performance staged for heritage tourism. A purchase can be an exchange; it does not purchase unlimited access to someone's story.

The market's building and its life should remain connected in interpretation. The goal is neither an empty architectural exhibit nor an uncritical celebration of congestion, but a place whose history and continuing work can both be understood.
