The disputed granaries of Indus archaeology

Harappa's so-called granary shows how a familiar building name can outlive the evidence for its function. The monument remains important even when its interpretation changes.

A name can become the most durable part of a ruin. Once a building appears on maps and in schoolbooks as a granary, visitors begin to see its walls as proof of grain storage. At Harappa, the question should run in the opposite direction: what evidence supports the name?

A building and an interpretation

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer's account identifies the so-called granary on Mound F as a substantial brick structure organised in two rows of rooms beside a passage. He also notes the absence of the concentrations of burnt grain or storage vessels that would help establish the proposed function. The granary interpretation relied on comparisons with buildings elsewhere, rather than a decisive local assemblage.

This does not mean the surviving architecture is unimportant. It means that scale, construction and arrangement are observations, while “granary” is an interpretation placed upon them. The difference should remain visible in a caption, a tour or a digital reconstruction. Quotation marks can be useful when a conventional name is retained for recognition.

What would strengthen a functional argument?

A building's function is more persuasive when several kinds of evidence agree. An architectural arrangement may suggest one activity, but contents, residues, access patterns and the sequence of alterations can support or complicate that suggestion. The absence of one expected feature is not automatically conclusive; preservation and the extent of excavation also matter.

The appropriate response is therefore not to replace one confident name with another equally confident guess. Calling the structure a palace, temple or administrative centre without the necessary evidence would repeat the same mistake. A description can remain exact about the surviving walls while being cautious about the activities once performed above or between them.

Harappa.com's account of renewed excavation reports further investigation of this area without the discovery of grain that might settle the old label. That is a reminder that archaeological interpretation is revisable. New excavation can question an inherited explanation even when the outline of a building has long been familiar.

For a visitor, a productive exercise is to separate three layers of information: what survives, what excavators reconstructed and what later interpreters proposed. A plan may show all three with similar visual authority unless its conventions are explained.

The disputed granary is consequently not an embarrassment to archaeology. It is an example of its corrective work. The most responsible account does not promise certainty that the evidence cannot supply. It lets a major public structure remain a major public structure, while keeping the question of its precise use open to further investigation.
