Sacred Heritage

Buddhist pilgrimage and the material presence of relics

Reliquaries show how small objects could give monumental Buddhist spaces religious significance. Their inscriptions and collection histories must be read as carefully as their decoration.

A monumental stupa and a small reliquary operate at very different scales, yet they can belong to the same sacred relationship. The building gives a deposit a place in the landscape. The deposit helps explain why that place mattered to worshippers. Looking at masonry alone can miss this connection; looking at a casket in isolation can lose the setting that gave it meaning.

Relics are not simply another category of archaeological treasure. Their religious significance concerns an association with an honoured person or sacred presence. Archaeology can document a container, an inscription and the circumstances of a discovery without being able to verify every devotional identification attached to the contents.

Inscriptions connect objects and people

Kurt Behrendt’s Metropolitan Museum account of Gandhara discusses reliquaries within the relationship between stupas and monastic complexes. The Indravarman reliquary provides an example of an inscription connecting dedication with named people and religious merit. Such evidence brings donors into view rather than leaving the institution as an anonymous group of ruined walls.

That does not mean every deposit has an equally informative inscription. An uninscribed casket may preserve evidence of materials and manufacture while leaving its donor unknown. The absence of a name is a limit of the surviving record, not proof that the object lacked an important religious role.

An original and a replica are different witnesses

The British Museum’s record for its Kanishka casket is especially instructive: the museum identifies the object as a replica, with the original in Peshawar Museum. A photograph circulated without that information can easily create a false impression about where the ancient object is held.

The replica may be useful for studying or displaying the form. It is not evidence that the original material is in London. The catalogue also discusses difficulties in reading the inscription. A confident date copied from an old label should therefore be checked against the present scholarly record rather than repeated as settled fact.

What a find record can preserve

A separate British Museum entry for a rock-crystal reliquary from Sonala Pind records its relationship to a stupa discovery and to other containers. It also preserves uncertainty about the identification of material once associated with the deposit. This is a reminder that an excavation or collection history can be informative without being complete.

For visitors to Buddhist sites in Pakistan, these objects suggest questions to bring to a site museum. Is a displayed item original, a cast or a modern reconstruction? Is its findspot known? Does the label distinguish an archaeological attribution from a religious tradition? Those questions deepen appreciation rather than diminishing the object’s sacred importance.

Pilgrimage cannot be reconstructed from stone alone

Sites such as Takht-i-Bahi and Bhamala belong within a wider exploration of Buddhist sacred space. Their architectural remains should not, however, be used to invent a single unchanged pilgrimage practice across centuries.

A more defensible account connects specific deposits, inscriptions and excavated contexts where the evidence permits. It acknowledges living Buddhist interpretations without pretending that present testimony automatically supplies every missing ancient detail. The small reliquary and the monumental landscape then illuminate one another, while each retains its own history.
