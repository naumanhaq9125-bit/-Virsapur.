Sacred Heritage

Abandoned does not mean ownerless

Disuse describes an activity, not the disappearance of rights or attachment. A quiet or damaged sacred building still requires permission, care and a precise account of its condition.

An empty doorway is not an invitation. A sacred building may appear unused during a brief visit, yet still have an owner, a caretaker, neighbours responsible for access or a community that remembers it from elsewhere. The word abandoned can conceal all of these relationships.

It is also imprecise. Does it mean worship has stopped, the roof is damaged, no one lives nearby or a previous use has ended? Each describes a different condition. A heritage account becomes more useful when it names the observed situation rather than applying a dramatic label to the whole place.

Two records, different circumstances

The federal catalogue describes the temple at Harnoli through its brickwork, shikhara, paintings and inscriptions. It records private ownership. That does not establish present access arrangements, but it clearly gives no basis for treating the building as an unclaimed object.

The Dalwal entry describes a temple and nearby samadhi and reports an altered use during the period documented by its source. It separately records government ownership. The two entries show why the visible state or religious affiliation of a building cannot be used to guess its administrative position.

Both are dated records. Their condition descriptions should not be repeated as though someone inspected the buildings this morning. A current photograph can update part of the record, but it still cannot establish ownership from appearance.

Attachment can survive interrupted use

A family photograph, a remembered festival or an institution’s papers may connect people with a building they no longer regularly visit. That attachment is not disproved by a locked gate. Nor should the existence of a distant connection be used to ignore people currently responsible for the site.

A useful enquiry asks about both. Who looks after the place now? Who has historical knowledge of it? What evidence explains a change of use? The answers may come from different people and may not agree completely.

The ETPB’s published responsibilities include maintaining records and managing relevant trust property. This provides one example of why institutional enquiries matter. It does not establish that either of these particular temples falls within its control, and no such conclusion is made here.

Documentation is not salvage

A detached tile, carved fragment or broken fitting can seem vulnerable. Removing it as a keepsake or an improvised rescue destroys its relationship to the place and may prevent proper recording. The appropriate response is to report a concern to the responsible institution, not to appoint oneself custodian of the object.

The same restraint applies to entry. A damaged stair, unfastened door or gap in a boundary is not confirmation that a visit is authorised or safe. Photographs taken from permitted positions can still contribute to a record without creating another risk.

Better language changes the encounter. “No worship observed during this visit” is narrower than “abandoned forever.” “Roof missing in a dated survey” is more informative than “forgotten.” Such precision leaves room for people, rights and future care. It also prevents heritage discovery from becoming an excuse to treat vulnerable places as though no one has a claim on them.
