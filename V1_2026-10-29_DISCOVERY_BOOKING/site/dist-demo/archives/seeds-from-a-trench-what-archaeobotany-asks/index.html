Seeds from a trench: what archaeobotany asks

Plant remains can reveal crop processing, fuel use and labour as well as food. Research at Harappa shows why seeds in a deposit should not be read as a straightforward ancient menu.

A charred seed from an excavation can seem like a direct message from an ancient meal. It may be evidence of food, but its route into the deposit needs investigation. A seed could arrive with processing waste, spilled grain, stored material or fuel. Archaeobotany asks how plants were used and how their remains survived, not merely which familiar foods can be named.

A recent study of Harappa makes that distinction especially clear. The researchers examined relationships between grain and weed seeds in the assemblage. Their analysis suggests that crops often reached the city in an already well-processed condition, while burning animal dung introduced many small weed seeds into archaeological deposits.

The route from field to sample

That interpretation changes what a collection of seeds can mean. Small weeds need not represent plants deliberately gathered for human consumption. They may have passed through animals and entered a fire as part of fuel. The material still reveals human activity, but the activity is different from the one suggested by a simple food list.

The Harappa study also records analytical limitations, including the difficulty of distinguishing some modern and ancient seeds without breaking them. Such decisions affect which material enters the final analysis. A published result is therefore inseparable from the procedures used to select and identify the evidence.

This makes the sample record important. Where was the material found? How much sediment was examined? What was preserved and what was excluded? These questions help prevent a very small or unusual group of remains from being treated as representative of an entire city.

Looking beyond visible seeds

Kashyap and Weber’s pilot study at Farmana in Haryana explored a complementary form of evidence: microscopic starch grains. They examined surfaces including pottery, stone tools and dental calculus. Their report argues that reliance on larger charred remains alone can overlook plants whose useful parts do not readily survive that process.

The Farmana work identified starches associated with several plant groups and linked observations to particular sampled surfaces. Those findings belong to Farmana; they should not be moved into Harappa’s record merely because both sites are associated with the Indus civilisation. The methodological comparison is valuable without inventing an identical diet.

From ingredients to work

Plant remains can also raise questions about where labour took place. If crops arrived at a settlement after substantial processing, some work may have occurred elsewhere. Establishing that possibility does not identify every worker or the terms of their labour, but it gives the investigation a more social dimension than a list of crops alone.

The strongest public account therefore follows evidence through a chain of reasoning. Identification comes first, then consideration of preservation and deposition, and only then an interpretation of practice. Seeds can reveal a great deal, but they do not speak in complete recipes. Their value lies in the careful questions that make ordinary fragments informative about ancient lives.
