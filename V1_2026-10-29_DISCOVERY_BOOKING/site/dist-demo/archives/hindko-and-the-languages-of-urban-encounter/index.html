Hindko and the languages of urban encounter

Hindko’s urban life is visible in changing language choices and specialised workplace vocabulary, not only in a list of places where it is spoken.

A city’s linguistic heritage is not confined to its oldest inscriptions. It also lives in workplaces, family conversations and decisions about which language to use with whom. Hindko offers a way to examine that everyday urban texture without pretending that one language belongs exclusively to one city.

Peshawar is an important setting for this inquiry, but a citywide label cannot reveal how different speakers navigate the same streets. Age, occupation, education and social relationships may all affect the language choices a person makes.

What a small study can show

A 2021 study of Hindko language maintenance in Peshawar used a questionnaire with 75 participants divided between two age groups. The authors reported a tendency among a substantial number of younger respondents to shift towards other dominant languages, linking this with social mobility.

The study is evidence about its participants and research design, not a census of all Hindko speakers. Its value lies in the questions it raises about use and aspiration. Knowing that someone identifies with Hindko does not tell us which language they use at work, with children or in a formal setting.

Language choice should not be reduced to personal loyalty. Opportunities and expectations can shape what people find useful or necessary. Equally, a person using several languages need not have abandoned the language they grew up with.

A workshop has its own vocabulary

Haroon-ur-Rashid and Raja Nasim Akhtar’s research on Hindko vehicle vocabulary offers a more focused view of contact. They interviewed motor mechanics and analysed how English loanwords were adapted to Hindko sound patterns, including adjustments to consonant clusters.

This is not the same phenomenon as language shift. Borrowing can occur within continued use of Hindko. A technical term becomes part of a working conversation through the speech patterns of those using it, rather than remaining an untouched foreign object.

The example also expands what counts as heritage knowledge. A workshop’s vocabulary records encounters with technologies, training and other languages. It may be less likely to appear in a ceremonial performance, but it belongs to the history of how people communicate and work.

Publications and recordings make another public

The Hindko Language and Culture Society presents publications and audiovisual material through its own platform. Such initiatives create places where language can be read and heard beyond a household or workplace. They should be understood as particular organisational efforts, not as a single institution speaking for every Hindko user.

A useful archive would distinguish a literary reading, an interview and a conversation about a trade. Each has a different purpose and audience. Their variety is more informative than searching for one sample that supposedly captures an entire urban culture.

Hindko’s heritage is consequently both literary and everyday. Following its use across settings makes urban encounter visible: languages meet, terms are adapted and speakers choose among possibilities. That living complexity deserves attention alongside the monuments and historic neighbourhoods with which the language is associated.
