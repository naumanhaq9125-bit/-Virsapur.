Why an atlas sometimes leaves a place unmapped

An empty map position can be more honest than a precise-looking guess. Coordinates need provenance, context and an account of uncertainty.

An atlas can know that a heritage place exists without knowing exactly where to put its marker. A catalogue may identify a village, describe a nearby landmark or repeat an old coordinate that conflicts with the written location. In that situation, filling the gap with a convenient point can make the map look complete while making its information less trustworthy.

GBIF’s Georeferencing Best Practices was developed for biodiversity records, not as a heritage-site designation standard. Its distinction between coordinate precision and location uncertainty is nevertheless useful for any reader of mapped records. More decimal places do not automatically mean better evidence. A coarse original measurement can acquire many digits when its format is converted.

The point should describe the place

Imagine a catalogue that places a ruin somewhere within a large district but gives no verified site coordinate. The district’s centre is a coordinate for an administrative area, not for that ruin. Assigning it to the heritage record would turn a known broad association into an unsupported exact location. A visitor might then mistake the marker for an entrance or navigable destination.

A location also needs context. Does a coordinate represent a building, the centre of a large archaeological property, a survey observation or a visitor facility? Two different points can both be legitimate if they describe different features. Without that explanation, an apparent disagreement can be impossible to interpret.

The GBIF guidance stresses the importance of retaining source information and considering locality descriptions alongside coordinates. Applied cautiously to a heritage atlas, this suggests an audit trail: record where the point came from, what it represents and why it was accepted or withheld. A later correction should improve that record rather than erase the reason for the earlier uncertainty.

Listing without pretending to locate

A platform can still make an unresolved place discoverable. It can provide a textual record, its documented district relationship and an explanation that precise mapping awaits verification. This is different from declaring the place unimportant or absent. It separates a question about identity from a question about spatial evidence.

There may also be reasons not to publish an exact location even when it is known. Those require an explicit policy and responsible review, not an invented coordinate that quietly disguises the decision. The public record should communicate the limitation appropriately without exposing sensitive information.

An honest atlas therefore contains both mapped knowledge and visible limits. Its quality is not measured only by the number of dots. It also depends on whether each dot can be defended, whether corrections remain traceable and whether the interface resists presenting uncertainty as an unwarranted promise of precision.
