Sethi House and the merchant courtyard

Sethi House connects the display of merchant wealth with the practical organisation of privacy, business and climate. Its courtyard is the hinge between those different demands.

The courtyard at Sethi House is more than a pleasant opening within a richly decorated building. It helps organise the relationship between reception, business, domestic life and movement through the house. In the dense fabric of old Peshawar, the residence turns inward without becoming disconnected from the commercial world that made it possible.

The federal archaeological catalogue associates the house with Seth Karim Bakhsh and records completion in 1884 after a long building campaign. Pakistan's 2026 Tentative List submission places it within Mohallah Sethian, close to major market routes, and describes it as both a family residence and a place of business. Those functions explain why its architecture must be read through access as well as ornament.

Who can move where?

The nomination describes stairways, screened corridors and service spaces that organised circulation while maintaining household privacy. A visitor entering today may follow a museum-style route that differs from historic patterns of movement. Doors and passages once helped distinguish the spaces available to a business visitor from those used by household members.

The courtyard provided a shared point of orientation without making all adjoining rooms equally public. This is an important distinction. An inward-facing plan can connect spaces visually while still controlling how they are reached. The arrangement allows proximity and separation to exist together.

The same submission identifies ventilated lower spaces and terraces among the house's responses to climate. Such features belong to the practical intelligence of the design. They should be studied alongside the decorative finish rather than treated as a less interesting technical background.

Light shaped by craft

The federal catalogue records carved entrance work, coloured glass in openings and decorated ceilings. These features change the experience of light and attention. A window is both an opening and a worked surface; a ceiling can become a field of pattern rather than a neutral cover above the room.

Look at how ornament is positioned. Does it emphasise a doorway, frame a view or concentrate attention in a reception space? Questions of placement help explain why craftsmanship matters architecturally. The value is not simply that expensive materials appear in large quantities, but that they participate in the organisation of a room.

One house in a larger merchant quarter

The Heritage Foundation's account of Peshawar conservation describes the Sethi neighbourhood as a wider group of merchant residences, some altered or lost. Its discussion of work on another property, Qadri Manzil, should not be silently transferred to Karim Bakhsh's house. Distinct buildings can share a neighbourhood history without sharing every date or repair campaign.

That wider context gives the surviving house added significance. A single carefully preserved interior cannot replace an entire historic residential fabric, but it can make the relationships between trade, family life and skilled work easier to understand.

At Sethi House, let the courtyard guide the visit. Follow the thresholds, notice changes in light and compare public-facing display with more contained spaces. The richness of the building lies in the way these practical and expressive decisions are brought together.
