Charsadda’s Bala Hisar: the mound, not Peshawar’s fort

Charsadda’s Bala Hisar is an archaeological mound associated with ancient Pushkalavati, not Peshawar’s standing fort. Its research history shows why preliminary interpretations need revisiting.

Two places can share a name without sharing a history. Bala Hisar at Charsadda is an archaeological mound associated with ancient Pushkalavati. It must not be confused with the conspicuous fort in Peshawar. That distinction is more than a navigation detail: the kinds of evidence available at the two places are different.

At Charsadda, buried deposits and excavated material are central to interpretation. A photograph of the mound cannot supply the sequence beneath it. Nor does the expression high fort establish that every part was a fortress, or that the visible earthwork belongs to one construction episode. Place names are evidence to investigate, not substitutes for excavation.

How a provisional sequence became influential

The University of Cambridge’s account of earlier research explains the importance of Mortimer Wheeler’s 1958 investigation. It also makes a revealing qualification: Wheeler regarded the work as a preliminary reconnaissance, and the published pottery represented a selection. That initial framework subsequently acquired more authority than its limited basis necessarily justified.

This is a useful lesson in how archaeological knowledge travels. A diagram or period table can be copied into later works until the original caution disappears. Repetition makes a claim familiar; it does not enlarge the excavation on which the claim rests. Returning to the first report can therefore be as important as obtaining a new date.

The Cambridge account also describes the historical identification of Pushkalavati, including an inscription referring to the district of Pushkala. Such evidence helps establish a geographical argument, but it should be kept distinct from the dating of an individual occupation layer. A name and a deposit answer different questions.

Returning to the ground

A collaboration between the University of Peshawar and Cambridge began renewed investigation in 2006, led by Muhammad Nasim Khan and Cameron Petrie. The project situated Charsadda near the meeting of the Swat and Kabul river systems and within wider routes through the region. Its purpose was to improve understanding rather than merely repeat an established label.

That setting encourages questions about movement and settlement. Rivers can support connections while also altering land and access. Yet an attractive regional map cannot determine which route was used in a particular century. Material from securely recorded contexts is needed to connect a broad geographical possibility to a historical claim.

What a visitor should take away

The mound deserves attention even where its architecture is not immediately spectacular. Its significance lies partly in the information that remains beneath an apparently simple surface. Surface disturbance can sever relationships that no later photograph will restore.

Charsadda’s Bala Hisar is best introduced through this combination of place and inquiry. Identify the correct site, explain the evidence for Pushkalavati, and show how the chronology has been questioned. The result is a stronger history than a single confident foundation date: one that makes clear both what researchers have learned and why the work continues.
