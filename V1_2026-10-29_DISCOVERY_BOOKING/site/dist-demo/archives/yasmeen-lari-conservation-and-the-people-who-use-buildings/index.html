Yasmeen Lari: conservation and the people who use buildings

Yasmeen Lari’s work connects conservation with everyday building knowledge, while raising questions about evidence, maintenance and community agency.

Yasmeen Lari’s career makes it possible to discuss conservation without confining it to the repair of monumental buildings. The Architekturzentrum Wien’s 2023 exhibition placed her modernist practice, heritage work and later self-build projects within one account. That framing is valuable because it asks how knowledge moves between preserving an existing structure and helping people construct something they can use now.

The exhibition drew on photographs, drawings and documents from Lari’s archive. It described the Heritage Foundation’s interest in both recognised monuments and ordinary buildings, and connected her later work with local construction techniques and materials such as bamboo, earth and lime. This is an institutional interpretation of a career, rather than a complete independent assessment of every project’s performance.

Two designs, different scales of use

The Lahore Biennale Foundation’s presentation identifies Lari Octagon Green as a design developed after the October 2015 earthquake. Its account explains a combination of a southern chaura-like roof form with the principle of a cross-braced northern frame, using bamboo and foldable parts. The significance is adaptation: inherited forms become resources for a new construction problem, rather than patterns reproduced merely to look traditional.

The same presentation dates Pakistan Chulah to 2014 and describes a raised cooking platform using sun-dried mud and lime. Here the architectural unit is not a whole house. It is a place for an everyday activity, with bodily comfort, smoke, fuel and water exposure among the design concerns. Recognising a cooking place as architecture broadens what enters a heritage discussion and whose knowledge becomes relevant to it.

Those descriptions establish intentions and methods. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate that every installed example performs identically, remains maintained or delivers a particular reduction in fuel use. Such claims require evidence about actual installations, testing conditions and the people using them. A material can be appropriate in one configuration without being a universal answer to every building risk.

Who retains the knowledge?

The most consequential question is what remains with a community after a project team leaves. Can residents repair a damaged element? Are replacement materials available? Who controls the decisions about layout and modification? These are useful tests of a self-build model, but this article has not interviewed users and should not supply invented answers on their behalf.

Conservation also depends on comparable relationships. A documented technique is not sustained simply because it appears in a report; someone must have the skill, authority and resources to practise it. Lari’s work makes that connection visible across otherwise separate categories of monument, shelter and domestic infrastructure.

The result is a richer subject than a sequence of prizes or heroic claims. It concerns how architectural expertise can be shared, revised and held accountable to use. Appreciating that ambition does not require suspending scrutiny. It requires taking residents’ experience, maintenance and long-term evidence as seriously as an architect’s original design.
