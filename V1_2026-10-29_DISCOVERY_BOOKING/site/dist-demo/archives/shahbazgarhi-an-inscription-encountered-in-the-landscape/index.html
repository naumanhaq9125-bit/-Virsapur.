Shahbazgarhi: an inscription encountered in the landscape

Shahbazgarhi’s two inscribed boulders show why a text’s physical arrangement matters. The separately carved twelfth edict also has a revealing history of nineteenth-century documentation.

At Shahbazgarhi, the arrangement of the text is part of the evidence. Ashoka's fourteen major edicts are distributed across two boulders rather than contained on one continuous surface. That physical separation once complicated their documentation and still invites a useful question: how does the placement of words affect the way a public message is encountered?

Pakistan's Tentative List description locates the inscriptions beside an old route connecting the Peshawar valley with northern valleys and Taxila. It dates them to the middle of the third century BCE and identifies Kharoshthi writing, read from right to left. The route, the rock and the script belong together in the interpretation.

The missing section that was nearby

Classicist Llewelyn Morgan, drawing on research undertaken with archaeologist Luca Olivieri, recounts a revealing episode in nineteenth-century scholarship. Charles Masson documented the principal inscription in 1838. Comparison with other versions showed that the twelfth edict was missing from the record. Harold Deane subsequently identified it on a separate nearby rock in 1888.

This was a change in scholarly documentation, not proof that local people had been unaware of the second boulder. The distinction matters whenever an archaeological account uses the language of discovery. What was new to a visiting recorder or a learned society was not necessarily new to the community around the site.

Morgan notes the separate treatment of the twelfth edict and discusses its emphasis on respect between religious groups. He offers the interpretation that this message may have had particular importance in the region. That is a historically informed reading of placement, not a fact explicitly explained by the rock's location alone.

Reading words and reading space

An edition that prints all fourteen edicts in sequence makes comparison easier, but it can conceal the physical experience of the originals. At Shahbazgarhi, moving between surfaces is part of encountering the text. Letter size, carving and separation can carry information that a plain transcription does not preserve.

The same principle applies to photographs. A tightly cropped image may help specialists examine letters while removing the inscription from its landscape. A wider record can show relationships between boulders and approaches but make the text illegible. Both forms of documentation have value because they answer different questions.

An imperial message, not a simple mirror of society

The edicts express a ruler's declared principles. They should not be treated as automatic proof that every subject followed those principles or that all religious relations were harmonious. The gap between a statement of intent and lived practice is itself a historical question.

For visitors, the most meaningful approach is to preserve the connection between text and place. Observe the boulders without touching or marking them, consult a reliable translation and notice how the words have been arranged. Shahbazgarhi's importance is not exhausted by its age. It also shows how political ideas were given a durable physical presence, and how careful documentation can recover details that a seemingly complete record once missed.
