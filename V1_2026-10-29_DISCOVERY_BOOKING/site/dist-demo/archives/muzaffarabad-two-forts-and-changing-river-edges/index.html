Muzaffarabad: two forts and changing river edges

Muzaffarabad’s Red and Black forts are separate monuments with different records; their river setting should not be used to merge their histories.

Muzaffarabad’s Red Fort and Black Fort are not alternative names for one monument. They occupy different locations and have different documentary records. Their shared association with the city makes comparison useful, but it does not justify combining their dates, functions or access arrangements into a single account.

The federal inventory places Red Fort, also called Rutta Qila, on the western bank of the Neelum. It describes a terraced arrangement with the entrance at an upper level and associates the fort broadly with the sixteenth century. The topography is fundamental to the building: changes of level are part of its organisation, not merely a scenic backdrop.

Defence and a river setting

A raised riverside position can offer visibility and constrain approaches. It can also expose a structure to environmental forces and make access difficult. Those are general relationships to investigate through plans and field evidence, not a reason to assume that every wall served the same defensive purpose. A riverbank photograph cannot reveal the complete sequence of construction.

The inventory records substantial damage in the 2005 earthquake. This provides a necessary caution when comparing older descriptions or photographs with later views. A missing stair or altered wall may reflect damage and subsequent work rather than an original design. The monument’s recent history belongs within its interpretation, not outside an account devoted only to its foundation.

A separate record for Black Fort

Black Fort, also identified as Gojra Fort, is catalogued on Police Line Road. Its entry associates it with the nineteenth-century historical period and reports military use with no public access. That is a different evidential and practical situation from a freely accessible visitor attraction. The catalogue should not be used to imply permission to enter or photograph restricted areas.

The shortness of the record also limits comparison. It does not provide enough information to reconstruct the fort’s original rooms or assign a detailed sequence of alterations. The correct response is to keep that limit visible, rather than borrowing Red Fort’s history to fill the gap. Two sites can be important within the same city while being documented very unevenly.

The city around the monuments

Muzaffarabad’s archaeology department also records structures associated with the Jhelum and Domel, widening the view beyond the forts. River edges can contain crossings, ceremonial buildings and changing urban approaches as well as defences. A city history needs to distinguish these functions rather than reducing every riverside structure to military control.

Reading the two forts separately is therefore the first step towards understanding their relationship. Establish the correct name and location; distinguish a historical phase from a current condition; and compare only features supported by records. The result is a more precise account of Muzaffarabad’s built landscape, in which rivers, changes of level and later urban life connect monuments without erasing the differences between them.
