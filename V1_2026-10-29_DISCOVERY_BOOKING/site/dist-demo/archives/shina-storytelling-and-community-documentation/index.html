Shina storytelling and community documentation

A Shina story or recollection is most useful when the record keeps its speaker, setting and permissions visible alongside the words.

A story recorded in Shina is not simply a sample of a language. It is an event in which someone chooses what to tell, how to tell it and what a listener is expected to understand. Community documentation becomes stronger when those choices remain attached to the recording.

This changes the emphasis from collecting “vanishing customs” to sustaining useful relationships. The storyteller is not merely a source from whom an archive extracts words. They may be a performer, interpreter, family historian or critic of the version someone else remembers.

A recollection with a name and a setting

Mountain Heritage Archives’ description of an interview with Khalifa Durr-e-Maknun identifies the recording as made at his home in Sher Qila in the summer of 2016. It describes his recollections of the Shina poet Khalifa Rehmat Malang Jan and the poetry associated with the poet’s love for Yourmas. The publicly posted account thus distinguishes the remembered poet from the person recounting his memory.

That distinction is essential. A recollection about a poem is not necessarily a contemporary record of its composition. The interview date is not the date of the events described. Both can be valuable, but their value depends on knowing which kind of evidence is being heard.

The recording’s public description also does not grant permission to extract and reuse its audio. Access to an archive and clearance for a new publication are separate matters.

A language contains more than one storytelling setting

Ruth Laila Schmidt’s study of a Naga-prince tale concerns a version recorded in the Shina of Indus Kohistan. Its abstract highlights a resourceful female protagonist and compares the tale with versions elsewhere. This is a different regional and documentary setting from the Sher Qila interview.

The comparison should not erase that difference. A shared language label cannot make every story a representative voice for all Shina speakers. Nor does resemblance between tales prove that one storyteller copied another. The form of the comparison must remain tied to the versions actually studied.

For a reader, this is an opportunity to ask better questions. Who drives the action in this telling? Which explanations does the narrator offer? What does a translation need to explain because the expected audience has changed?

Documentation that returns something

A community-led project should agree how recordings will be named, stored, accessed and reused before treating public upload as the default outcome. Speakers should be able to identify the language variety and decide whether personal names or sensitive parts of a narrative should appear.

The useful outputs need not all be scholarly. A family copy, an accessible transcription or material chosen for local teaching may matter as much as a searchable catalogue. Where translation is made, its maker and reviewer should receive credit alongside the recorder.

The aim is not to preserve one supposedly final version of Shina storytelling. It is to keep an accountable record of particular tellings, with room for other speakers to respond, differ and add. An archive becomes a conversation when its contributors can still recognise themselves within it.

  2. The Transformation of a Naga Prince Tale, abstract — Ruth Laila Schmidt, Oriental Archive81,2013; contents hosted by Columbia University (accessed 2026-10-22)
