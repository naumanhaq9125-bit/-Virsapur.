Pucca Qila: surviving walls in an inhabited city

Pucca Qila’s walls are the surviving edge of a much-altered Hyderabad fort. Its history includes royal residence, military reuse and the difficult work of conserving an inhabited urban place.

Pucca Qila is not simply a complete fort waiting to be viewed from outside. Its surviving walls enclose a place whose buildings, uses and urban relationships have changed substantially. The heritage record’s emphasis on the boundary wall is therefore meaningful: the perimeter is a major surviving part of a much more complicated historical interior.

Sindh’s Directorate General of Antiquities dates the founding of Hyderabad’s fort under Mian Ghulam Shah Kalhora to 1768. It records a further phase under Mir Fateh Ali Khan Talpur from 1789, when buildings were added for the ruling household. The same account describes extensive clearance of internal structures by the British in 1857 and subsequent military uses. These episodes explain why the enclosure cannot be read as an intact snapshot of one court.

The wall as an architectural document

The fortification’s crest carries distinctive merlons, the raised elements along the top of a defensive wall. They give the skyline a recognisable pattern, but the wall should also be examined below that outline. Changes in masonry, repaired openings and the relationship between wall and ground can reveal a different kind of history from the silhouette alone.

The provincial account identifies surviving domestic fabric associated with the Talpur period, including painted elements. Its older language judges the paintings’ taste, a judgement that need not be inherited by a modern archive. Description, attribution and condition are useful information; an unexplained dismissal of aesthetic quality is not a substitute for them.

The contrast between the strong outer boundary and the loss or alteration of internal buildings is central to the visitor’s understanding. A plan showing known historical structures and present conditions would be more informative than a reconstruction presented without explanation. It would allow absence to be understood as evidence of change rather than as an invitation to fill every gap with an imagined palace.

An urban place, not an empty stage

Conserving a fort within a living city requires attention to more than historic masonry. The surrounding streets and the people using the area are part of the present context in which any intervention occurs. A heritage account should not treat inhabitants as anonymous obstacles or imply that an attractive photograph is a sufficient measure of success.

Equally, present use does not remove the need to document and protect historic fabric. The useful discussion concerns specific conditions and responsibilities: what survives, what is vulnerable, who maintains it and how proposed work affects both buildings and people. Such questions require current evidence, not assumptions derived from the word encroachment.

A Sindh Culture Department report records a visit to Pacca Qila in September 2024, confirming continuing institutional attention without establishing the outcome of every conservation issue. The fort’s public story should maintain that same distinction between attention and completion. Pucca Qila matters because its walls frame several histories at once, and because their survival asks how an inhabited city can care for the material record within it.
