Phulkari: stitch, cloth and regional variation

Phulkari belongs to a shared regional textile history. Specific objects show how stitch, cloth and density vary within the tradition.

Phulkari is often introduced through a translation of its name, but “flower work” does not prepare us for the full range of its surfaces. The relationship between stitch and ground cloth is more informative than the expectation that every piece must look like a garden. A close examination begins with the individual textile rather than an ideal pattern assigned to the whole tradition.

The Victoria and Albert Museum describes phulkari in relation to the wider Punjab region, spanning parts of present-day India and Pakistan. Its account explains work in darning stitch from the reverse of a cotton ground, using silk thread for a lustrous effect. It also distinguishes densely embroidered bagh pieces. These are useful technical and regional anchors, not a claim that every surviving example is identical.

One illustrated object is a skirt attributed to Bannu, dated about 1867 and numbered 05668(IS). The record identifies yellow silk embroidery on indigo-dyed cotton. The named place and approximate date make this a particular object rather than a generic illustration of “Punjabi dress.” Its attribution also reminds readers that the historical geography of craft is not neatly contained by a single present-day administrative boundary.

Cloth remains part of the design

Embroidery does not replace the ground entirely in every piece. Where the cloth remains visible, its colour participates in the design. Where stitching becomes dense, another relationship emerges between coverage, reflected light and the surviving ground. Comparing these effects helps a viewer see variation without ranking one object simply by how little unworked cloth can be seen.

The reverse-side technique is also worth explaining without turning the article into a tutorial. The finished face can conceal the order of work. A documented reverse photograph or a clearly labelled modern demonstration may help make that labour intelligible, while keeping the historical textile protected from unnecessary handling.

Shared history, specific attribution

Recognising a cross-border regional tradition does not make provenance unimportant. A maker, workshop, district or family history should be retained when documented. The wider category provides a connection between objects; it should not erase their differences or transfer one community's account of use to everyone else.

Likewise, an approximate nineteenth-century date cannot establish an unchanged practice stretching indefinitely into the past. It establishes what that catalogue attributes to that object. A stronger history grows by connecting securely recorded examples and testimony, not by treating visual resemblance as a complete chronological bridge.

Phulkari can therefore be approached through a sequence of close observations: the ground, the stitch, the density, the full garment and its recorded history. This way of looking keeps regional belonging and individual variation together. It also gives an unnamed maker a more substantial presence through demonstrable choices, without claiming to recover a personal story that the surviving record does not contain.
